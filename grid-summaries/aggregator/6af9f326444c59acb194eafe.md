## A Senate First Reveals a Divide Over Political Islam

An article argues that Abdul El-Sayed's election as America's first Muslim senator highlights a blind spot in U.S. politics. It says both major parties struggle to talk clearly about Islam and Islamism.

### Two Opposing Failures

According to the piece, Republicans often treat Muslims and Islamists as the same thing, lumping a religious community together with a political ideology. Democrats, it says, often wave away scrutiny of Islamism by calling it Islamophobia. The article calls for debate grounded in evidence about Islamist influence, antisemitism, anti-Zionism, and political alliances, without singling out Muslims as a group.

### Why the Moment Matters

El-Sayed's rise comes amid heightened tension over Israel and Hamas, concern about antisemitism, questions of Muslim political representation, and arguments about extremism in American politics. The article presents his election as a test of whether the parties can discuss political Islam seriously without feeding anti-Muslim bigotry.
