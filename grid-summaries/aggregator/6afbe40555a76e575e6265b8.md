## Chaotic feeding may explain early giant black holes

A new study in Astronomy & Astrophysics proposes that supermassive black holes in the young universe could have grown quickly through a process called chaotic accretion. The work offers a possible answer to a puzzle about how such giants appeared so early.

### How chaotic growth works

The model describes gas disks that feed a black hole from randomly changing directions. That randomness keeps the black hole's spin low, which makes growth more efficient. According to the study, this means ordinary stellar-mass seeds could account for the earliest known giant black holes. Rare heavy seeds or long-term super-Eddington feeding would not be required.

### Why the debate is heating up

JWST and other surveys are uncovering surprisingly massive black holes and "little red dot" galaxies in the early universe, sharpening questions about how these objects formed so fast. Upcoming missions such as Euclid, Roman, and NewAthena could soon test whether these black holes were slow-spinning products of chaotic feeding.
