## Study links social identity to a drive to appear morally good

A new analysis from Leipzig University proposes that social identity grew out of a basic human need: being seen as a good member of one's cultural group who follows its norms. Its authors reach this view by drawing together research on children, great apes, and cooperation.

### What the researchers found

By connecting these separate lines of study, the team argues that even young children care about group norms, shared knowledge, and their moral reputation. In their account, early humans who wanted to be viewed as good, norm-following group members laid the foundations for the social identities people hold today. The comparison with great apes helps frame what may be distinctive about this human concern.

### Why it matters now

Social identity sits at the heart of current debates over belonging, polarization, group loyalty, and moral signaling. The analysis adds a developmental and evolutionary perspective to the question of why people care so strongly about being perceived as good by their group.
