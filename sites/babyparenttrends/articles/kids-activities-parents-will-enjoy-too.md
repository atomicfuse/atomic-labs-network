BuzzFeed Shopping recently rounded up 27 kids' activities with a cheeky promise: things you might enjoy doing, too. The full list isn't reproduced here, but the idea behind it is worth borrowing. When an activity is fun for you as well, you stay in it longer, you're more present, and your kid notices.

By the end of this piece, you'll have a simple test for picking activities you won't dread, plus concrete ideas to match the kind of grown-up payoff you're after. It's aimed at parents of kids roughly 4 to 11, the school-age stretch where you can finally play together without constant supervision.

## Why "fun for the adult" matters

Most kid activity advice quietly assumes you'll tolerate it. That works for ten minutes. Then you're checking your phone while your child asks you to watch, again.

The BuzzFeed roundup's premise flips that. Choose activities with something in them for you, and the time stops feeling like a chore. That's the angle here: **pick activities that give the grown-up a reward too**, whether that's nostalgia, creativity, or a way to decompress.

## The 10-second test before you start

Ask yourself one question: *what do I get out of this?* If you can name something, it's a keeper. Typical answers fall into three buckets:

- **Nostalgia:** it reminds you of something you loved at their age.
- **Creativity:** you get to make or build something, not just supervise.
- **Stress relief:** it's repetitive, absorbing, or physical in a way that quiets your head.

Those three buckets mirror the roundup's own framing, and they're a handy filter when you're staring at a long list of options.

## Nostalgia picks: play what you loved

The easiest way to enjoy kid time is to hand your kid something you adored at their age. Think classic board games, card games, or old-school backyard and living-room play.

**How to make it work:**
1. Pick one childhood favorite, not five.
2. Tell your kid why you loved it. Kids like origin stories.
3. Let them change the rules. Their version is often funnier than yours.

## Creativity picks: make something together

If you like making things, choose activities where you're a co-creator rather than a helper. Building, drawing, crafting, and storytelling all fit.

**Try this:**
- Set a 20-minute timer and each make something from the same pile of materials, then compare.
- Take turns adding one sentence or one drawn panel to a shared story.
- Give your kid a real job (designer, builder) and take a smaller one (assistant, supplier). You'll often get more engagement from both of you.

## Stress-relief picks: the quiet win

Some activities calm the adult as much as the child. Anything with steady, repetitive motion or absorbing focus tends to do this: puzzles, coloring, building sets, or sorting and arranging projects.

The benefit is practical. You sit side by side, hands busy, with little pressure to talk. Kids often open up in exactly this kind of low-key setting, so you may get a better conversation than if you'd asked directly.

## Rainy-day and weekend tactics

Indoor-weather days and school breaks are when boredom peaks. A few ways to stay ahead of it:

- **Keep a "yes box."** Stash a few activities you'd happily do, so you're not inventing one at 2 p.m. on a gray Saturday.
- **Rotate, don't stockpile.** Bring out one option at a time so it feels new.
- **Set a start and end.** "Let's do this until the timer goes" is easier on you and gives your kid a clear window of full attention.

## Keep it screen-free, keep it low-effort

The appeal of this approach is that it needs very little prep. You're not planning a production. You're choosing something with a built-in reason for you to show up, then letting it run.

If an activity needs a long setup or a long cleanup, it probably fails the test. Aim for things you can start in two minutes and abandon without guilt.

## What to do this week

Pick one activity from whichever bucket sounds most appealing to you, not to your kid. Put it on the calendar for the next gray afternoon or free weekend morning. If you're genuinely a little excited about it, your child will probably catch that, and that's the whole point.
