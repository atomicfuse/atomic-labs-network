You already know how to calm a overwhelmed child. You name the feeling, give a little warning, offer a snack, take a breath together. The harder part is doing any of it for yourself.

That's the core idea in a CNN piece by Amanda Schupak, republished by KRDO, on five child-rearing strategies that work just as well on adults. Schupak, who is raising a 7-year-old, writes that parenting involves a lot of learning on the job. Her argument, as summarized in the piece, is that adults often give kids compassion, structure and basic care, then forget to offer the same to themselves.

Here are the five strategies, with ways to try each one today. The examples and how-tos are my own take, not quotes from the article.

## 1. Put yourself in time-out

We use time-outs to help kids reset before a small problem becomes a big one. Schupak's first tip is to apply the same idea to yourself.

The adult version isn't a punishment. It's a pause. Here's how it might look:

- Step out of the room when a conversation is heating up, and say you'll be back.
- Pick a spot to reset: a bathroom, the porch, a parked car.
- Come back when your body has settled, not when the clock says so.

It works because you're interrupting the spiral before you say something you'll apologize for later. That's the same logic you use with a child.

## 2. Ask: is this a big problem or a little problem?

Parents often help young kids sort their troubles. A broken cracker is a little problem. A scraped knee that's bleeding is a bigger one. The sorting itself calms the reaction down.

Try it on yourself when an email lands badly or the dishes pile up. Ask whether this is a big problem, a little problem, or something that only feels big right now. Many daily irritations are small. Naming them that way helps you save your energy for the ones that really are big.

## 3. Give yourself a five-minute warning

Kids handle transitions better when they know one is coming. "Five more minutes, then shoes on" is a classic for a reason.

Adults struggle with transitions too, especially when you're deep in work or scrolling and need to switch to dinner, pickup or bedtime. Set a timer that gives you a heads-up before you have to change gears. You can use it to:

- Wrap up a task and jot down where you left off.
- Finish a thought instead of being yanked away mid-sentence.
- Arrive at the next thing a little less frazzled.

If you also manage attention or time-management struggles, a built-in warning can make the day feel less like a series of ambushes.

## 4. Take three deep breaths

This one is probably already in your parenting toolkit. When a child is upset, slowing the breath is one of the simplest ways to help them settle.

It works for you, too, and it costs nothing. Before you respond to the text, the toddler's tantrum or the boss's message, take three slow breaths. It's small enough to do in a grocery line or a meeting. Think of it as a tiny regulation tool: it won't fix your burnout, but it can give you a moment of space before you react.

## 5. Just eat something

When a kid is melting down, a good first question is whether they've eaten. Often a snack does more than a lecture.

Adults are not so different. Skipped meals can leave you short-tempered and foggy, and then you wonder why everything feels like too much. Before you decide a bad mood means something deeper, check the basics. Have you eaten? Had water? Slept? Fix that first, then reassess.

## Why this resonates right now

Burnout, emotional overload and attention struggles are familiar parts of modern life, and many people are looking for simple self-regulation tools. There's also growing interest in nervous system care and in applying therapy-informed habits to everyday adulthood. These five tips fit that mood because they're low-shame and easy to start. You don't need a new app or a free weekend.

There's also a quiet relationship benefit. When you model these habits in front of your kids, partner or coworkers, you show what regulation looks like. A parent who says "I'm taking a few breaths before I answer" teaches more than one who only tells a child to calm down.

## A simple way to start

Don't try all five at once. Pick the one that matches your most common stress point:

- Blowing up in conflict: time-out.
- Sweating small stuff: big problem or little problem.
- Rough transitions: five-minute warning.
- Reactive moments: three breaths.
- Afternoon crankiness: eat something.

Use it for a week and see what changes.

The next time you're coming apart at the seams, ask yourself what you'd say to your child in that exact moment, then say it to yourself.
