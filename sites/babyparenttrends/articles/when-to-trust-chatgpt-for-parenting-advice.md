Fox News anchor and executive editor Martha MacCallum, host of "The Story," has a warning for parents: don't rely on ChatGPT for child-rearing advice. She made the case on "Outnumbered," according to Fox News. Her core point is that AI can be tempting for quick guidance, but parenting decisions call for judgment, context, and trusted human expertise.

That's a useful frame, because most of us are already asking. Per Fox News' coverage, parents are increasingly turning to AI tools for instant answers about health, discipline, education, and emotional issues. The better question isn't whether ChatGPT can answer. It's when you should trust the answer.

Here's a practical way to decide, built around one idea: use AI as a starting point for thinking, never as the final word on your child.

## Sort Your Question Before You Type It

Before you open the app, ask yourself one thing: *If this answer is wrong, what happens?*

- **Low stakes:** a rainy-day activity list for a 6-year-old, a script for asking a neighbor to turn down music at naptime, ideas for a birthday party theme.
- **Medium stakes:** how to handle bedtime battles, sibling squabbles, or homework resistance.
- **High stakes:** anything involving your child's physical health, mental health, safety, or a sudden change in behavior.

Low-stakes questions are fair game. Medium-stakes questions can use AI for brainstorming, as long as you filter the ideas through what you know about your kid. High-stakes questions belong with a professional.

## Where AI Can Genuinely Help

Used carefully, a chatbot can save you time on the mental load of family life:

- **Brainstorming:** Ask for five ways to make morning routines smoother, then pick the one that fits your household.
- **Wording things:** Need a kind but firm way to explain a rule to a 4-year-old? Drafting language is a good use.
- **Preparing for experts:** Ask it to help you write a list of questions for your pediatrician or your child's teacher. You walk in more organized, and the human still makes the call.
- **Planning:** Meal ideas, chore charts, and weekly schedules carry little risk.

Notice the pattern. In each case, you stay in charge and the tool does the grunt work.

## Red Flags That an Answer Doesn't Fit Your Child

A chatbot doesn't know your child. It doesn't know their temperament, history, allergies, learning style, or what happened at school last Tuesday. That gap is where bad advice slips in. Pause if you see any of these:

- **Confident, one-size-fits-all answers.** Real child development varies, and good guidance usually acknowledges that.
- **Advice that contradicts your gut.** If something feels off for your kid, that feeling counts as information.
- **Medical or diagnostic language.** If the response sounds like it is diagnosing your child, treat it as a prompt to call a doctor, not as a diagnosis.
- **No mention of when to seek help.** Trustworthy guidance on health or emotional struggles points you toward a professional at some point.
- **Advice that skips the relationship.** Parenting is built on connection. A tidy tactic that ignores how your child feels is probably missing something.

## How to Fact-Check What You Get

If an AI answer has you considering a real decision, run it through a quick check:

1. **Ask for the reasoning.** If it can't explain why, don't act on it.
2. **Cross-check with a trusted source.** Your pediatrician's office, your child's school, or established children's health organizations are better anchors than a single chat response.
3. **Compare it to what you know.** Does it match your child's age, personality, and circumstances?
4. **Sleep on anything big.** Quick answers feel urgent, but few parenting decisions need to be made in the next five minutes.

## What Humans Still Do Better

The appeal of AI is speed. The cost is everything a person brings that a chatbot can't:

- **Pediatricians** can examine your child, know their medical history, and spot what doesn't show up in a typed description.
- **Teachers** see how your child behaves in a group, how they learn, and how they've changed over the school year.
- **Therapists and counselors** read tone, body language, and patterns over time, and they're accountable for their guidance.
- **Other parents and family** offer lived experience and honest perspective, and they know you.

This lines up with MacCallum's takeaway, as Fox News reports it: parenting calls for judgment, context, and trusted human expertise.

## Keep Your Instincts in the Driver's Seat

There's a quieter risk beyond bad answers. If you route every small decision through a screen, you practice trusting yourself less. You're the person who has watched your child's face when they're nervous, who knows the difference between tired-cranky and something-is-wrong. That knowledge doesn't show up in a prompt.

Try a simple habit: before you ask AI, write down what you would do on your own. Then see whether the tool adds anything. Often it won't, and that's a good sign your instincts were already working.

## A Quick Checklist to Save

Before acting on any AI parenting advice, ask:

- Is this low stakes, or could a wrong answer affect my child's health or safety?
- Does it fit *my* child, not just children in general?
- Have I checked it against a trusted human source?
- Does it sit right with what I know about my kid?

If you can't answer yes to all four, close the app and call someone who knows your child. Use ChatGPT to help you prepare for that conversation, not to replace it.
