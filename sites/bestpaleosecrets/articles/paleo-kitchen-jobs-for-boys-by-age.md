A recent Fox News piece asks who is teaching boys how to become men. It points to two numbers: nearly 80% of public school teachers are women, and millions of boys grow up without a father at home. The article's argument is that boys need steady male formation at home, at school, and in the community, and that character, discipline, and responsibility are learned over years, not fixed later.

You can debate the causes all day. This article is about something smaller and doable: the kitchen. Cooking is one of the few daily routines where an adult and a boy work side by side with a real task, a deadline (dinner), and a visible result. If you are a dad, uncle, grandfather, coach, or family friend, it is an easy place to show up consistently. Below is a plan for ages 7, 10, and 13, with Paleo-friendly recipes to match.

To be clear about the limits: cooking dinner together will not solve a national problem, and nothing here comes from the Fox News piece except the concern behind it. The age-based jobs are my own practical suggestions. But steady, shared work is a good way to build the kind of relationship that piece says is missing.

## Why the kitchen works as a mentoring space

Boys often talk more when their hands are busy and nobody is staring at them. Chopping, stirring, and flipping give you side-by-side time without the pressure of a formal talk.

Cooking also teaches the traits the article names: restraint (wait for the pan to heat), responsibility (clean up your station), and resilience (the eggs stuck, try again). Dinner either gets made or it doesn't, which gives a boy a clear sense of contribution.

The rule that makes it mentoring and not just chore-assigning: do it **with** him, at the same time, on a regular schedule. Pick one night a week and protect it.

## Age 7: Learn the basics and own one job

At seven, the goal is a sense of belonging in the kitchen. Give him a permanent job so he knows dinner depends on him.

**Good jobs for a 7-year-old:**
- Crack eggs into a bowl (expect shell; teach him to fish it out with a larger piece of shell)
- Wash and dry vegetables
- Tear lettuce and herbs
- Stir batter or a cold mixture
- Set the table

**Recipe to try: Egg muffin cups.** Whisk 8 eggs with salt and pepper. Let him add chopped spinach, diced cooked sausage, and bell pepper to a greased muffin tin, then pour the eggs over. Bake at 350°F for about 18 to 20 minutes. They keep for several days in the fridge and reheat quickly for school mornings.

Teach one rule: hot things get announced. Say "hot behind you" every time. It is a small habit of care for others.

## Age 10: Real knife skills and a full step

Ten-year-olds can handle more than most parents assume, with supervision. Move him from helper to the person responsible for a whole step.

**Good jobs for a 10-year-old:**
- Cut soft vegetables with a small, sharp knife (a sharp knife is safer than a dull one; show the claw grip)
- Season and toss sheet-pan ingredients
- Brown ground meat on the stove with you next to him
- Read the recipe aloud and track the steps

**Recipe to try: One-pan sausage and sweet potato hash.** Dice 2 medium sweet potatoes small so they cook quickly. Brown them in 2 tablespoons of avocado oil or ghee for 10 to 12 minutes, covered part of the time to soften. Add sliced Paleo-friendly sausage (check labels for added sugar and fillers) and diced onion, cook another 8 minutes, then top with fried eggs. He runs the pan; you coach.

Let him make a mistake and fix it. A slightly burnt hash he rescues teaches more than a perfect one you made for him.

## Age 13: Plan, shop, and cook a full meal

At thirteen, the aim is independence with accountability. He should be able to feed himself and others by the end of the year.

**Good jobs for a 13-year-old:**
- Choose the week's dinner, write the grocery list, and help shop
- Cook one full meal a week, start to finish
- Batch cook proteins on Sunday for lunches
- Handle his own cleanup, including the pans

**Recipe to try: Sheet-pan chicken thighs with vegetables.** Toss bone-in chicken thighs with olive oil, salt, paprika, and garlic powder. Roast at 425°F for 35 to 40 minutes alongside broccoli and carrots, until the chicken reaches 165°F. Teach him to use a meat thermometer, since checking instead of guessing is a habit that carries over to other things.

This is also the age to talk about why you cook this way: whole foods, simple ingredients, reading labels. Keep it conversational, not a lecture.

## Smart swaps boys actually like

Picky eaters and growing appetites need familiar food. A few easy swaps keep it Paleo without a fight:

- **Pancakes:** blend a ripe banana with 2 eggs and a spoonful of almond butter, then cook small rounds in a skillet.
- **Breading:** use almond flour or crushed pork rinds on chicken tenders and bake them.
- **Sweet snack:** apple slices with almond butter, or frozen grapes.
- **Burgers:** wrap in lettuce or serve over a sweet potato round instead of a bun.

Let him choose which swap to try. Ownership beats persuasion.

## If you are not his dad

The Fox News piece stresses that boys need male role models across home, school, and community, not only fathers. If you are an uncle, grandfather, neighbor, or mentor, offer a standing cooking date with a boy whose dad is absent, with his guardian's blessing. A weekly pancake morning or Sunday meal prep session is an easy ask, and a boy remembers who shows up every week.

## Start this week

Pick one boy, one night, and one recipe from above. Put it on the calendar, hand him a job that matters, and cook next to him. Repeat next week. Consistency is the whole point.
