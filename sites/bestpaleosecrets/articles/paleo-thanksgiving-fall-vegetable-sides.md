Farmer Lee Jones of The Chef's Garden appeared on "Fox & Friends Weekend" to showcase fresh fall vegetables and Thanksgiving side dishes, per Fox News. The pitch: seasonal produce brings brighter, fresher flavors to the holiday table without overcomplicating the meal.

That idea fits Paleo cooking well. Vegetables are already the center of a Paleo plate, and Thanksgiving sides are where most of the wheat, dairy, and refined sugar sneak in. Below is a practical way to build a vegetable-forward spread, with swaps for the usual offenders. The segment as reported doesn't list specific recipes, so the dishes and techniques here are my own suggestions, not Jones's.

## Start With the Vegetable, Not the Casserole

The classic sides often hide vegetables under cream soups, crispy toppings, and marshmallows. Flip that. Pick one or two fall vegetables that look great at the market, then cook them in a way that lets their flavor lead.

Good candidates for a Paleo table:

- Carrots, any color
- Brussels sprouts
- Sweet potatoes and winter squash
- Cauliflower
- Beets
- Green beans

Buy what looks freshest. Fresh vegetables need less seasoning and less help, which is the whole point.

## Roast Everything on One Sheet Pan Schedule

Oven space is the real Thanksgiving bottleneck. Roasting is the simplest approach because it is mostly hands-off.

1. Heat the oven to 425°F.
2. Cut vegetables into similar sizes so they finish together: roughly 1-inch pieces for carrots and squash, halves for Brussels sprouts.
3. Toss with avocado oil or melted ghee (or olive oil if you avoid dairy), salt, and pepper.
4. Spread in a single layer with space between pieces. Crowding steams them instead of browning them.
5. Roast 25 to 40 minutes, flipping once. Softer vegetables like green beans take less time than dense squash.

If the turkey is hogging the oven, roast the vegetables ahead and reheat them in a hot skillet just before serving.

## Smart Swaps for the Usual Suspects

You can keep the familiar dishes and change what's inside them.

### Mashed potatoes to mashed cauliflower or sweet potato
Steam cauliflower until very soft, then blend with olive oil or ghee and roasted garlic. For sweet potatoes, a splash of coconut milk replaces cream.

### Cream-based green bean casserole
Sauté green beans and mushrooms, then add a simple sauce of blended cashews or coconut milk thickened with arrowroot starch. Top with sliced shallots roasted until crisp instead of fried onions with wheat flour.

### Candied sweet potatoes
Skip the marshmallows and brown sugar. Roast sweet potato wedges with cinnamon and a little orange juice, then finish with chopped pecans. The natural sweetness does the work.

### Stuffing
Use sautéed celery, onion, mushrooms, and herbs with chopped nuts and sausage in place of bread. Bake until the top crisps.

### Gravy
Thicken pan juices with arrowroot or tapioca starch instead of wheat flour. Mix the starch with cold water first, then whisk it in and simmer until glossy.

## Quick Plating Upgrades That Cost Nothing

A plain roasted vegetable looks far better with a few finishing touches. Fresh produce rewards a little attention at the end:

- **Acid:** a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar wakes up heavy dishes.
- **Crunch:** toasted pecans, walnuts, or pepitas add texture.
- **Fresh herbs:** parsley, thyme, or chives scattered at the last second.
- **Color contrast:** put orange carrots next to dark green sprouts, or scatter pomegranate seeds over roasted squash.
- **Warm platter:** serve on a platter, not in the roasting pan, and pile vegetables loosely rather than flattening them.

## A Make-Ahead Plan for the Week

Spreading the work out keeps Thanksgiving day calm.

- **Two or three days before:** wash and cut hard vegetables like carrots, squash, and beets. Store in airtight containers in the fridge.
- **The day before:** make the gravy base, toast the nuts, and mix any sauces. Cook mashed cauliflower or sweet potato and refrigerate it.
- **Thanksgiving morning:** trim green beans and Brussels sprouts, which are best cut fresh.
- **Just before serving:** reheat, add acid and herbs, and plate.

Leftovers are easy too. Roasted vegetables fold into a breakfast hash with eggs or a quick soup with broth the next day.

## Keep the Menu Small

You don't need ten sides. Three or four well-made vegetable dishes, one of them a little sweet, one creamy, one crisp, will cover everything a table wants. Fresh ingredients do most of the heavy lifting when you let them.

This week, pick two fall vegetables, roast them with good oil and salt, finish with something sharp and something crunchy, and see how little else the table needs.
