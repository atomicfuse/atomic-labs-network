Paleo cooking can drift into sameness: chicken, sweet potato, repeat. One way out is to learn from cooks who treat a meal as more than fuel. By the end of this piece, you'll have a framework for building Paleo-friendly meals around heritage and hospitality, plus a few practical swaps to keep traditional-style dishes within your way of eating.

## The Cook Behind the Idea

Per Jsonline, Milwaukee food artist Muna Riyad Sharma grew up in Palestine and honors that heritage through her tablescape and halal catering company, Bint Jamila's Table. The article frames Palestinian food as both cultural preservation and an act of resistance.

I'm not going to claim her recipes or tell you what's on her menu, because that's her story to tell. Read the original profile for it. What her work offers any home cook is a way of thinking: food carries memory, family, and place, and the table is where that gets shared.

## Start With the Table, Not Just the Plate

Sharma's work combines tablescaping with catering, which suggests a useful habit: treat setting the table as part of the cooking. You don't need a catering budget for this.

- Serve family-style. Put food on shared platters in the center rather than plating in the kitchen.
- Use what you own. One large board or platter, a few small bowls, and a handful of fresh herbs scattered on top changes how a weeknight dinner feels.
- Add a bowl of something fresh. Sliced cucumbers, tomatoes, or olives make the table look generous and cost very little.

This matters for meal prep too. A batch of cooked protein looks like leftovers in a container and like a meal on a platter.

## Build a Heritage-Inspired Paleo Plate

You can borrow the shared-table format without pretending to replicate any specific tradition. Here's a simple structure for a Paleo spread that works for four people.

### 1. One main protein

Roast bone-in chicken thighs or lamb with olive oil, lemon, garlic, and salt. Bake chicken thighs at 425°F for about 35 to 45 minutes, until the skin is crisp and the juices run clear (165°F internal). One tray, almost no cleanup.

### 2. Two or three vegetable sides

Roast cauliflower or eggplant, and add a chopped cucumber and tomato salad dressed with olive oil and lemon. Different textures keep a plain protein interesting.

### 3. A dip or sauce

Traditional dips often lean on ingredients like chickpeas, which Paleo skips. Use a swap: blend roasted cauliflower or zucchini with tahini, lemon juice, garlic, and olive oil until smooth. It's not a replica, but it fills the same role on the table.

### 4. Something to scoop with

Bread is the obvious gap. Offer cucumber rounds, endive leaves, or lettuce cups, or make a simple flatbread from almond or cassava flour if you eat those.

## Smart Swaps That Keep the Spirit

Swaps work best when they do the same job as the original ingredient.

- **Legume-based dips:** swap in roasted cauliflower, zucchini, or eggplant blended with tahini.
- **Wheat flatbread:** swap in cassava or almond flour flatbread, or use lettuce leaves.
- **Rice or grain sides:** swap in cauliflower rice, sautéed with onion and a little olive oil.
- **Yogurt sauces:** if you avoid dairy, use a coconut-based yogurt with lemon, garlic, and chopped mint.
- **Sweeteners in desserts:** serve fresh fruit, such as sliced oranges or figs when in season, with a few chopped nuts.

Check labels on spice blends and store-bought sauces, since some include fillers or added sugar.

## Batch It for the Week

A spread like this adapts well to meal prep.

1. Sunday: roast a large tray of chicken thighs and a tray of vegetables. Blend a double batch of your tahini dip.
2. Store the protein, vegetables, and dip separately in airtight containers. They'll hold about three to four days in the fridge.
3. Each day, assemble a plate with fresh cucumber and tomato added at the last minute so they stay crisp.

For portable lunches, pack the dip in a small jar and the lettuce or endive leaves separately so nothing gets soggy.

## Why Hospitality Is the Real Technique

The takeaway from Sharma's work, as Jsonline describes it, is that food and the table can hold heritage and identity. You can apply that without borrowing anyone's culture wholesale: cook the dishes your own family would recognize, and serve them as if the people eating matter.

That might mean your grandmother's roast, a regional dish from your own background adapted with Paleo swaps, or a dish you learned from a neighbor. Ask where a recipe came from before you change it, and keep the story alive when you do.

This week, pick one dish with a story, make it Paleo-friendly with one or two swaps, and serve it family-style on a real platter. Then tell the people at the table where it came from.
