A small study out of Maastricht University has people talking about the humble peanut as a brain-health snack. If you eat Paleo, you have a wrinkle: peanuts are legumes, which most Paleo plans skip. So what should you do with the news? This article covers what the researchers found, what they didn't prove, and how to build a smart, Paleo-friendly snack habit without overreaching.

## What the study found

Per Fox News' report, healthy older adults ate 60 grams of unsalted, skin-roasted peanuts every day for 16 weeks. Afterward, researchers saw signs of improved insulin responsiveness in memory-related regions of the brain. They also saw a slight increase in brain blood flow.

That's the whole headline finding. It's interesting, and it fits a growing line of research connecting metabolism and insulin function to how the brain ages. But it's narrow.

## What the study did not show

Experts quoted in the coverage caution that this does not prove peanuts prevent Alzheimer's disease or cognitive decline. The study was small, and the changes measured were biological signals, not proof that anyone's memory got better or that disease risk dropped.

A useful rule for any small nutrition study: ask what was actually measured. Here, it was insulin responsiveness and blood flow in the brain. Those are promising markers. They are not outcomes like "fewer diagnoses" or "sharper memory in daily life."

## The Paleo problem: peanuts are legumes

Peanuts grow underground and are botanically legumes, which puts them outside most Paleo guidelines alongside beans and lentils. You have a few honest options:

- **Stay strict.** The study doesn't change the rules of your plan, and one small trial is no reason to abandon them.
- **Treat it as a personal call.** Some people eat a flexible, Paleo-inspired diet and include peanuts occasionally. That's a decision for you (and your doctor, especially if you have health conditions or allergies).
- **Skip the swap myth.** Nothing in this study shows that almonds, walnuts, or other nuts produce the same effect. Don't assume they do.

The honest takeaway for Paleo eaters is not "switch to peanuts." It's "notice what the study's snack looked like, and build similar habits with foods you already eat."

## Borrow the habits, not the peanut

The study's snack had a few qualities worth copying:

- **Unsalted.** No added sodium, no flavored coatings.
- **Whole and minimally processed.** Skin-roasted, nothing fancy.
- **Consistent.** The same portion, every day, for four months.
- **A measured portion.** 60 grams is about 2 ounces, which is a real handful, not a nibble.

You can apply all four to Paleo-friendly nuts and seeds. Just keep your expectations honest: you'd be building a sensible snack habit, not replicating the study's results.

## Easy Paleo snack ideas that follow the same logic

### Pre-portioned nut cups
Weigh out a daily serving of unsalted almonds, walnuts, or pecans and bag them for the week. Weighing once with a kitchen scale takes five minutes and removes the guesswork. Nuts are calorie-dense, so portioning matters.

### Dry-roast your own
Spread raw, unsalted nuts on a sheet pan and roast at 325°F for about 10 to 15 minutes, stirring once, until fragrant. Cool them completely before storing in a jar. You control the salt and avoid the oils some store-bought roasted nuts use.

### Pair them with fruit
A small handful of nuts with an apple or a few berries makes a snack that holds you longer than fruit alone. It's also easy to pack for work or errands.

### Savory trail mix, no sweeteners
Mix unsalted nuts, pumpkin seeds, and unsweetened coconut flakes. Make a week's worth on Sunday and divide into small containers.

## Why insulin keeps coming up

Insulin is best known for blood sugar, but researchers have been looking at how well the brain responds to it as part of the aging picture. That's the thread this study pulls on. You don't need to chase a single food to act on the general idea. Meals built around protein, vegetables, and healthy fats, with fewer refined sugars, already fit a pattern that supports steady blood sugar. That's a reasonable approach on its own merits, separate from any one study.

## How to read a small study without overhyping it

Next time a food gets a headline, run through four quick questions:

1. **How big was it?** Small studies suggest; they rarely settle anything.
2. **Who took part?** Here, healthy older adults. Results may not apply to everyone else.
3. **What was measured?** Biological markers are not the same as health outcomes.
4. **Does it need replication?** One trial is a starting point.

The reasonable response to this study is mild interest, not a grocery overhaul.

## The bottom line

Pick one unsalted, whole-food snack that fits your eating style, portion it out tonight, and make it a daily habit. Consistency counts for more than any single headline ingredient.
