Your kid comes home crushed because she wasn't invited to a birthday party. Every instinct says to fix it: call the other parent, plan something bigger, make the hurt disappear. A child psychologist, writing for Fox News, argues you should do something harder and more useful. Stay with her in the feeling instead of rescuing her from it.

By the end of this piece, you'll have a simple way to tell real harm from ordinary childhood pain, a few scripts for the moment a letdown lands, and an easy family-table habit that makes those conversations more natural.

## The Core Idea: Support Is Not Rescue

Per the Fox News piece, the argument is that manageable struggles build resilience, self-efficacy, and emotional coping skills. When a parent swoops in to erase disappointment, the child misses the chance to learn she can get through it. Rescue sends a quiet message: this was too much for you to handle.

Support sends the opposite one. You are here, the feeling is real, and you trust her to work through it.

The article uses the birthday-party exclusion as its example. It stings, but it is a normal childhood hurt, not a crisis. The author also points to a broader parenting culture focused on intervention and preventing discomfort, which can blur the line between real harm and ordinary pain.

## Scripts for the Moment It Happens

These are my adaptations of the support-over-rescue idea, not quotes from the article. Use your own words, but keep the shape: name the feeling, stay close, and hold back the fix.

- **Name it:** "That sounds really disappointing. I'd feel left out too."
- **Stay present:** "Do you want to talk, or do you want company while we do something else?"
- **Hand the problem back, gently:** "What do you think might help you feel a little better tonight?"
- **Trust her:** "This is hard, and I know you can get through hard things."

Notice what's missing: no "it's not a big deal," no "they're not nice anyway," and no plan to confront anyone. Dismissing the feeling and fixing the cause both skip the part where she learns to cope.

## Signs You Might Be Over-Intervening

Ask yourself honestly:

- Do you feel an urgent pull to make the bad feeling stop, for her or for you?
- Are you contacting teachers, coaches, or other parents about small social bumps?
- Do you solve problems before your child has tried to?
- Does she look to you first when something goes wrong, instead of trying something herself?

If you said yes more than once, you're in good company. The urge to protect is loving. It just works best when aimed at real danger.

## When to Step In vs. Stay Out

The article's distinction is between real harm and ordinary pain. Here is a practical way to sort them. This framework is my own reading of that idea, so treat it as a starting point.

### Stay close but stay out when:
- The hurt is a normal social or performance letdown: a missed invitation, a lost game, a part she didn't get.
- She is upset but still functioning: eating, sleeping, and bouncing back within a reasonable time.
- She has some ability to cope or respond on her own.

### Step in when:
- Her safety is at risk.
- The problem is ongoing and she is clearly overwhelmed.
- Her mood, sleep, or daily routine changes sharply and stays changed.

In those cases, involve the adults and professionals who can help. Letting kids struggle with manageable things does not mean ignoring serious ones.

## Make the Table a Soft Landing

Hard conversations go easier when they don't feel like conversations. Side-by-side activity lowers the pressure, and cooking together is a natural one. When your child is stewing over a letdown, try handing her a task instead of a lecture.

A one-pan dinner works well because the jobs are simple and hands stay busy:

1. Toss chopped sweet potato and chicken thighs with olive oil, salt, and pepper on a sheet pan.
2. Roast at 425°F for about 30 to 35 minutes, until the chicken reaches 165°F inside.
3. Let your child squeeze the lemon, add the herbs, or set the table while you listen.

You're not forcing a talk. You're making room for one. If she opens up, use the scripts above. If she doesn't, you've still shown up, which is the point.

## What She Learns When You Don't Fix It

The payoff the article describes is self-efficacy: the belief that she can handle hard things. That belief only forms through handling them. Each small disappointment she survives, with you beside her, is practice for bigger ones later.

Next time the hurt arrives, resist the first fix. Say what you see, stay in the room, and trust her to find her footing.
