Vogue has put a question to six stylish people that plenty of us have been muttering over a bag of plastic spiders: can Halloween be chic? The magazine's roundup, published this week, gathers advice from Zoë de Givenchy, Romilly Newman, Rebecca Gardner, Kelly Wearstler, Luke Edward Hall and Jon Gray on how to host a spooky-season party without the kitsch. Per Vogue, the advice covers decor, costumes, food, drinks and what to avoid.

I can only work from Vogue's framing and the guest list here, not each tastemaker's individual quotes, so I won't put words in anyone's mouth. What I can do is read the moment, and the lineup itself says plenty.

## The Lineup Is the Thesis

Six names from fashion, interiors and design, all asked about the same holiday. Whatever each of them says, the premise is clear: Halloween has become a serious hosting and style occasion, and a certain kind of host wants it to look as considered as a dinner party.

That tracks with the broader appetite Vogue's piece taps into: curated entertaining, dramatic tablescapes and what the outlet describes as "quiet luxury" seasonal dressing. Picture a party that feels like an atmospheric film set rather than the aisle of a party store.

## The Angle: Grown-Up Halloween Is an Editing Exercise

Here's my take, and it is only that: a chic Halloween is mostly about what you leave out. The complaint behind Vogue's premise is specific. People want something more grown-up than plastic pumpkins and disposable partyware. That's a subtraction problem.

If you want to borrow the spirit of the piece at home, the editing questions are simple:

- **Does it look like a party-store product?** If so, it's the first thing to go.
- **Is it disposable?** Partyware is the other named offender. Dishes you already own, set properly, do more work than anything printed with a skeleton.
- **Would it make sense in your home in November?** Decor that survives past November 1 usually reads as design rather than costume.

## Where to Spend Your Effort

Vogue's roundup covers four territories, and they make a handy hosting checklist.

### Decor

The report for decor is elevated rather than themed. Dim the lighting, keep the palette tight, and let a few deliberate choices carry the mood instead of scattering novelty items everywhere. Think restraint, not volume.

### Costumes

Costumes are on the agenda too, which tells you something: the grown-up Halloween party still wants dressing up, just with more intention. If you're someone who dreads the whole thing, a single committed gesture beats a full head-to-toe gag outfit. That's my suggestion, not the panel's.

### Food and Drinks

Vogue includes menus and drinks in its coverage, and this is the easiest place to look sophisticated. A thoughtful cocktail and a few well-made bites signal "host with taste" faster than a punch bowl with a plastic hand floating in it.

### What to Avoid

The piece is framed around a kitsch-free party, so avoidance is half the point. The general lesson: when in doubt, cut the gag item.

## A Fair Counterpoint

There's a real argument that kitsch is the whole charm of Halloween. The holiday has always been about a little campy overacting, and a party with zero cheese can tip into stiff. It's hard not to wonder whether the best version is a hybrid: one gorgeous, moody table, plus one unapologetically silly element you actually love. A chic host can still own a fog machine.

## The Takeaway

Vogue's six tastemakers are essentially answering a question many hosts are already asking: how do you keep the fun of Halloween without the look of a clearance bin? The answer, filtered through my own read, is that taste here means discipline. Pick a mood, commit to it, and let the plastic pumpkins have their own party.
