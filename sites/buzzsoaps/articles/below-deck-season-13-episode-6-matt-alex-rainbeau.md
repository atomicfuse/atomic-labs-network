Matt got the job. According to Vulture's recap of *Below Deck* Season 13, Episode 6, titled "Over the Rainbeau," Matt's maneuvering paid off this week when he replaced Alex as bosun. Meanwhile, Rainbeau's tense leadership style is starting to fracture the interior team. Vulture's description sums up the state of the boat: finishing a good charter helps the vibes, but the crew keeps being dysfunctional as the attitudes of Matt, Alex, and Rainbeau wear on everyone.

The angle worth committing to here is the bosun swap. Is Matt's promotion earned leadership, or the opening chapter of a villain origin story?

## The Swap Everyone Saw Coming (Or At Least Matt Did)

Per Vulture, the episode lands at a turning point in the season, where simmering personality conflicts stop being just personality conflicts and become structural crew changes. That's the real news. Bad vibes on a yacht are a dime a dozen. A reshuffled deck department is a plot.

Vulture frames Matt's rise as the result of his maneuvering. That word choice matters. Nobody is accusing him of anything beyond playing the game, and on a Bravo boat, playing the game is arguably the job description. But it's hard not to wonder where ambition ends and opportunism begins, and that's the question the episode seems built to leave hanging.

The case for Matt as strategic truth-teller: when a department is struggling, someone who says so out loud and then steps up has at least a defensible story. The case for Matt as power hungry: someone who says so out loud and then gets the promotion also fits the story. Both readings fit the same facts, which is exactly why reality TV editors love a guy like this.

## Alex and the Perils of Passive Management

On the other side of the swap is Alex, demoted from bosun. Vulture's recap lists Alex's attitude among the things wearing on the crew, so this isn't a case of a flawless leader getting ambushed. Still, the take here is ours, not theirs: a demotion is often less about one bad day than a slow drip of not managing. If the deck team lost faith, it's easy to see how a more assertive colleague found daylight.

It also makes for a nice workplace parable. The person who doesn't take charge leaves a vacuum, and the person who does, whatever their motives, fills it. Anyone who has sat through an office reorg knows the genre.

## Rainbeau's Iron Fist Meets the Interior Team

Upstairs, the story is a different flavor of trouble. Vulture reports that Rainbeau's tense leadership style is fracturing the interior team, and the recap's title is a pun on exactly that: the crew is, to put it plainly, over Rainbeau.

The parallel with the deck is neat. Downstairs, the issue is a leader who couldn't hold the team. Upstairs, it's a leader holding it too tightly. Two opposite management failures, one boat. It's the kind of symmetry that suggests the season is going to keep poking at the same question: what does a crew need from the person in charge?

## Why This Is the Good Stuff

Vulture's argument is that both departments are sliding into dysfunction, with production-friendly power shifts setting up even more chaos. That tracks with how this franchise works. A new bosun, an isolated chief stew, and a charter that went well enough to mask the cracks is a recipe for tension that boils over later.

There's a real pleasure in watching a good charter coexist with a bad workplace. The guests leave happy. The crew does not. It's *Below Deck* at its most honest: the job gets done, and the people doing it are barely speaking.

## What to Watch Next

Two questions to carry into the next episode:

- **Does Matt lead, or just win?** Getting the title is the easy part. How he runs the deck now is the actual test of the villain-or-truth-teller debate.
- **Does Rainbeau adjust?** An alienated team doesn't usually fix itself, and the interior crew has shown it's reached a breaking point.

The real tell of the week is that nobody on this boat seems to be asking whether the dysfunction is the problem. They're too busy asking who gets to run it.
