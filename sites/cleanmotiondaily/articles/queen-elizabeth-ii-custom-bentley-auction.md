A custom Bentley built for Queen Elizabeth II is heading to auction, and it comes with a detail that has caught people's attention: a feature tied to the Queen's handbag. Per Forbes, the Barnato Green sedan is one of only two of its kind made for the monarch, and it stayed in royal household service until 2019.

If you follow the collector car market, this is a useful case study. You'll come away knowing what is actually confirmed about this car, and how to think about why provenance can change what a luxury car is worth.

## What we know about the Bentley

The details available so far are short, so here they are plainly, as reported by Forbes:

- It is a sedan built for Queen Elizabeth II.
- It is finished in Barnato Green.
- It is one of only two such sedans made for the Queen.
- It served the royal household until 2019.
- It is going to auction, and it includes a handbag-related twist.

The reporting I have doesn't spell out the auction house, the date, the estimate, or exactly how the handbag feature works. Check the auction listing for those specifics before you make any plans around the sale.

## The handbag detail, and why it's the story

The handbag element is the part people will remember, and it points to something real about bespoke cars. A vehicle built for a head of state isn't just a standard model with nicer trim. It gets shaped around the habits of the person riding in it.

For a monarch who was famously rarely seen without her handbag, a feature built around it makes intuitive sense. I can't tell you from the available reporting what the feature physically is, so I won't guess. What I can say is that this kind of small, personal accommodation is exactly what separates a commissioned car from a catalog one.

## Why provenance moves the price

This is the most useful takeaway for any buyer, royal-adjacent or not. Two cars can share an engine, a badge and a body style, yet sell for very different sums because of who owned them and how they were used.

Here is how to think about it:

### Rarity is documented, not assumed

"One of only two" is a claim that can be verified, and verifiable scarcity carries weight. When you evaluate any collector car, ask what paperwork backs up the production number.

### A continuous chain of use matters

This Bentley served the royal household through 2019. A clear, known service history gives buyers confidence about what the car is and where it has been.

### A story you can tell in one sentence

The cars that become auction headlines usually have a hook anyone can repeat. A green Bentley, built for the Queen, with a handbag twist, fits that perfectly.

## What to look for if you're tempted to bid

I can't predict where this car will land on price, and nothing in the reporting offers an estimate. But if you're considering any provenance-heavy car, these steps help:

1. **Read the full catalog entry.** Look for documentation of the royal service, build records and any modifications.
2. **Separate the car from the legend.** Ask what you'd value if the royal link vanished. If the answer is "not much," you're buying the story, which is fine, but price it that way.
3. **Ask about the bespoke features.** Custom touches like the handbag detail can be hard to replace or service, so understand their condition.
4. **Factor in running costs.** A rare Bentley still needs specialist maintenance, storage and insurance.
5. **Set a ceiling before the auction.** Provenance sales are emotional, and bidding wars reward people who skip this step.

Royal memorabilia and ultra-rare luxury cars both attract strong interest, and this sale sits where they overlap.
