Some meals are about more than dinner. They carry a family's history, a place that's hard to reach, and a way of saying *we are still here*. That's the idea at the center of a profile from the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel (Jsonline) on Muna Riyad Sharma, a Milwaukee food artist who cooks Palestinian food as an act of resistance. Read on and you'll come away with a clearer sense of how heritage cooking works as cultural preservation, and a few ways to bring that lens to your own table, whether you're hosting, cooking, or just paying attention to where your food comes from.

## Who Muna Riyad Sharma is

Per Jsonline, Sharma grew up in Palestine and honors that heritage through her tablescape and halal catering company, Bint Jamila's Table. She is described as a food artist, which is a useful clue to how she works. The food is only part of it. The table itself, how it's set and arranged and presented, is part of the message.

That's the first takeaway: if you cook for other people, presentation can be storytelling, not decoration.

## Why the table matters as much as the dish

A tablescape is the whole composition of a meal: the dishes, the serveware, the arrangement, the way guests are invited to gather. Sharma's business pairs this with halal catering, meaning the food is prepared in line with Islamic dietary guidelines. Together, the two describe a way of cooking that puts hospitality and identity in the same frame.

If you want to borrow the idea at home, a few practical moves follow from it:

- **Serve family-style.** Shared platters put the focus on gathering, not individual plates.
- **Know the story of each dish.** Before you cook something from a heritage tradition, find out who it belongs to and how it's traditionally served. Tell your guests.
- **Give the table a point of view.** Choose serveware and arrangement to support the food rather than compete with it.

These are our suggestions, not details reported about Sharma's own events. The profile's point is the principle: the setting is part of the meal.

## Food as cultural preservation

Jsonline frames Palestinian food as both cultural preservation and resistance. That framing rests on something simple. Recipes are one of the few pieces of heritage that can travel with a person. They don't need a passport, and they pass between generations through hands and kitchens, not archives.

For anyone living far from where their family comes from, cooking becomes a way to keep a place present. Each dish is a small record of family and homeland, and making it again means keeping that record alive.

The timing of this conversation matters too. Palestinian identity, culture, and visibility are at the center of global discussion, and food is an approachable way into the story. A meal invites curiosity without requiring anyone to start with a debate.

## An immigrant entrepreneur's kitchen in Milwaukee

Sharma's company also belongs to a broader pattern: local food businesses built by people carrying a cuisine from elsewhere into an American city. Bint Jamila's Table is a Milwaukee example. For readers who care about local food scenes, the useful habit is to seek out and book these caterers and cooks directly, since a catering company is often a way to taste something you won't find on a standard restaurant menu.

If you're planning an event, consider:

1. **Ask what the caterer is proudest of.** Their answer usually points to the dishes with the most history behind them.
2. **Confirm dietary standards early.** If you need halal food for guests, say so at the start of the conversation.
3. **Let them shape the menu.** A cook working from heritage knows the right order and balance of a spread better than a generic template does.

## Where coffee fits in

This is a coffee site, so it's fair to point out the obvious connection. Coffee is often the closing gesture of hospitality in many cultures: the pot comes out when the meal ends and the conversation begins.
