Ahead of Australian Food Safety Week (Nov. 7 to 14, 2026), the Food Safety Information Council has released research showing 60% of Australians eat raw fish or seafood, and 17% do so weekly, per Medicalxpress. This year's theme is "Raw food know the risk." This is a food-safety piece rather than a coffee one, but if you like making sushi or ceviche at home, it's practical reading for the kitchen. Below: where people buy raw fish, what can go wrong, and the specific steps that reduce your risk.

## Where Australians Get Their Raw Fish

The survey figures, as reported, show the split between buying out and making at home:

- 68% of raw fish purchases come from sushi bars
- 40% come from cafes or restaurants
- 32% are premade from supermarkets or fishmongers
- 18% prepare their own raw fish dishes at home
- 5% make raw dishes from self-caught fish
- 4% buy premade raw fish dishes from unregulated social media vendors

The last figure is small, but it's the one to think about. A regulated outlet has to meet food safety rules. An anonymous social media seller may not.

## What Can Actually Make You Sick

The warnings cover several distinct hazards:

- **Bacteria:** Vibrio parahaemolyticus and Vibrio vulnificus, linked to time-temperature abuse (fish left warm too long).
- **Scombroid poisoning:** associated with fish like tuna and mackerel.
- **Shellfish toxins.**
- **Ciguatera poisoning:** from predatory fish such as Spanish mackerel, barracuda and grouper.
- **Mercury:** a concern with shark (sold as flake).
- **Parasites** in raw seafood.
- **Listeria monocytogenes:** a particular risk for vulnerable people.

The common thread is that several of these come down to handling and temperature, which is the part you control at home.

## Step 1: Buy From a Source You Can Trust

Use reputable suppliers, and look for fish labelled under the Australian Fish Names Standard. Your fishmonger should be able to tell you what the fish is. If a seller can't or won't, buy something else.

## Step 2: Freeze It First

To kill parasites, freeze fish for a minimum of seven days at minus 20°C. Check that your freezer actually holds that temperature, since a door that's opened often can run warmer than the dial suggests.

## Step 3: Skip the Marinade Myth

Vinegar, lemon juice and salt do not reliably kill parasites or pathogens. That matters for ceviche in particular, where citrus is the whole technique. Treat the acid as flavour, not protection. Freezing is the step that handles parasites.

## Step 4: Keep Your Hands, Tools and Surfaces Clean

- Wash your hands for 20 seconds before and after handling raw fish.
- Wash and dry utensils and cutting boards thoroughly.
- Scrub bamboo rolling mats with a brush, soap and hot water. Their texture is easy to overlook, but it's exactly where residue hides.

## Step 5: Cool Sushi Rice Quickly

Rice is part of the safety picture, not just the fish. Cool sushi rice quickly by spreading it into small containers and putting them in the fridge, rather than leaving a big pot on the bench. This limits the time-temperature abuse mentioned above.

## Step 6: Handle Your Own Catch Immediately

If you're in the 5% who use self-caught fish, gut and ice the fish immediately after catching it. Speed and cold matter here. Also remember that ciguatera is linked to predatory fish like barracuda and grouper, so the species you catch matters too.

## Step 7: Know Who Should Skip Raw Fish

Pregnant women, older adults and people with weakened immune systems are considered vulnerable. For these groups, the guidance is to cook fish to at least 63°C. Listeria is a specific concern for them, so a cooked option is the safer order at a sushi bar or at your own table.

## The Takeaway

Before your next batch of sushi or ceviche, put the fish in the freezer for seven days at minus 20°C, and don't count on lemon juice to do that job for you.
