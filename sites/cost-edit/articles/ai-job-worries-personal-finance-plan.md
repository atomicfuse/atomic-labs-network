Mark Tepper, CEO of Strategic Wealth Partners, says fears of AI causing mass layoffs are overblown. Per Fox Business, he argues AI will mostly automate monotonous tasks and act as a productivity and efficiency tool for businesses rather than a mass-unemployment machine.

Nobody can say for certain how this plays out, and Tepper's view is one perspective. The good news is that you don't need to settle the debate to act. A few inexpensive moves leave you in decent shape whether he turns out to be right or wrong. Here they are, in order of usefulness.

## Start With the Boring Safety Net

If AI changes your industry, the thing that helps most is time: time to look for the next role without taking the first bad offer. That means cash.

A simple way to size it:

1. Add up your monthly must-pay costs: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments, phone, internet.
2. Multiply by three for a starter cushion, or six if your income is variable or your field is changing fast.
3. Divide by the number of months you want to take to get there.

Example with made-up numbers: $2,800 in essentials x 3 months = $8,400. Saving over 14 months works out to $600 a month. If that is too steep, $150 a month is still $1,800 in a year, which covers a missed paycheck or two of surprises.

This is not an AI-specific tactic. It protects you from layoffs for any reason, which is the point.

## Free Up the Money by Auditing Recurring Costs

The fastest source for that $150 or $600 is usually subscriptions and bills you've stopped noticing. Spend one evening on this:

- Pull the last two months of card and bank statements.
- Highlight every recurring charge: streaming, apps, cloud storage, gym, delivery memberships.
- Mark each as keep, pause, or cancel. If you haven't used it in 30 days, it's a pause or cancel.
- Check your internet and mobile plans for promo rates that have expired. A plan that quietly jumped in price is a common leak.

Use a simple table so the math is easy to verify:

| Item | Monthly cost | Annual cost (x12) |
|---|---|---|
| Example: unused streaming service | $15.00 | $180.00 |
| Example: duplicate cloud storage | $3.00 | $36.00 |
| Example: gym you skip | $30.00 | $360.00 |
| **Total** | **$48.00** | **$576.00** |

These figures are illustrations, not quoted prices. Swap in your own, and note the date you checked so you can compare after renewals.

## Treat AI Tools as a Skill, Not a Subscription Pile

Tepper's framing, as reported by Fox Business, is that AI takes over repetitive work. If that holds, the workers who benefit are likely those who can use the tools to get routine tasks done faster. That is our reading of his point, not something he spelled out as a to-do list.

You can test this cheaply:

- Pick one repetitive task in your week, such as summarizing notes, drafting routine emails, or organizing data.
- Try a free tool on it for two weeks and time yourself before and after.
- Only pay for a tool if it saves you measurable hours. A $20 monthly plan costs $240 a year, so it should clearly earn that back.

Watch for the trap of stacking several paid tools that overlap. That is just another subscription leak, and you can run it through the same audit above.

## Know Which Parts of Your Job Are Repetitive

If automation targets monotonous tasks, as Tepper argues, it helps to know how much of your own week fits that description. Try this:

1. List what you did over the past week.
2. Label each task as repetitive (same steps every time) or judgment-based (dealing with people, making decisions, handling exceptions).
3. Estimate the share of your hours in each.

If most of your time is repetitive, that is a signal to build skills toward the judgment side, such as client communication, problem-solving, or oversight of the tools themselves. If most of it is already judgment-based, your priority is learning to hand off the dull parts.

## Keep Your Investing Calm

AI headlines can push people toward panic selling or chasing the latest hot theme. Tepper is a wealth manager, and Fox Business framed his comments around business efficiency, but his view is not a recommendation for you to buy or sell anything.

A steady approach is boring and effective:

- Keep emergency cash separate from investments, so a layoff never forces you to sell at a bad time.
- Keep retirement contributions going, at least up to any employer match.
- Avoid concentrating money in one theme because a story sounds convincing.

If you want personalized advice, a fee-only financial planner can review your situation. Ask how they are paid before you sign anything.

## What If He's Wrong?

Tepper dismisses the mass-layoff fears, but worry about displacement is real and widespread as companies adopt more automation. The plan above does not require you to pick a side. A bigger cash cushion, a leaner monthly budget, and a practical skill set all pay off if AI is merely a helpful tool, and they matter even more if it disrupts your field.

## Your Next Step

This week, set aside 30 minutes, open your last two statements, and cancel or pause one subscription you haven't used. Send that monthly amount straight into a savings account. It is a small move, but it starts the cushion that makes every other career decision easier.
