If you own your home but live mostly on a government pension, aged care can look affordable on paper and feel unaffordable in practice. A new study out of Australia shows just how common that gap is, and it gives you a clear reason to check your own numbers before care is needed.

## What the study found

Researchers from Flinders University and the South Australian Health and Medical Research Institute (SAHMRI) looked at more than 920,000 older Australians applying for aged care, as reported by Medicalxpress. Three findings stand out:

- Nine in 10 applicants receive social welfare payments.
- For those who rely on welfare, payments make up 84% to 91% of their income.
- Most appear asset-rich but income-poor, despite owning a home.

The researchers raise the question of whether Australia's retirement income system can sustain aged care demand as the population ages. Per the report, the ratio of working-age people to retirees is also shifting in an unfavourable direction.

## Why a home doesn't solve the cash-flow problem

A house is a large number on a balance sheet, but it doesn't pay a weekly bill. If nearly all your regular income is a pension, your day-to-day cash is fixed, while the value sits locked in bricks and mortar.

That is the trap the study describes. On paper you look comfortable. In practice, any new recurring cost has to come from a small, steady income or from the home itself.

This matters because of timing. Australia is rolling out reforms, including the new Support at Home Program, which includes means-testing, per the report. When a program tests means, how your assets and income are counted can change what you pay. We can't tell you from this study exactly how the formulas apply to you, so treat the next section as a checklist of questions, not a calculation.

## A simple worksheet to run before you need care

You can do this in an hour at the kitchen table. Use the same unit, dollars per fortnight, for everything so the comparison is easy to check.

### Step 1: List your regular income

Write down every payment that arrives each fortnight: pension, any superannuation income, any other source. Note what share is government payment. The study found 84% to 91% for welfare-reliant applicants, so see where you sit against that range.

### Step 2: List your fixed costs

Rates, utilities, insurance, phone, internet, food, medications. These are the bills that continue whatever happens with care. Use your last three statements, and write the date next to each figure so you can see when it goes stale.

### Step 3: Find the gap

Income minus fixed costs is what you have available for care contributions. If that number is small, the home is likely to be part of the conversation. Knowing this early gives you time to explore options rather than deciding in a hurry.

### Step 4: Ask the means-testing questions

Before you or a relative applies, ask the assessor or the relevant government service:

1. How is my home treated under the Support at Home Program's means-testing?
2. How is my income counted, and does the pension count in full?
3. What would I pay each fortnight at my current income?
4. What changes if my income or assets change?

Write down the answers and the date you received them. Rules in a reform period can shift.

## The debate you should know about

Means-testing is contested. Supporters argue that people with assets should contribute to their own care. Critics worry that, for people whose wealth is mostly a family home, it can push toward selling. The study doesn't settle that argument. What it does show is that the group being tested is mostly on low regular income, which is why the design details matter.

## What younger readers should take from this

If you're in your 30s, 40s or 50s, the study is a preview, not someone else's problem. The researchers' concern about the worker-to-retiree ratio suggests future retirees may face pressure on the same system. Practical moves that cost little:

- Check your superannuation balance and contribution history once a year. Gaps in contributions compound over decades.
- Plan for retirement income, not just retirement wealth. A paid-off home is valuable, but you also need a reliable cash stream.
- Keep a running list of your recurring bills now. Trimming them is easier at 45 than at 80.

## If you're helping a parent

Ask to sit down with their statements and fill in the worksheet together. Many people only discover the gap between their assets and their income when care is urgent. Doing it calmly, with dates on every figure, means you can compare options fairly.

Start this week: add up your fortnightly income, subtract your fixed costs, and write down the number. That single figure tells you how much room you really have.
