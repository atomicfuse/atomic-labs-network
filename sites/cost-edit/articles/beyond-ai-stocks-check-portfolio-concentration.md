Among the non-AI names mentioned as opportunities: Eli Lilly and UnitedHealthcare. This article isn't a stock tip. ## Step 1: Find out what you already own

Most people who say they have no AI exposure are wrong, because broad index funds hold large technology companies. Here's how to check:

1. Log in to your 401(k), IRA, or brokerage account.
2. Open each fund's page and look for "top 10 holdings" and "sector weights."
3. Write down the percentage in technology and any large tech names you recognize.
4. Add up the results across all your accounts, not just one.

A simple worksheet looks like this. The numbers below are made up for illustration, so replace them with yours:

| Account | Balance | Tech share of fund | Tech dollars |
|---|---|---|---|
| 401(k) index fund | $40,000 | 30% | $12,000 |
| IRA growth fund | $20,000 | 45% | $9,000 |
| Brokerage (individual stocks) | $10,000 | 50% | $5,000 |
| **Total** | **$70,000** | | **$26,000 (37%)** |

The math: tech dollars divided by total balance. $26,000 ÷ $70,000 = 0.371, or about 37%. If that number surprises you, that's the point of the exercise.

## Step 2: Decide what concentration you're comfortable with

There's no universal right answer. A useful test: if your tech-heavy holdings dropped 20% in a bad month, what would that do to your plans?

Using the example above, 20% of $26,000 is $5,200. Ask yourself whether losing $5,200 on paper would change when you retire, buy a house, or pay for tuition. If yes, you're likely more concentrated than your timeline allows. If no, you may be fine.

## Step 3: Understand why healthcare gets mentioned

The coverage describes healthcare as a durable theme because demand for drugs and insurance doesn't depend on tech hype. You can see the logic in your own budget: you pay premiums and prescriptions whether or not a chatbot company has a good quarter. That recurring-demand idea is the reason analysts often discuss healthcare as a counterweight to growth-heavy portfolios.

Two cautions, which are our analysis and not the panel's:

- **A single company is not a sector.** Owning one drugmaker or one insurer adds company-specific risk. A healthcare fund spreads that out.
- **Different does not mean safe.** Healthcare stocks can fall too, and they carry their own risks, including regulation and pricing pressure.

## Step 4: Pick a method and count the costs

If you want more non-AI exposure, there are a few routes. Compare them using the same unit, annual cost per $10,000 invested:

| Option | What you get | Cost to check | Example math |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual stocks (e.g., the names the panel mentioned) | Concentrated bet on one company | Trading commission (often $0 at major brokers, confirm yours) | $0 fee, but high single-company risk |
| Healthcare sector fund or ETF | Many healthcare companies at once | Expense ratio | A 0.10% ratio costs $10 per year per $10,000 |
| Broad equal-weight or value fund | Less weight on the biggest tech names | Expense ratio | A 0.20% ratio costs $20 per year per $10,000 |

The expense ratios above are examples to show the math, not quotes. Look up the actual figure on each fund's page, and compare it with what you pay now. Fees compound over decades, so a 0.5-point gap on $70,000 is $350 a year.

## Step 5: Shift gradually, and mind the tax bill

If your numbers say you're overweight, you don't need to sell everything. Easier options:

- **Redirect new contributions.** Send your next several paychecks' worth of investing to the underweight area instead of selling anything.
- **Rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts.** In a 401(k) or IRA, swapping funds generally doesn't trigger a tax bill the way selling in a taxable brokerage account can.
- **Check holding periods in taxable accounts.** Selling a winner can create capital gains. Confirm with a tax professional if the amounts are large.

## The takeaway

Headlines about Eli Lilly, UnitedHealthcare, and the AI trade are a prompt, not an instruction. Spend ten minutes this week adding up the tech dollars across all your accounts, divide by your total, and decide whether that percentage matches how much risk you can actually sit through.
