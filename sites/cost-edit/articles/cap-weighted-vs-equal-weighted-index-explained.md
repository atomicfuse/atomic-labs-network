When the stock market headline says an index is up, it's natural to assume most stocks are up. That assumption can be wrong. Liz Ann Sonders, chief investment strategist at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, argued on Fox Business's "The Claman Countdown" that the true story is told under cap-weighted indexes, meaning the way a headline index is built can mask what's happening beneath the surface. Here's how to read an index more skeptically and check what your own money is actually exposed to.

## What "cap-weighted" means in plain terms

A cap-weighted index gives each company influence in proportion to its size (its market capitalization). Bigger companies move the index more. Smaller companies barely nudge it.

Think of it like a household budget index where rent counts for far more than streaming subscriptions. If rent jumps, your total spending jumps, even if every small expense stayed flat. In a cap-weighted stock index, the mega-cap companies are the rent.

An equal-weighted index does the opposite: every company in the index counts the same, whether it's enormous or modest. That makes it a useful comparison, because it shows how the typical stock is doing, not just the giants.

## Why a rising index doesn't always mean a healthy market

Per the framing of Sonders' discussion, a small group of large companies can dominate headline returns. When that happens, an index can rise while many of its members struggle. The headline number tells you about the biggest names. It may say much less about the rest.

This matters for a simple reason: you may feel diversified because you own an index fund, but the performance you see may depend heavily on a handful of companies.

### A simple way to picture it

Here's an illustrative example with made-up numbers, not real market data:

| Stock group | Share of index | Return |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mega-cap company | 50% | +20% |
| 9 other companies (combined) | 50% | -4% |

Cap-weighted result: (0.50 × 20%) + (0.50 × -4%) = 10% - 2% = **+8%**

Equal-weighted result (each of 10 companies counts 10%): (0.10 × 20%) + (0.90 × -4%) = 2% - 3.6% = **-1.6%**

Same ten companies, same moment, and one version says "up 8%" while the other says "down 1.6%." Real indexes are less extreme, but the mechanism is the same.

## What this means for everyday investors

You don't need to trade differently because of this. You do need to read the numbers differently. Three practical takeaways:

### 1. Compare the headline index to an equal-weighted version

Many fund providers publish equal-weighted versions of major indexes. If the cap-weighted index is well ahead of its equal-weighted twin over the same period, that gap is a clue that a few large stocks are doing the heavy lifting. If the two move together, gains are more broadly shared.

### 2. Look at what your fund actually holds

Open the fund's fact sheet and check the top ten holdings and what percentage of the fund they make up. This takes about two minutes and is free. If a small number of names make up a big slice, your "broad" fund is more concentrated than the label suggests.

### 3. Check your overlap

If you own an S&P 500 fund, a total market fund, and a growth fund, you may own the same mega-cap companies three times. Add up your exposure to the largest names across all accounts, including your 401(k), before assuming you're spread out.

## How to spot hidden market weakness

Sonders' core point, as framed in the Fox Business segment, is that headline performance can distort your read on market health, diversification, and risk. A few habits help you look past it:

- **Watch the gap.** A widening difference between cap-weighted and equal-weighted returns suggests narrow leadership.
- **Ask what's driving the gain.** If one or two sectors or companies explain most of a rally, the rally is narrower than the headline implies.
- **Remember the flip side of concentration.** When gains depend on a few giants, a setback in those same names can hit the index harder than the broader market's condition would suggest.

None of this predicts what happens next. It's a way of seeing your own exposure more clearly.

## Should you switch to equal-weighted funds?

Not automatically. Equal-weighted funds come with their own tradeoffs: they hold more of the smaller companies in an index, and they can behave differently from the headline index in both good and bad stretches. Fund fees also vary, so compare expense ratios the same way you'd compare plans from two internet providers: look at the full annual cost, not just the sticker rate.

For most people, the first step isn't buying something new. It's understanding what you already own.

## Your next step

Pull up the fact sheet for your biggest index fund today, write down the percentage held in its top ten companies, and compare its recent return to an equal-weighted version of the same index. That one number tells you how much of your "diversification" is really a bet on a few big names.
