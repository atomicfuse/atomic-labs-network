Fox Business ran a segment this week in which host Larry Kudlow argued that Nvidia's Jensen Huang undermined U.S. national security. Fox Business's description of the segment also refers to Huang's resignation as Nvidia's president and CEO amid an alleged affair with a suspected Chinese spy. We haven't independently confirmed those claims, and this article isn't about whether they're true. It's about what you do when a headline like that lands and you own the stock, directly or through a fund.

By the end, you'll have a five-minute routine for sizing your real exposure and verifying the news before you touch your money.

## Separate the report from the commentary

A segment like Kudlow's is commentary built on a news event. Those are two different things, and they deserve different levels of trust.

- **The event:** something a company or regulator has formally announced.
- **The take:** what a host, analyst, or commentator argues the event means.
- **The allegation:** a claim about conduct that hasn't been established through a formal process.

Fox Business describes Kudlow's view as a critique. It is an opinion, not a finding. If a headline hinges on an allegation, treat it as unproven until a primary source says otherwise.

## Check the primary source first

For a U.S.-listed company, leadership changes generally show up in the company's own press release and in filings on the SEC's EDGAR database (search the company name and look for Form 8-K). Here's the order to work in:

1. Open the company's investor relations page and read the announcement itself.
2. Search EDGAR for recent 8-K filings.
3. Only then read commentary, and note who is speaking and what they're selling, whether that's ratings, subscriptions, or a point of view.

If you can't find the claim in a primary source, you're reading someone's interpretation. That's fine to read. It's not a reason to trade.

## Size your actual exposure

Most people feel a headline about one famous company more than they should, because they don't know how much of their money is actually tied to it. Find out. In your brokerage or 401(k) app, open the fund's holdings page and look up the top ten positions and their percentage weights.

Here's the math using a hypothetical $10,000 balance. These are illustrative numbers, not real prices or real fund weights.

| Stock's weight in your portfolio | Dollars tied to it | Value after a 20% drop | Hit to your total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2% | $200 | $160 | -$40 (0.4%) |
| 5% | $500 | $400 | -$100 (1.0%) |
| 10% | $1,000 | $800 | -$200 (2.0%) |
| 25% | $2,500 | $2,000 | -$500 (5.0%) |

The formula is simple: balance × weight × drop percentage. A scary headline about a 5% holding costs you about 1% of your total even if the stock falls a fifth. A 25% holding is a different conversation, and that concentration is the real issue regardless of the news.

## Count the cost of reacting

Selling isn't free, and the costs are easy to miss.

- **Taxes:** In a regular taxable brokerage account, selling a winner can trigger capital gains tax. Shares held a year or less are generally taxed at higher short-term rates than shares held longer. Inside an IRA or 401(k), selling typically doesn't create a tax bill.
- **Trading fees:** Many brokers charge nothing for stock trades, but some funds charge redemption or short-term trading fees. Check the fund's prospectus.
- **Re-entry risk:** If you sell after a drop and the price recovers before you buy back in, you've locked in the loss and missed the rebound. If you never buy back, you've made a second decision, about where the money goes next, under stress.

Run the numbers before you click. If a 5% holding drops 20% and you sell, you've avoided further loss on $400. Paying $150 in tax on an old gain to do it would be a bad trade.

## Ask whether the story changes the business or just the mood

A headline can move a stock for a day without changing how the company earns money. Ask three questions:

1. Does this change what the company sells, to whom, or at what margin?
2. Is it a leadership or governance event, which can matter but is often short-lived, or a structural one?
3. Would I have bought this holding for reasons that this news actually touches?

Kudlow's segment touches a bigger theme, the way AI chips, China, and national security policy increasingly overlap. That's a genuine long-running debate in markets, and it can affect companies in the sector over years. It's also exactly the kind of story that is easy to turn into a reason for a panic sale. Match your response to the time horizon of your money. Cash you need for next year's tuition and a retirement account you won't touch for 25 years shouldn't react the same way.

## Set a rule before the next headline

Write a one-line policy now, while you're calm. For example: "I rebalance twice a year, or if any single holding exceeds 10% of my portfolio." A rule like that gives you something to do besides react. It also keeps you from making decisions based on a talking head, however confident.

If a story makes you nervous enough to want to sell, don't trade today. Open your holdings, find the weight, run the table above, and read the primary source. Then act on the math, not the segment.
