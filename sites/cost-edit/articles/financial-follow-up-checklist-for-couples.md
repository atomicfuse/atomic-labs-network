Caleb Hammer's channel posted a trailer this week for the return of Al & Stella to his "Financial Audit" series, with the full episode set to go live the next day, per the video description. Returning guests are a useful idea to borrow. Most of us do one big budget cleanup, feel good, and never look at the numbers again.

Here is how to run your own follow-up in about an hour, so you know whether your last money reset actually stuck.

We can't say what happens in that episode, and the trailer description doesn't say either. What we can do is take the premise, a second look at someone's finances after a first one, and turn it into something you can use with your partner, a roommate, or alone.

## Step 1: Pull the same numbers you used last time

A follow-up only works if you compare like with like. Open the budget, spreadsheet, or notes from your last review. If you never wrote anything down, that is your first finding. Start a simple record today.

Gather these, using the last full month of statements:

- Total take-home income
- Rent or mortgage and utilities
- Groceries and takeout, tracked separately
- Subscriptions and other recurring charges
- Minimum debt payments and the current balance on each debt
- Savings balance

Use the same time window each check-in. A single month is fine, as long as you keep using a month.

## Step 2: Build a before-and-after table

This is the core of the whole exercise. Make a table with three columns: last review, now, and change. Here is a template with made-up figures for illustration only:

| Category | Last review | Now | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Groceries | $500 | $450 | -$50 |
| Takeout | $300 | $340 | +$40 |
| Subscriptions | $80 | $65 | -$15 |
| Credit card balance | $4,000 | $3,700 | -$300 |
| Savings | $600 | $900 | +$300 |

These numbers are examples, not real data. Swap in yours. The goal is to see direction, not perfection. A category that moved the wrong way is not a failure, it is the item for your next 30 days.

## Step 3: Separate groceries from takeout

Food is where most households drift, because the spending is small and frequent. Keep two lines, not one.

Show the math per week so it feels real. If takeout was $340 last month, that is about $85 a week (340 ÷ 4). Ask whether you would knowingly pay $85 a week for that. If yes, keep it. If not, pick a number and write it down.

For groceries, compare price per unit, not package price, when you're deciding what to buy in bulk. A bigger pack only wins if the per-ounce or per-count price is lower and you will use it before it spoils.

## Step 4: Audit every recurring charge

Go line by line through your bank and card statements and list everything that repeats: streaming, apps, memberships, cloud storage, delivery passes.

For each one, ask:

1. Did I use it in the last 30 days?
2. Did the price change since I signed up?
3. Was it a promotional rate that has since ended?

Promo rates are the quiet culprit. Internet, mobile and streaming plans often start low and then renew higher. Check the renewal price and date for each, and if a bill jumped, call and ask what the current best plan is before you accept it.

## Step 5: Re-rank your debts

List each debt with its balance, interest rate and minimum payment. Credit cards, personal loans and student loans all go on the list. Then check two things:

- Is the total going down, or are you only paying the minimums while new charges pile on?
- Is any rate high enough that refinancing, consolidating, or a balance transfer could be worth pricing out?

If you shop for a loan or card, compare the total cost, including fees, not just the headline rate. Many products come with origination fees or transfer fees that change the math. Also be aware that some links on creator pages like Hammer's are affiliate links, so compare offers independently.

## Step 6: If you share money, talk about it with a script

Couples are where follow-ups tend to get tense. Hammer's show is built around exactly this kind of conversation, and the format suggests a useful rule: put the numbers on the table first, then talk.

Try this order:

1. Each person says one thing that improved since the last check-in.
2. Look at the before-and-after table together.
3. Each person names one spending category they would change, including their own.
4. Agree on one target number for the next month, not five.

Don't litigate individual purchases from weeks ago. Agree on the rules going forward.

## Step 7: Set the next date before you close the spreadsheet

The follow-up is only useful if there is another one. Put the next check-in on the calendar now, 30 days out for a tight budget or 90 days if things are steady. Keep it short: update the table, circle the biggest mover, pick one change.

## What to do tonight

Open your last statement, make the five-row table from Step 2, and fill in just the "now" column. Twenty minutes of honest numbers will tell you more than any amount of guessing about whether your money habits changed.
