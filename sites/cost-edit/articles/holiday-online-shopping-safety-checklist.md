Holiday shopping is the one stretch of the year when you buy from unfamiliar sites, expect packages constantly, and click fast on a deal. Scammers know it. Forbes reports that fraudsters are now using AI to make seasonal scams more convincing, which means the old advice of looking for typos only goes so far. Below are four scam types Forbes highlights in its "12 Scams of Christmas" series, plus a quick check for each that takes under a minute.

## Why These Scams Work Better This Year

Per Forbes, holiday spending, heavy shipping volume, and last-minute deal hunting create ideal conditions for scammers, who lean on urgency and distraction. AI adds polish: phishing messages, fake storefronts, and customer service impersonation can all look more believable than they used to.

That changes how you defend yourself. Instead of judging a message by how sloppy it looks, judge it by what it asks you to do. Hurry up, pay now, log in here, buy a gift card. Those requests are the signal, however clean the design.

## Fake Retailers

A fake retailer is a storefront built to take your money or card details and deliver nothing. AI makes it cheaper to produce professional-looking product pages and descriptions.

**Quick check before you buy from a store you don't know:**

1. Leave the ad or link. Type the retailer's name into a search engine yourself and see what independent sources say.
2. Be suspicious of a price far below every other seller. A deal that beats everyone by a wide margin deserves extra scrutiny.
3. Look for a real way to reach the company and a clear return policy before entering payment details.
4. Pay with a credit card rather than a debit card where you can, since it keeps your bank account out of the line of fire. (This is general consumer advice, not a claim from the Forbes piece.)

If you feel pushed by a countdown timer or a "only 2 left" banner, treat the pressure itself as a reason to slow down.

## Delivery Scams

With packages arriving constantly, a message saying your delivery is delayed or needs action feels routine. That is exactly why Forbes flags delivery scams: they borrow the normal rhythm of the season.

**What to do instead of tapping the link:**

- Don't click through from a text or email about a missing package. Open the retailer's app or type the carrier's website into your browser, then enter your tracking number there.
- Ask yourself whether you actually ordered anything that could match. If not, ignore it.
- Be wary of any message asking for a payment, however small, or personal details to "release" a package.

Keep a simple running list of what you've ordered and from where. It makes a fake notice stand out immediately.

## Gift Card Draining

Forbes lists gift card draining among the season's risks. In general terms, this is when a scammer gets hold of a card's number and PIN, often before the recipient uses it, and takes the balance. Gift cards are popular presents, which makes them a popular target.

**Ways to lower your risk:**

- Inspect cards on the rack. If packaging looks tampered with or the PIN area looks scratched or exposed, choose a different card.
- Buy cards from the issuer directly or from a retailer you trust, rather than a third-party seller you found through an ad.
- Tell recipients to use the balance soon rather than letting it sit.
- Treat any request to pay for anything with gift cards as a red flag. Legitimate businesses and agencies don't demand them as payment.

The last two points are standard consumer-protection advice rather than specifics from Forbes, but they fit the risk it describes.

## Identity Theft

The fourth item is the one that can outlast the holidays. A fake store, a phishing email, or a bogus delivery page can each collect your name, address, card number, or login details. The charge on your statement is the visible problem; the stolen identity is the long one.

**A short prevention routine:**

- Share only what a purchase needs. A retailer doesn't need extra personal details to ship a gift.
- Use unique passwords for shopping accounts, ideally with a password manager and two-step verification where offered.
- Check card and bank statements weekly through the end of January, not just monthly.
- If you entered details on a site you now doubt, contact your card issuer promptly and ask about replacing the card.

## A 30-Second Pre-Checkout Test

Before you pay anyone this season, run through these questions:

| Question | If the answer is "no" |
|---|---|
| Did I reach this site myself, not through a message or ad? | Search for the retailer independently |
| Does the price make sense compared to other sellers? | Assume it may be a trap |
| Can I find a real contact method and return policy? | Walk away |
| Am I being rushed? | Pause for ten minutes |

None of these checks cost anything, and they work whether the scam was written by a person or by AI.

## The One Habit That Covers All Four

Never act from the message. Whenever a text, email, or ad asks for money, a login, or a click, close it and go to the retailer, carrier, or card issuer yourself through an app or an address you typed. That single habit defuses fake stores, delivery notices, and most identity theft attempts before they start.
