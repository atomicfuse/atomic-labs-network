## What a tariff is and who pays it

A tariff is a tax on imported goods. It's collected from the importing business at the border, not from the foreign country. What happens next is a business decision. The importer can:

- Raise the shelf price to pass the cost along.
- Absorb the cost and accept thinner profit.
- Switch suppliers to a source that isn't tariffed.
- Do some mix of all three.

That's why tariff announcements don't produce an overnight price jump on everything. Effects tend to be uneven: some products change, others don't, and timing varies by store and by how much inventory is already on shelves. We can't predict which products will move from the announcement alone, and anyone claiming exact percentage increases is guessing.

## Where to look in your own budget

The reporting doesn't list affected products, so don't assume. Instead, watch categories where goods are commonly imported and where you buy often or in big-ticket amounts:

- **Household essentials and small appliances:** coffee makers, kitchen gadgets, vacuum cleaners.
- **Electronics and accessories:** printers, chargers, cables. Remember ink and replacement parts are part of the total cost.
- **Groceries with imported ingredients:** items where the label shows a country of origin.
- **Clothing and footwear.**

This is a watch list, not a forecast. Your own receipts will tell you what's actually changing.

## Build a 15-minute price log

You don't need a spreadsheet wizard's skills. Pick 8 to 10 items you buy regularly and record the same details each time.

### Step 1: Record a baseline this week

For each item, write down the store, the date, the size, and the price. Convert to a common unit so comparisons stay fair. Example format (these figures are placeholders to show the math, not real prices):

| Item | Store / date | Size | Price | Unit price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Item A | Your store, today | 32 oz | $6.40 | $0.20 per oz |
| Item B | Your store, today | 64 oz | $11.52 | $0.18 per oz |

The math: price divided by size equals unit price. $6.40 ÷ 32 = $0.20.

### Step 2: Recheck monthly

Same store, same size, same day of the month if you can. Changing any of those muddies the comparison. Note sales separately, since a promo can hide a real increase.

### Step 3: Calculate the change

New unit price minus old unit price, divided by the old one. If your $0.20 per oz item becomes $0.22, that's $0.02 ÷ $0.20 = 10%. Now you know the real number, not a headline.

## Respond to price changes without panic

When something does rise, you have options that cost nothing:

- **Compare unit prices across sizes and brands.** Bulk isn't always cheaper; the math decides.
- **Check store brands.** They're often made domestically or sourced differently, though that's not guaranteed, so check the label.
- **Delay non-urgent purchases.** For appliances and electronics, prices swing; waiting a few weeks and tracking costs nothing.
- **Factor in ongoing costs.** A cheaper printer with pricey ink can cost more than a pricier one with cheap refills. Add the purchase price to a year of consumables before deciding.

## The credit card rate cap: don't plan around it yet

Per the Fox Business segment, Trump is pushing for a 10% cap on credit card interest rates, and Liz Peek discussed possible consequences for credit card networks. A push is not a law. Nothing in the reporting says a cap is in place or when it might be, so don't change your plans based on it.

What holds regardless: if you carry a balance, your current APR is the number that matters. Look at your statement, find the rate, and multiply. A $2,000 balance at 24% APR costs roughly $480 a year in interest if left untouched, since $2,000 × 0.24 = $480. At 10% it would be $200. That gap is why paying down the highest-rate card first remains the most reliable move, whatever happens in Washington.
