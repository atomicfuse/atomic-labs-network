Rep. Jeff Van Drew, R-N.J., said on Fox Business's "The Evening Edit" that the major technology outages hitting companies across the U.S. show we have "vulnerabilities" and need to be careful, and he called for a federal response to secure critical infrastructure. Whatever you think of the policy debate, the household takeaway is simple: if the systems you use to pay, shop, and manage bills go down, you want a plan that costs little and takes an afternoon.

This guide covers what you can set up at home. It doesn't depend on any particular outage, and it will read the same in six months.

## What the outage story actually tells you

Per Fox Business, Van Drew's argument is that recent outages reveal serious weaknesses in critical systems and the need for more caution and resilience. The broader concern, as the coverage frames it, is how dependent businesses, government, and everyday life have become on digital systems that can be fragile.

That dependence shows up in your own budget. Rent, utilities, groceries, subscriptions, and paychecks often run through apps and online accounts. When one link breaks, the practical problem is rarely dramatic. It's a failed payment, a missed due date, or a checkout that won't load.

The steps below are my suggestions, not claims from the report.

## Step 1: Keep a small cash buffer

If card readers or banking apps are down, cash still works at many places. You don't need a large stash.

A simple way to size it: take your weekly spending on essentials and keep a portion on hand.

| Item | Example weekly cost (your numbers) |
|---|---|
| Groceries | $\_\_\_ |
| Fuel or transit | $\_\_\_ |
| Pharmacy/basics | $\_\_\_ |
| **Total** | **$\_\_\_** |

Fill in your own figures. Keeping even a few days of that total in small bills covers a short disruption. Store it somewhere safe at home, not in your wallet.

## Step 2: Make a one-page bill sheet

Write down, on paper or a printed page, each recurring bill with:

- the company name and customer service phone number
- the due date and typical amount
- how you normally pay (autopay, app, website)

If a payment fails during an outage, you can call and confirm the due date instead of guessing. Many providers can note an account or waive a late fee when a failure is on their end, but ask rather than assume. Keep a note of the date and who you spoke with.

## Step 3: Don't depend on one payment method

Check that you have at least two ways to pay for essentials: say, a debit card and a credit card from different institutions, plus the cash buffer above. If one network has trouble, another may not.

While you're at it, review which bills are on autopay. Autopay is convenient, but it also means a glitch can go unnoticed. Once a month, glance at your accounts to confirm payments actually went through.

## Step 4: Save offline copies of key documents

Store copies of the following somewhere that doesn't need the internet, such as a folder at home or a USB drive:

- account numbers and insurance policy details
- recent statements for your main accounts
- lease or mortgage information
- medication lists and prescriber contacts

This is also a good moment to audit what you keep. If a subscription's login info is only in an app, note how you'd reach the company if the app won't load.

## Step 5: Know your fallback for internet and phone

Home internet and mobile service are bills you pay for, so know what each gives you. Check whether your phone plan allows hotspot use and how much data it includes, since that can serve as a backup if home service drops. Look at the plan details for any limits or fees, and compare against your actual use before paying for extras.

A backup doesn't have to be a new purchase. Often it's a feature you already have but haven't read about.

## Step 6: Stock a modest pantry buffer

If a store's payment or inventory systems go down, a few days of shelf-stable food means you aren't forced into a pricey last-minute run. Buy extras when items you already eat are on sale, rather than all at once. Check unit prices (price per ounce or per count) so the buffer doesn't cost more than normal shopping.

## Step 7: Keep devices and chargers ready

Keep a charged power bank and a cable or two in a known spot. It's a small expense, and it keeps your phone usable for calls, texts, and contacting providers.

## What this doesn't solve

None of this secures critical infrastructure. That's the part Van Drew argues needs a federal response, and it's beyond what a household can fix. What you can control is how much a short disruption costs you in late fees, rushed purchases, and stress.

## The one thing to do this week

Print your one-page bill sheet and put a small cash buffer next to it. Those two steps cover most of what goes wrong when a system you rely on stops working.
