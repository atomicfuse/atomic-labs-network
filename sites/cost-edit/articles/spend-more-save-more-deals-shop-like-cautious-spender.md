A "spend $50, save $10" banner feels like a favor. A study involving Durham University Business School, reported by Phys, suggests it may be closer to a targeting decision. By the end of this article, you'll know how to test any threshold promo in about thirty seconds and how to avoid paying extra to unlock a discount.

## What the research found

Per Phys, the researchers found that retailers strategically target "carefree" shoppers with personalized offers. These are people who spend well beyond a discount threshold. "Cautious" shoppers, who spend only slightly above the minimum, receive fewer discounts.

The logic, as we read it, is that a retailer has little reason to hand a discount to someone who will spend freely anyway, unless that discount keeps them buying. Meanwhile, the cautious shopper who barely clears the bar is the one a threshold promo is supposedly built for. The study suggests that shopper gets fewer offers.

That's the reported finding. Anything beyond it, such as exactly how any given store scores you, isn't something the study summary tells us. Treat the rest of this piece as practical advice, not a description of any one retailer's system.

## The product type matters too

Per the same report, spend-more-save-more promotions work differently depending on what's being sold. They had little effect on preventive products like sunscreen, but a strong impact on promotional items like cosmetics.

The practical read: if you buy something you need regardless, such as sunscreen, a threshold deal probably isn't changing what you buy. If the item is a want, the deal is doing real work on your decision. That's the category where you should be most careful.

## The 30-second threshold test

Before you add items to reach a threshold, do this math.

1. **Write down your cart total without the filler.** This is what you came to buy.
2. **Find the gap to the threshold.** Threshold minus cart total.
3. **Compare the gap to the discount.** If the extra spend is larger than the savings, you lose money unless you'd have bought those items anyway.
4. **Ask whether you'd buy the add-on at full price this month.** If not, it's not savings.

### Worked example (illustrative numbers)

These are made-up figures to show the math, not a real store's prices.

| | Cart A: stay under | Cart B: hit the threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Items you need | $42.00 | $42.00 |
| Filler added | $0.00 | $8.00 |
| Subtotal | $42.00 | $50.00 |
| Discount ("spend $50, save $10") | $0.00 | -$10.00 |
| **You pay** | **$42.00** | **$40.00** |

Here the filler nets you $2 in savings, but only if the $8 item is something you'd use. If it sits in a drawer, you spent $40 on $42 worth of need, and the $8 item is a cost. Now change the gap: if you needed $30 of stuff and had to add $20 to reach $50, you'd pay $40 for $30 of need. The deal costs you $10 more than skipping it.

## Shop like a cautious spender

The study's "cautious" shopper stays close to the minimum. You don't need to change your personality to copy that. A few habits do the work.

### Keep a running list

Write what you need before opening the site or app. Anything not on the list gets a pause before checkout.

### Let the cart sit

If the promo is real, it will usually still be there tomorrow. A day's wait filters out impulse add-ons. We can't promise every offer lasts, so decide in advance what you're willing to lose.

### Plan the add-on in advance

If you must add something to hit a threshold, choose a consumable you already use: detergent, pet food, toothpaste. Check your usage first. A product you'll finish within a few months is a safer filler than anything decorative.

### Check the unit price

Threshold filler is often priced higher per unit than what you'd pay elsewhere. Compare in the same units, such as price per ounce or per sheet, before deciding the discount is worth it.

### Watch for fees that erase the savings

If shipping is free over a threshold, compare that with the actual shipping charge on your smaller order. Say shipping is $6 under the threshold. Adding $9 of filler to avoid it costs you $3 more than just paying shipping, and that's before you consider whether you wanted the filler.

## What to do if you get fewer offers

The research suggests discount offers may vary by shopper behavior. We can't tell you from this study whether a particular store is doing it to you. But you can test your own situation without guessing:

- **Compare logged in and logged out.** If the prices or promos differ, you've learned something about that store.
- **Look at the cart before signing up for emails.** Some stores send a first-order offer; check whether it beats the current sale.
- **Don't chase the "better" offer by spending more.** A bigger discount tied to a bigger spend is only a deal if you were going to spend that much.

## The bottom line

The Durham-linked research, as reported by Phys, points to a simple pattern: the shoppers who spend freely are the ones courted with discounts. You can't control what a retailer's system offers you, but you can control your cart. Before you add anything to reach a threshold, subtract what you'd have spent anyway from what you're about to spend. If the answer is bigger than the discount, close the tab.
