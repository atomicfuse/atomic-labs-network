Brian Belski, founder, CEO and CIO of Humilis Investment Strategies, compared current tech market conditions to the 1990s dot-com bubble on Fox Business's "Making Money," per Foxbusiness. You don't need to decide whether he's right. You can do something more useful: find out how much of your own money depends on the answer, and fix it in about twenty minutes.

This is general education, not personalized investment advice, and I can't tell you where the market is headed. Neither can the experts making the comparisons.

## What the comparison actually says

The reporting is thin on specifics, so here is what can be said. Per Foxbusiness, Belski drew a parallel between today's tech momentum and the dot-com era. The broader context in coverage: AI and mega-cap tech companies are driving much of the market's gains, which is why bubble comparisons keep surfacing.

A comparison is not a prediction. It's a prompt to ask a practical question: if a few big tech companies carry the market, how much of my portfolio is riding on them?

## Step 1: Find out what your funds actually hold

Most people who own a broad index fund or a target-date fund in a 401(k) don't know what's inside. Here's how to check:

1. Log in to your retirement account or brokerage.
2. Open each fund's page and look for "Top 10 holdings" and "Sector breakdown."
3. Write down the percentage in technology and the combined percentage of the top 10 holdings.

If you own a fund that tracks a market-cap-weighted index, the largest companies take up the largest share by design. That's how the index works, and it's why a rally led by mega-cap tech lifts your balance without you doing anything.

## Step 2: Add up your overlap

Overlap is where people get surprised. You might own an S&P 500 index fund, a growth fund, and a tech sector fund, and all three hold the same few companies.

Use a simple worksheet. The numbers below are made-up examples to show the math, not real fund data:

| Account | Balance | Tech share of fund | Tech dollars |
|---|---|---|---|
| 401(k) index fund | $40,000 | 30% | $12,000 |
| IRA growth fund | $10,000 | 45% | $4,500 |
| Brokerage tech ETF | $5,000 | 90% | $4,500 |
| **Total** | **$55,000** | | **$21,000** |

Here, $21,000 ÷ $55,000 = about 38% in tech. Swap in your own figures. The number itself isn't good or bad. What matters is whether you chose it on purpose.

## Step 3: Ask the one question that matters

If your tech-heavy holdings dropped 30%, what would that do to you? Using the example above, 30% of $21,000 is $6,300 on paper.

Then ask when you need the money:

- **More than 10 years away:** a drop is uncomfortable but usually recoverable with time and continued contributions.
- **Within 5 years (house down payment, tuition):** a big concentration in one sector is a bigger risk, because you may have to sell at a bad moment.

That timeline question matters more than any bubble debate.

## Step 4: Know the warning signs of hype

Bubble comparisons are about the gap between enthusiasm and fundamentals. You can watch for this in your own behavior, too:

- You're buying something mainly because it went up recently.
- You can't explain how the company makes money.
- You're putting in more than you planned because you feel you're missing out.
- One holding has quietly grown to a large share of your portfolio.

Momentum can last a long time, and it can reverse. A rising price alone doesn't tell you which is happening.

## Step 5: Rebalance without drama

If your check turned up more tech than you want, you have low-drama options:

- **Redirect new contributions** to other funds instead of selling anything. This avoids tax bills in a taxable account.
- **Rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts** like a 401(k) or IRA, where selling and buying typically doesn't trigger a tax bill.
- **Check fees** before switching. A fund with a higher expense ratio costs more every year. On $10,000, a 0.75% fee is $75 a year, compared with $5 at 0.05%.
- **Set a rule** such as reviewing once or twice a year, so you aren't reacting to headlines.

If you're unsure, a fee-only financial planner can review your mix for a flat fee, which is worth considering for larger balances.

## What not to do

Don't sell everything because an expert drew a historical comparison. Don't pile in because the market is hot, either. Both moves are bets on timing, and the people making the comparisons, Belski included, are offering a view rather than a certainty.

## The action to take this week

Open your retirement account, find the top 10 holdings of each fund, and fill in the overlap table above. Once you know your real tech percentage, you can decide whether it matches your timeline, and that's a decision you control no matter what the market does next.
