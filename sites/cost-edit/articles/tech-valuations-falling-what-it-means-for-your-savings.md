The Nasdaq fell this week as technology stock valuations dropped to their lowest levels since the pandemic era, per Fox Business. If you own a 401(k), an IRA, or an index fund, you probably own some of those stocks. Here is what "sinking valuations" means in plain English, and a short checklist for seeing how much of your own money is exposed.

## What the news actually says

Fox Business reports that the Nasdaq tumbled as tech valuations sank to their lowest point since the pandemic. The outlet says investors are reassessing the premium they have been willing to pay for high-growth tech names amid broader market uncertainty. Citi's Tyler Radke, co-head of U.S. software equity research, joined Fox Business's "Making Money" to discuss falling tech valuations and potential software bargains.

That is the extent of what we can say with confidence. The coverage we're drawing on does not include specific index levels, percentage drops, or price targets, so this article doesn't offer any. Check your own account for exact figures.

## "Valuation" in plain English

A valuation is how much investors pay for a company relative to what it earns or is expected to earn. Think of it like the price per ounce at the grocery store. If the same cereal costs more per ounce than it did last year, you are paying a premium. A falling valuation means the price you pay for each dollar of earnings has dropped.

That can happen in two ways:

- **Prices fall** while earnings stay the same.
- **Earnings grow** while prices stay flat.

The Fox Business headline describes a drop, and the Nasdaq's tumble suggests prices are doing at least part of the work. Either way, the tech premium is shrinking.

## Why this matters even if you never pick stocks

Tech has been the market's dominant growth engine, so a reset there can ripple across portfolios. You may not own a single tech stock by name and still be exposed through:

- **Target-date funds** in your 401(k), which hold broad stock indexes
- **S&P 500 index funds**, where large tech companies make up a big share
- **Nasdaq-100 funds**, which are concentrated in tech by design
- **Sector or "AI" funds** you may have added after the recent boom

The more of your balance sits in the last two categories, the more a tech selloff shows up in your statement.

## A five-minute exposure check

You can do this today without making a single trade.

1. **Log in to your retirement account** and list every fund you hold.
2. **Look up each fund's top 10 holdings** on its fact sheet. Your plan provider or the fund company's website usually has it.
3. **Note any overlap.** If your S&P 500 fund, your Nasdaq fund, and your growth fund all list the same large tech names, you own more of them than it looks.
4. **Write down your tech-heavy share** as a rough percentage of your total balance.
5. **Compare it to your comfort level.** If a 20% drop in that slice would keep you up at night, that is useful information.

Show your math. If you hold $50,000 total and $15,000 is in tech-heavy funds, that slice is 30% of your balance. A hypothetical 20% drop in that slice would be a $3,000 hit, or 6% of the total. These numbers are an example, not a forecast.

## Warning sign or bargain?

That is the question Fox Business's segment with Radke centers on: whether falling valuations point to potential software bargains. The segment description frames it as an open question, and so should you. Nobody reliably knows whether a drop is the start of something deeper or a discount.

What you can say with certainty is that a lower valuation makes a stock cheaper relative to its earnings than it was before. It does not make it safe. Cheap can get cheaper.

## Why interest rates and earnings both matter

The coverage points to interest rates, earnings expectations, and whether the recent tech boom has gone too far as the forces at play. Here is how they connect, as our own plain-language explanation:

- **Rates:** Growth companies are valued largely on profits expected far in the future. When rates are higher, those future profits are worth less today, which tends to pressure growth stocks.
- **Earnings:** If companies report results that fall short of expectations, investors may decide the premium isn't justified.
- **Risk appetite:** When people feel uncertain, they pay less for anything that depends on a bright future.

## What to do with your money this week

Most everyday investors do best by changing very little in a volatile week. A few moves are sensible:

- **Keep contributing on schedule.** Regular 401(k) deposits buy more shares when prices dip.
- **Don't chase the bargain headline.** A single analyst appearance is not a reason to shift thousands of dollars.
- **Rebalance only if your plan calls for it.** If tech has grown to a bigger share of your portfolio than you intended, trimming back to your target is a rules-based move, not a market call.
- **Check fees while you're in there.** Overlapping funds often mean paying two expense ratios for the same exposure.

If you will need the money within a few years, this is also a good moment to confirm that your cash and bond holdings match your timeline.

## The takeaway

Open your retirement account today, list your top holdings, and find out what percentage of your balance rides on tech. You can't control the Nasdaq, but you can know exactly how much of it you own.
