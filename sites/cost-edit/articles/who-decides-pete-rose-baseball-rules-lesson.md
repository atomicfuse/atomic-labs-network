President Donald Trump has pardoned the late Pete Rose. According to Fox Business, David Samson, a former Miami Marlins president, told the network's 'Varney & Co.' that the pardon will not get Rose into the Baseball Hall of Fame. Samson's argument, as reported, is that MLB eligibility and Hall of Fame rules are separate from presidential clemency. The segment also touched on the looming MLB lockout.

This is a baseball story, but it carries a lesson that saves real money: **a yes from one authority doesn't change the rules of another.** Once you spot that pattern, you can stop wasting time on the wrong phone call.

## The Rose case in plain terms

A pardon comes from the government. Rose's lifetime ban and Hall of Fame eligibility are tied to baseball's own institutions. Per Samson, as reported by Fox Business, those are different rulebooks with different keepers, so a pardon from the first doesn't rewrite the second.

Whether that's fair is a separate debate, and the story has plenty of people on both sides. What matters here is the structure: two bodies, two sets of rules, and no automatic hand-off between them.

## Where this shows up in your own spending

The same structure appears all the time in household bills and purchases. What follows is my analysis, not part of the Rose reporting.

| Situation | The tempting assumption | Who actually decides |
|---|---|---|
| A store employee promises a price match | "It's approved." | The store's written policy, and sometimes the corporate office |
| A sales rep says your promo rate will "carry over" | "My rate is safe." | The terms in your contract or billing agreement |
| A retailer says a warranty claim is "covered" | "I'll get a replacement." | The manufacturer or warranty provider, which may differ from the seller |
| A friend says a subscription is "easy to cancel" | "One click and done." | The company's actual cancellation terms |

In each case, the person talking to you may be sincere. They just may not be the one holding the pen.

## Three steps before you rely on a promise

### 1. Ask who owns the decision

A direct question works: "Is that your policy, or is that something the manufacturer/provider/billing department controls?" If the answer is vague, assume the promise is soft.

### 2. Get it in writing

A chat transcript, email, or a note on your account beats a verbal assurance. If a rep agrees to a price or credit, ask them to confirm it in the account notes and send you a confirmation. Save it with the date.

### 3. Check the document that governs

The contract, terms of service, or billing statement usually outranks what anyone says out loud. Look for the lines on renewal rates, fees, and cancellation. These are the lines that tend to decide your total cost over time.

## Do the math on what a promise is worth

When someone offers you a deal, put it on one line so you can compare it with the written terms. Use the same units for every option, such as dollars per month and total over 12 months.

A simple template:

- Promised monthly price × number of months = what you were told you'd pay
- Written monthly price (including fees and any renewal rate) × number of months = what the document says you'll pay
- The difference is the cost of trusting the wrong authority

If the two numbers don't match, the written terms are the ones to plan around until someone with real authority changes them.

## When a symbolic win isn't a practical one

Some gestures feel like progress but don't change the outcome. An apology, a goodwill statement, or a sympathetic rep can all be real and kind, and still leave your bill exactly where it was.

The pardon story shows the gap clearly. A high-profile action happened, and per Samson, the practical result Rose's supporters want still sits with a different body. Before you celebrate a promise, ask whether it actually changes the thing you care about.

## Go to the right desk

Next time you're told something "has been taken care of," ask one question: who has the power to make this stick? Then take your request to that person, in writing, before the next bill arrives.
