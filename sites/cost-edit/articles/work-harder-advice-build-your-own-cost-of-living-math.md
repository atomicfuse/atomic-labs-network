When a wealthy investor tells you to work harder and earn more, it sounds like advice. It rarely comes with a number attached. This article shows you how to build the number yourself: a one-page cost-of-living check that tells you what your income actually has to cover and where you have room to move.

## What happened, and why it's useful

Per HuffPost, "Shark Tank" investor Kevin O'Leary took part in YouTube's "Surrounded," where he debated 20 Gen Z workers about making a living today. HuffPost's recap argues he came across as dismissive and out of touch, with vague advice on wages, rent, wealth inequality, and job prospects. It contrasts his pro-capitalist talking points with statistics on homeownership, unemployment, and the growing wealth gap.

We're not here to referee the debate. The takeaway is practical: general advice like "earn more" or "get a better job" can't tell you whether your own budget works. Your own math can.

I'm not citing specific rent or wage figures here, because the recap doesn't give me any I can verify. Use your own numbers, from your lease, your pay stubs, and your statements.

## Step 1: Start with take-home pay, not salary

Pull your last three pay stubs and average the net amount deposited. Convert it to a monthly figure:

- Weekly pay x 52 / 12
- Biweekly pay x 26 / 12
- Semi-monthly pay x 2

If your hours vary, use the lowest of the three months, not the average. Budgets built on your best month fail in your worst one.

## Step 2: List fixed costs with their real totals

Write down every bill that arrives on a schedule. Check each one for fees and renewal rates, because the advertised price is often not what you pay.

| Bill | What to check | Your monthly total |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | Parking, pet, and amenity fees; renewal increase | |
| Utilities | Average of the last 12 bills, not one month | |
| Internet | Price after the promo period ends, plus equipment rental | |
| Mobile | Taxes and fees on top of the plan price | |
| Insurance | Any monthly installment fee | |
| Debt payments | Minimums actually due | |

Add the column. Divide it by your monthly take-home pay. That percentage is your fixed-cost load. If it's above half, you have less flexibility than most budgeting advice assumes, and the fix may be on the cost side as much as the income side.

## Step 3: Audit the flexible spending

Open your last 60 days of card and bank statements. Sort spending into groceries, takeout, subscriptions, and everything else. Show the math, same units across the board: monthly dollars.

- **Groceries:** total spend divided by the number of weeks, then multiplied by 4.33 for a monthly figure.
- **Takeout:** count orders, then multiply by your average order including delivery fees and tip. Delivery fees can turn a modest meal into a much bigger charge.
- **Subscriptions:** list each one with its monthly price. For annual plans, divide by 12.

Then mark each subscription as used weekly, used monthly, or forgotten. Cancel the forgotten ones today. You can resubscribe later if you miss them.

## Step 4: Compare options on total cost

When you're deciding between plans, buying in bulk, or buying a gadget, compare the full cost over a set period, such as 12 months.

Example format (use your own local prices and note the date and store):

| Option | Upfront | Monthly | 12-month total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plan A (promo) | $0 | promo price x months + full price x remaining months | add up |
| Plan B (no promo) | $0 | flat price x 12 | add up |

For bulk packs, divide price by quantity to get a unit price, then compare it to the regular size. Bulk only wins if you'll use it before it spoils or you tire of it.

## Step 5: Find your gap, then pick one lever

Subtract fixed costs and flexible spending from take-home pay. A positive number is your room for savings or debt payoff. A negative one is the real problem to solve.

Then pick one lever, not five:

1. **Cut a recurring bill.** Call your internet or mobile provider and ask what the current lowest-price plan is for your usage. Compare it with what you pay.
2. **Trim a flexible category.** Reduce takeout orders by a set number per week.
3. **Raise income.** Now "earn more" has a target: the exact monthly figure that closes your gap.

That last point is where the generic advice becomes useful. "Earn more" is vague. "Earn $X more per month after taxes" is a plan you can pursue through a raise request, extra hours, or a job search.

## Why this beats a debate

The HuffPost recap frames the exchange as a clash over whether "work harder" advice still fits today's economy. People will keep arguing that, and the big-picture questions about wages, housing, and wealth are real. But your budget is a smaller, solvable problem. Your own figures show whether the issue is rent, a bloated bill stack, or income, and each has a different fix.

Spend 30 minutes this week filling in the tables above. Once you can name your gap in dollars, no one's vague advice can talk you out of what your numbers say.
