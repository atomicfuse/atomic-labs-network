Halloween night at home doesn't need a haunted house. It needs a warm kitchen, something simmering or baking, and a good story to tell over a drink. Three cocktails with unsettling histories give you exactly that: the Monkey Gland, Arsenic and Old Lace, and the Broken Shoeshiner. Below you'll find each one's backstory, as covered in a video from WineOceans, and a plan for building a cozy evening around them.

A quick note on tone: two of these stories involve real deaths. Tell them with a light touch for the cocktail names, but with respect for the people behind them.

## The Monkey Gland: A Drink Named for a Strange Experiment

This one is the easiest to tell at a dinner table because it's more odd than grim. Per WineOceans, the Monkey Gland was inspired by Dr. Voronoff, a 1920s surgeon whose controversial gland transplant experiments aimed at rejuvenation. The video describes those experiments as failed.

The takeaway for your guests: cocktail names often preserve pieces of history that people have otherwise forgotten. A drink with a bizarre name usually has a story behind it.

## Arsenic and Old Lace: From a Real Case to a Play to a Glass

The name will ring a bell if you know the play. According to the video, the play was based on a real serial killer, Amy Archer Gilligan, who poisoned approximately 60 elderly residents. The cocktail borrows the play's title.

If you share this one, keep it brief and factual. It's a good example of how a true crime becomes a piece of pop culture, and then a menu item, several steps removed from the people it affected.

## The Broken Shoeshiner: How Absinthe Took the Blame

This is the most thought-provoking of the three. Per WineOceans, the Broken Shoeshiner was created in response to the 1905 Swiss murders, in which Jean Lanfrey killed his family. The event sparked an anti-absinthe movement.

The video's point is that absinthe was scapegoated in early 20th-century Europe, even though it was only one of many alcoholic factors in the crime. It's a handy reminder that panic over a single substance can distort the real story of what happened. That makes a good conversation starter, and a gentler one than the crime itself.

## Build a Cozy Night Around the Stories

You don't need a complicated menu. The drinks are the entertainment, so let the food be easy and filling. Here is a simple plan for four people.

### Start something before guests arrive

Pick a one-pot dish that holds well, such as a big pot of chili or a baked pasta casserole. Plan on about 20 minutes of prep and 30 to 45 minutes of cooking for either. Both keep warm without fuss, which frees you to tell the stories and pour drinks.

### Serve in "chapters"

The video pairs story, recipe, and tasting in sequence, and that rhythm works at a table too. Pour one drink, tell one story, then let people eat and talk before the next round. Three drinks over a long evening keeps things relaxed.

### Keep a non-alcoholic option

Set out hot cider or sparkling water with a slice of orange so every guest has something festive to hold. Apple cider is inexpensive and can be warmed in a pot or slow cooker on low.

### Finish with a nostalgic dessert

A pan of warm baked apples or a simple gingerbread cake rounds things out. Both use pantry basics. If you don't have fresh apples, canned pie filling spooned over a crumble topping is a fine swap.

## Before You Pour

The video's recipes aren't reproduced here, so look up the full specs for each drink before guest night and test them once. Cocktails are easier to hand out when you've already tasted them. Also confirm you have the ingredients, since absinthe in particular is a specialty bottle you may need to plan ahead for.

Pick one story, one pot of something warm, and one drink to practice this week. That's enough to make Halloween night feel like an occasion.
