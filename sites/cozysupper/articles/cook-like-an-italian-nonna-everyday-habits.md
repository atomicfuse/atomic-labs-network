Vogue recently profiled Vicky Bennison, the author and creator of Pasta Grannies, and the older Italian women her project documents: nonne who make pasta by hand and keep regional traditions alive. Bennison also has a new cookbook, *Eat Everything*, and Vogue's piece includes a recipe from a grandmother named Rosa for cicatelli con ciambutella.

I don't have the full recipe or Bennison's exact words in front of me, so I won't pretend to reprint them. What I can do is take the ideas behind the project and turn them into habits you can use at your own table this week. Think of it as la dolce vita for a regular Tuesday night.

## What Pasta Grannies Is Really About

According to Vogue, Bennison's project documents Italian grandmothers preserving handmade pasta traditions while sharing life wisdom along the way. The pasta is the entry point. The bigger story is how these women cook, how they eat, and how they pass knowledge down.

Vogue frames the project as a counterweight to hyper-optimized wellness trends. Rather than tracking and tweaking, the nonne simply cook, share, and repeat. That's a comforting idea if your dinner routine feels like one more thing to get right.

## Habit 1: Cook With What You Have

The title of Bennison's new book, *Eat Everything*, points to a low-waste approach to food. You can borrow that mindset without owning a single Italian cookbook.

Here are some practical ways to try it:

- **Check the fridge before you shop.** Pick one dinner this week built entirely from what's already there.
- **Save the odds and ends.** Vegetable trimmings, a Parmesan rind, or a heel of bread can all go into a pot of soup instead of the trash.
- **Stretch a small amount of meat.** Chop it fine and stir it through beans, pasta, or rice so a little flavors the whole pot.
- **Turn leftovers into a second meal.** Last night's roasted vegetables can become today's soup or pasta sauce.

These are my suggestions in the spirit of the book's idea, not instructions from Bennison. They're also easy on the grocery budget, which is a nice side effect.

## Habit 2: Make One Thing by Hand

Handmade pasta is the heart of the Pasta Grannies project, and Vogue notes that Bennison's interest is in keeping these traditions alive. You don't need to commit to a full pasta-making day. Pick one small thing to make from scratch and see how it feels.

A few low-pressure ideas:

1. Make a simple flour-and-water pasta on a slow Sunday, when there's no clock to beat.
2. Bake a dessert your own grandparent made, even if the first try is wobbly.
3. Put on a pot of soup and let it simmer while you do something else.

Handmade food takes longer, but it also gives the evening a shape. For a chilly night, that's half the appeal.

## Habit 3: Cook for Other People

Vogue describes the piece as tapping into a broader hunger for intergenerational knowledge. The nonne aren't just cooking; they're teaching and sharing. That suggests a simple practice: cook with someone.

Try one of these:

- Invite a kid, grandchild, or neighbor into the kitchen and give them a real job, like stirring or shaping.
- Ask an older relative how they make a dish you loved growing up, and write down what they say.
- Make a double batch of something and take half to a friend.

If you cook for two, a small-batch dinner shared across the table counts too. The point is company, not crowd size.

## Habit 4: Write Down the Family Recipes

Part of what makes Pasta Grannies resonate is that these traditions are held in people's hands and memories. Recipes that never get written down can fade quietly.

You can do something about that in an afternoon. Choose one dish from your family, then record the ingredients, rough amounts, and the small habits that make it taste right. Note things like how thick the dough should feel or how long it simmers. A photo of the finished dish and the person who made it makes the page even better.

## Rosa's Cicatelli con Ciambutella

Vogue includes Rosa's recipe for cicatelli con ciambutella, a handmade pasta dish. Since the full ingredient list and method aren't something I can reliably reproduce here, I'd point you to the Vogue piece and to Bennison's *Eat Everything* for the exact instructions.

What you can take from it before you cook is the spirit: a grandmother's dish, made by hand, from a tradition worth keeping. When you do try it, give yourself a relaxed afternoon and expect the first batch to be imperfect. That's part of learning any handmade food.

## La Dolce Vita Is Smaller Than You Think

Vogue's framing is that la dolce vita can be a practical lifestyle rather than just a travel aesthetic. That fits what the nonne seem to model: cook what's in front of you, stay connected to people, and treat a shared meal as the main event.

None of this requires a trip to Italy or a pasta machine. It requires a pot, a few ingredients, and someone to feed.

This week, pick one night, make one dish from what you already have, and set a place for someone else.
