Country singer and author Jessie James Decker has a new cookbook, "The Secret Sauce," and the idea behind it is one anyone with a stove can use tonight: the feelings you bring into the kitchen shape the meal. Per Fox News, she talks about food as a reflection of care, mood, and connection, and about a cooking lesson she learned the hard way.

We only have the broad outline of her message, so we won't put words in her mouth. What we can do is take that idea and make it practical. Below is a simple way to cook with intention on a chilly evening, plus a forgiving one-pot recipe to practice on.

## The Idea Worth Borrowing

According to Fox News, Decker's philosophy is that cooking is shaped by the emotions people bring into the kitchen. Most home cooks have seen this for themselves. A rushed, frazzled dinner tends to involve skipped steps, scorched onions, and a cook who is eager to be done. A calm one tends to taste better, and it's more fun to eat with the people around the table.

That's our read, not a claim from the cookbook. But it's easy to test, and the test costs you nothing.

## Three Small Rituals That Change the Mood

You don't need candles or a playlist (though music is nice). Try these.

### 1. Do a five-minute reset first
Before you chop anything, clear the counter, wash your hands, and put everything you need within reach. Reading the whole recipe once takes about two minutes and prevents most mid-cooking panic.

### 2. Pick one task to enjoy
Maybe it's the smell of onions softening or the sound of a wooden spoon scraping the pot. Pay attention to just that. It pulls you out of the day's noise.

### 3. Invite one helper
A kid can stir, tear herbs, or grate cheese. Family dinners often feel warmer when the cooking is shared, even if it's messier.

## A Practice Recipe: Cozy One-Pot Chicken and Rice Soup

This is a forgiving recipe for testing the approach. It serves 4 with leftovers for lunch, takes about 45 minutes, and uses one pot.

### What you need
- 1 tablespoon olive oil or butter
- 1 medium onion, diced
- 2 carrots, sliced
- 2 celery stalks, sliced (or an extra carrot if you don't have celery)
- 3 garlic cloves, minced
- 6 cups chicken broth (water plus 2 bouillon cubes works fine)
- 1 pound boneless chicken thighs or breasts
- 3/4 cup long-grain white rice
- 1 teaspoon dried thyme (or Italian seasoning)
- Salt and pepper
- Lemon juice or a splash of vinegar, to finish

### Steps
1. **Soften the vegetables.** Heat the oil over medium heat. Add onion, carrots, and celery with a pinch of salt. Cook 6 to 8 minutes, stirring now and then, until the onion turns translucent.
2. **Add the garlic.** Stir for 30 seconds, until fragrant.
3. **Add broth and chicken.** Pour in the broth, add the chicken and thyme, and bring to a boil. Reduce to a gentle simmer, cover, and cook 15 minutes for thighs or 12 to 15 for breasts, until the chicken reaches 165°F.
4. **Shred the chicken.** Lift it onto a plate and pull it apart with two forks.
5. **Cook the rice.** Stir the rice into the simmering broth and cook uncovered 12 to 15 minutes, until tender.
6. **Finish.** Return the chicken to the pot, add a squeeze of lemon, and taste. Adjust salt and pepper.

The soup thickens as it sits, so add a splash of broth or water when reheating leftovers.

## Affordable Swaps

- **No fresh vegetables?** A bag of frozen mixed vegetables stirred in during the last 5 minutes works well.
- **Rice alternatives:** Small pasta or egg noodles cook in about 8 to 10 minutes. Add them later than you would rice.
- **Cutting cost:** Bone-in chicken thighs are often cheaper. Simmer them a little longer, then discard the skin and bones when shredding.
- **Cooking for two:** Halve everything and use a smaller pot. Cooking times stay about the same.

## When Dinner Goes Sideways

The headline lesson here is that mood matters, but nobody stays calm every night. If the rice overcooks or the soup tastes flat, fix what you can. Acid (lemon or vinegar) and salt rescue a surprising number of bland pots. If you're tired, make the soup simpler: skip the celery, use frozen vegetables, and call it done.

Cooking mistakes are also how habits improve. Burn the garlic once and you'll remember to add it late.

## Turn Dinner Into a Family Story

Decker's cookbook, per Fox News, centers on food as connection, and family recipes are a natural place for that. Next time you make a dish, write down who taught you, what you changed, and who asked for seconds. Tape the card inside a cabinet door. Over time, a simple soup becomes something your family can point to.

## Try This Tonight

Before you turn on the burner, take one slow breath, clear your counter, and hand someone a spoon. Then make the soup.
