Western Australian researchers have launched Food Waste PRO, an online toolkit that helps small and medium food businesses measure their waste and judge whether reduction or upcycling ideas are technically, legally and financially viable, according to Phys. The idea is simple: before you fix waste, measure it, then ask whether the fix is worth the effort.

That logic works in a home kitchen too. Here is how to run a quick audit of what you throw away, then turn the findings into a hearty pot of soup that costs almost nothing.

## Step 1: Measure Before You Fix

The toolkit starts with measurement, and you can do the same without any software. For one week, keep a bowl or bag on the counter and put in everything you would otherwise toss: wilted greens, carrot tops, onion ends, stale bread, the last scoop of rice.

At the end of the week, look at what piled up. Most households notice a pattern, such as the same bag of celery going limp or half a loaf going stale. That pattern is your starting point.

## Step 2: Ask Whether Each Fix Is Worth It

Food Waste PRO asks businesses whether an idea is viable. Borrow that question in a home version:

- **Is it safe?** Use only food that is still good. When in doubt, throw it out.
- **Is it easy?** If a fix takes an hour of fiddly work to save 50 cents, skip it.
- **Does it save money?** The best fixes replace something you would have bought.

Soup passes all three tests, which is why it is the classic home answer to leftovers.

## Step 3: Make a Cozy Scrap Soup

This is a flexible, forgiving recipe. It serves 4 and takes about 10 minutes of prep and 35 minutes of simmering.

### What You Need

- 2 tablespoons oil or butter
- 2 cups chopped vegetable odds and ends (onion, carrot, celery, potato, cabbage, mushroom stems)
- 2 cloves garlic, chopped, or 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
- 6 cups broth or water plus 2 bouillon cubes
- 1 can (15 oz) beans, drained, or 1 cup leftover cooked rice or pasta
- 2 cups tender greens, wilted ones are fine
- Salt, pepper, and 1 teaspoon dried herbs
- A splash of vinegar or squeeze of lemon

### How to Make It

1. Heat the oil in a large pot over medium heat. Add the harder vegetables (onion, carrot, celery, potato) and cook 6 to 8 minutes until softened.
2. Stir in the garlic and herbs for 30 seconds.
3. Add the broth and beans. Bring to a boil, then lower to a simmer and cook 20 minutes.
4. Add the greens and any cooked rice or pasta. Simmer 5 more minutes.
5. Season with salt, pepper, and the vinegar or lemon. Taste and adjust.

For a creamier bowl, blend about a third of the soup and stir it back in. It keeps in the fridge for up to 4 days and freezes well for about 3 months.

## Affordable Swaps

- **No fresh garlic?** Garlic powder works fine.
- **No broth?** Water plus bouillon, or a spoonful of tomato paste for depth.
- **Stale bread?** Toast cubes in a 400°F oven for about 8 minutes and float them on top as croutons.
- **Leftover roast chicken or sausage?** Chop it and add it with the beans for a heartier dinner.
- **Parmesan rind?** Drop it into the simmering pot for savory flavor, then remove before serving.

## Keep the Habit Going

A few small routines help the audit pay off:

- Keep a freezer bag labeled "soup" and add clean vegetable trimmings as you cook. When it is full, make stock or soup.
- Move the oldest food to the front of the fridge each time you shop.
- Plan one "use it up" dinner at the end of each week.

The same thinking appears in the business world. Food Waste PRO is aimed at companies facing tighter margins and growing pressure to operate efficiently, per Phys, and the framing there is that waste is both a sustainability problem and a missed opportunity. At home, that opportunity is dinner.

## Your Next Move

This week, set a bowl on the counter, collect your scraps, and make the soup on the night your fridge looks most hopeless. You will know what you waste, and you will have a warm supper to show for it.
