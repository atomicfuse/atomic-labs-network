A new national study from Australia, reported by Medical Xpress, finds that meal delivery apps like Uber Eats and DoorDash are seen as "treat food" platforms, and that deals and app design nudge users toward less healthy choices. The researchers argue governments should update nutrition policy to cover digital food environments. Australia is already considering stronger rules on promoting unhealthy food, especially to children.

You don't need to wait for policy. Below: how to spot the nudges on your own phone, and a one-pot dinner that is ready in about 35 minutes, so a chilly evening doesn't default to a delivery order.

## Why the app makes treat food feel like the obvious choice

The study, per the report, found people think of these apps as places for treats. Add discounts and convenience, and the logic writes itself: it's cheap, it's easy, I've earned it.

That's the pattern to watch. The report doesn't claim anyone is being tricked, only that deals and design steer choices. The report's details are limited, so the specifics below are my own practical read, not the study's findings:

- **Notice what the deal is on.** If the discounted items are the fried or sugary ones, the promo is choosing for you.
- **Notice the "treat" feeling.** If you opened the app tired and hungry, the decision was mostly made before you scrolled.
- **Compare to home.** A discount on a $28 order isn't much of a bargain next to a $10 pot of soup that feeds four.

## Three simple rules for ordering without falling for promos

1. **Decide before you open the app.** Pick what you want, then search for it. Browsing the home screen is browsing the ads.
2. **Set a delivery night.** One planned treat night a week feels like a choice, not a slip.
3. **Keep a backup dinner ready.** A freezer portion or pantry meal removes the "nothing to eat" pressure that sends you to the app.

## The backup dinner: One-Pot Chicken and Rice Soup

This is the kind of meal that makes a delivery order unnecessary on a cold night. It's my own simple recipe, not from the study.

**Serves:** 4 generous bowls
**Time:** 10 minutes prep, 25 minutes simmer
**Rough cost:** a few dollars per serving, depending on where you shop

### Ingredients

- 1 tablespoon oil or butter
- 1 onion, diced
- 2 carrots, sliced
- 2 celery stalks, sliced (optional, see substitutions)
- 3 garlic cloves, minced
- 6 cups chicken broth (or water plus 2 bouillon cubes)
- 3/4 cup long-grain white rice
- 2 boneless chicken breasts or 4 thighs (about 1 pound)
- 1 teaspoon dried thyme
- Salt and pepper
- Squeeze of lemon, to finish

### Steps

1. **Soften the vegetables.** Heat the oil in a large pot over medium heat. Cook the onion, carrots, and celery for 5 minutes, until the onion turns translucent. Add garlic and cook 30 seconds.
2. **Add liquid and chicken.** Pour in the broth, add the chicken and thyme, and bring to a gentle boil.
3. **Simmer.** Reduce to a low simmer, cover, and cook 12 minutes.
4. **Add the rice.** Stir in the rice and cook, partly covered, another 12 to 15 minutes, until the rice is tender and the chicken reaches 165°F (74°C) inside.
5. **Shred and finish.** Lift the chicken out, shred it with two forks, and return it to the pot. Season with salt and pepper and add a squeeze of lemon. If the soup is thicker than you like, add a splash of broth or water.

### Affordable substitutions

- **No celery?** Use extra carrot or a handful of chopped cabbage.
- **Chicken thighs** cost less than breasts and stay juicier.
- **Leftover roast chicken or rotisserie meat:** skip step 2's chicken, add shredded meat with the rice's last 5 minutes.
- **Frozen vegetables:** stir in a cup of peas or mixed vegetables at the end.
- **No rice?** Small pasta works; cook about 8 minutes.

## Make it a delivery-proof habit

The real trick is having something ready when the craving hits.

### Freeze in single portions

Rice soaks up broth as it sits, so for freezing, cook the soup with a little less rice or add extra broth when reheating. Cool completely, then freeze in 2-cup containers. Reheat from frozen over low heat for 10 to 15 minutes.

### Plan one "treat" on purpose

There's nothing wrong with takeout. The study's concern is about environments that nudge, not about enjoying a meal out. Pair the soup with something you love: buttered toast, a grilled cheese, or a warm baked apple for dessert. It scratches the treat itch at home.

### Small-batch version for two

Halve everything: 3 cups broth, 1/3 cup rice, 1 large chicken thigh or breast, one small onion, one carrot. Use a medium saucepan and keep the same timing.

## The takeaway

Next time you feel the pull of a delivery promo, pause for 60 seconds and ask whether you actually want that meal or just the discount. Then check whether a pot of soup is already in your freezer.
