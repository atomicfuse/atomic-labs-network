Vogue recently profiled Vicky Bennison, the author and creator of Pasta Grannies, and her work documenting the older Italian women who keep handmade pasta traditions alive. Her new cookbook is called *Eat Everything*. Here is the practical payoff: you can borrow the grannies' habits for your own weeknight table, starting this week, without a pasta machine or a trip to Italy.

## What Pasta Grannies Actually Is

Per Vogue, Pasta Grannies documents Italian *nonne* who make pasta by hand, preserving regional traditions while also sharing life wisdom. It is less a glossy travel fantasy than a record of what these women cook and how they live.

That is the useful reframe. According to Vogue, the project offers an antidote to hyper-optimized wellness trends, and it treats *la dolce vita* as a practical way of living rather than an aesthetic. For a home cook, that means the sweet life starts in the kitchen on an ordinary Tuesday.

## Lesson One: Cook With What You Have

The title of Bennison's new book, *Eat Everything*, points to a no-waste mindset, and it is the easiest habit to adopt. Here is how to apply it on a chilly evening:

- **Start with the fridge, not the recipe.** Check what needs using first: the half onion, the celery heel, the rind of Parmesan.
- **Make soup the default landing place.** Odds and ends of vegetables, a handful of beans, and any small pasta shape make a pot that feeds four for about 30 to 40 minutes of simmering.
- **Substitute without guilt.** No pancetta? Use bacon or leave it out. No fresh herbs? A pinch of dried works. Stale bread can be toasted into croutons or stirred into soup.

This is my own translation of the idea, not a quote from Bennison, but it fits the spirit Vogue describes.

## Lesson Two: Keep Recipes Alive by Cooking Them

The grannies' work is a reminder that handmade food traditions depend on people actually making them. A recipe in a drawer isn't preserved; a recipe cooked every few months is.

Try this small habit: pick one dish a relative makes and cook it alongside them, writing down what they actually do. Note quantities in handfuls and mugs if that's how they measure, then weigh it once so you can repeat it. Even one recipe a season adds up.

## Lesson Three: Make the Table Social

Vogue notes the project's focus on intergenerational knowledge and community. You don't need a big gathering to borrow that. A pot of something hearty invites a second bowl and a longer conversation. Cook with a kid, a neighbor, or a parent, and let one person knead while the other talks.

Vogue's piece includes Rosa's recipe for *cicatelli con ciambutella*, a handmade pasta dish. I can't reproduce the recipe here, because I only have the dish's name and not its ingredients or method. Look for the full recipe in the Vogue article or in Bennison's cookbook, and read it all the way through before you start.

In the meantime, here are general tips for any first attempt at handmade pasta:

1. **Set aside an afternoon.** Shaping pasta by hand is slow the first time. Give yourself a couple of hours and treat it as the activity, not a chore.
2. **Work in small batches.** A small amount of dough for two is far less daunting than enough for a crowd.
3. **Use a clean, dry surface** and keep unshaped dough covered so it doesn't dry out.
4. **Expect uneven shapes.** Irregular pieces are part of handmade pasta, and they cook up just fine.

## Making Dolce Vita Weeknight-Sized

You can't live in an Italian village, but you can adopt a version of the grannies' routine:

- Cook one pot of something simple on a Sunday and plan to eat from it twice.
- Use up produce before buying more.
- Share a meal with someone, even once a week.
- Cook one family recipe each season and write it down.

None of this costs extra. It mostly asks you to slow down a little and use what's already on your shelves.
