A pot of rice sits under so many cozy dinners: the curry, the stew, the saucy chicken you ladle over the top. When the rice turns out gummy or clumped, the whole bowl suffers. According to Fox News, chefs say the fix is usually small: a few habits that most home cooks skip or overdo. Here's how to use them tonight.

One caveat up front. The chef guidance Fox News reported doesn't include exact water ratios or timings, so I won't invent them. For those, follow the package directions for your rice, and use the habits below to improve the result.

## Rinse your rice (especially basmati and jasmine)

This is the step most home cooks skip. Per Fox News, chefs say rinsing basmati and jasmine rice before cooking is the key to fluffy, distinct grains that don't clump in the pot.

A simple way to do it:

1. Put the rice in a bowl or fine-mesh strainer.
2. Run cold water over it, swishing gently with your fingers.
3. Drain and repeat until the water looks noticeably clearer.
4. Shake off the excess water before it goes in the pot.

It takes a minute or two and costs nothing.

## Don't use one method for every rice

Another mistake chefs flag is treating all rice the same. Rinsing, soaking, water ratios, resting and fluffing all depend on the variety you're cooking and the texture you want.

That's good news for your pantry. Instead of memorizing one universal rule, match the method to the bag:

- **Basmati and jasmine:** rinse first for separate, fluffy grains.
- **Any rice:** check the package for the water ratio, since the right amount varies by variety.
- **Texture goals matter:** if you want something softer or stickier for a particular dish, that changes how you handle the rice, so decide before you start.

Budget tip: store-brand long-grain rice is usually much cheaper than specialty bags. The same habits apply, so you can practice with the inexpensive option.

## Keep the spoon out while it cooks

Stirring rice during cooking is one of the habits chefs say can ruin the texture. The likely reason is that stirring works against the separate, tender grains you're after. So once the rice is in the pot with its water and the lid is on, resist the urge to poke at it. Set a timer, keep the lid closed, and walk away.

This suits one-pot cooking well. If your dinner is a pot of something simmering, your hands are free to set the table.

## Let it rest before you serve

Resting is on the chefs' list of steps that matter. When the rice finishes cooking, don't dive in right away. Take the pot off the heat, keep the lid on, and give it a few minutes to settle. The exact time can vary with the rice, so follow your package guidance. Rice that's been left alone for a bit tends to be easier to fluff and less wet on top.

## Fluff gently, not aggressively

The last mistake is fluffing too hard. Chefs say gentle fluffing is part of the answer, and it makes sense: grains that have just cooked are delicate. Use a fork or a rice paddle and lift the rice from the bottom, turning it over rather than mashing or stirring. A few easy folds is enough.

## If your rice still goes wrong

Gummy, clumpy or undercooked rice happens to everyone. Rather than guess, work backward through the list:

- **Gummy or clumpy?** Did you rinse? Did you stir while it cooked? Did you fluff too roughly?
- **Undercooked?** Check the water ratio against the package for your specific variety, and make sure you let it rest with the lid on.
- **Not the texture you wanted?** You may have used a method meant for a different type of rice.

Change one thing at a time so you know what made the difference.

## A quick rice routine for chilly-evening dinners

Here's the whole approach in doing order:

1. Rinse basmati or jasmine rice in cold water until it runs clearer.
2. Cook using the ratio on your package.
3. Keep the lid on and don't stir.
4. Rest the pot off the heat before opening it.
5. Fluff gently with a fork.

Then ladle your soup, curry or braise right over the top.

Next time you make a big pot of something comforting, spend the extra minute rinsing the rice first. It's the easiest upgrade on the list, and the chefs Fox News spoke to say it matters most.
