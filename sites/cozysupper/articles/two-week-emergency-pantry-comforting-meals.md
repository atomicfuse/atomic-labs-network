A UK government watchdog has said homes in the UK aren't prepared for possible food supply disruptions caused by things like cyberattacks, power outages, disease outbreaks or extreme weather, according to reporting from Medicalxpress. The advice that follows is often vague: keep some "nonperishable food." This article makes that concrete. By the end, you'll have a balanced pantry plan and a way to turn it into warm, filling one-pot suppers, without spending a fortune or living on crackers.

The nutrition-focused guidance Medicalxpress highlights goes beyond shelf-stable snacks. It points to balanced protein, whole-grain carbohydrates, canned vegetables, water, supplements, and practical gear like a can opener. I've built the rest of this around those categories, and the cooking ideas and quantities below are my own suggestions, not figures from that report.

## Start With the Five Categories

Think of your pantry as a plate. If each category is covered, you can mix and match into almost any simple meal.

### Protein

Canned beans, lentils, chickpeas, canned fish and canned chicken all keep well. Beans and lentils are usually the cheapest. If you only buy one kind, choose beans: they work in soup, chili, and casserole.

### Whole-grain carbohydrates

Brown rice, rolled oats, whole-wheat pasta, barley and whole-grain crackers give you energy and fiber. Fiber matters because an emergency stash can quickly drift toward refined, low-fiber food. Barley is a good soup thickener and costs very little.

### Canned vegetables

Canned tomatoes, carrots, peas, corn, spinach and mixed vegetables bring vitamins and color to a pantry that can otherwise look beige. Look for lower-sodium versions when you can, and drain and rinse beans and vegetables to cut some of the salt.

### Water

Water is easy to forget because it's always been in the tap. Store some, and remember it also goes into your cooking: rice, oats, pasta and soup all need it.

### Supplements and the can opener

Medicalxpress's guidance includes supplements, such as a multivitamin, to help fill gaps if fresh produce is scarce. Also include a manual can opener. A cupboard full of cans and no way to open them is a real, and very fixable, problem.

## A Simple Way to Size Your Stockpile

A two-week supply is a sensible target for a household. Here is a rough starting point for two adults. These quantities are my own estimates, so adjust for appetites and dietary needs:

- 8 to 10 cans of beans or lentils
- 4 to 6 cans of fish or chicken
- 10 to 12 cans of tomatoes and vegetables
- 1 kg (about 2 lb) brown rice, plus 500 g (1 lb) whole-wheat pasta
- 500 g (1 lb) rolled oats
- Stock cubes or powder, dried herbs, garlic powder, and a jar of spices
- Water for drinking and cooking

Buy a few extra items each shop rather than all at once. It spreads the cost, and you can rotate older cans into your regular cooking so nothing expires forgotten.

## Pantry Meals That Feel Like Supper

The trick to making emergency food not feel depressing is to cook it the way you'd cook any cozy dinner: one pot, plenty of seasoning, and something warm in a bowl.

### One-pot tomato, bean and pasta soup (serves 2 to 3, about 25 minutes)

1. In a pot, warm a spoonful of oil if you have it, or a splash of water. Add 1 teaspoon garlic powder and 1 teaspoon dried Italian herbs.
2. Add one 400 g (14 oz) can of tomatoes, one can of rinsed white beans, and 3 cups of stock or water with a stock cube.
3. Bring to a simmer, then add 1 cup of whole-wheat pasta.
4. Cook 10 to 12 minutes, stirring now and then, until the pasta is tender.
5. Stir in a handful of drained canned spinach or peas for the last minute.

**Substitutions:** Swap any short pasta for barley (simmer 30 to 40 minutes instead), or use chickpeas for the beans.

### Pantry chili (serves 4, about 30 minutes)

Combine one can of kidney beans, one can of black beans, one can of tomatoes, one can of corn, 1 cup of water, and 1 to 2 tablespoons chili powder or mixed spices. Simmer 20 minutes. Serve over brown rice. It reheats well and stretches easily with a handful of extra rice or lentils.

### Tuna and veg rice skillet (serves 2, about 30 minutes)

Simmer 1/2 cup brown rice in 1 1/4 cups water for about 30 minutes, since brown rice is slow. Stir in a drained can of tuna, a can of peas and carrots, and pepper or dried herbs. Fast to assemble, filling, and gentler on a budget than you'd think.

### Warm oats for the morning

Cook 1/2 cup rolled oats in 1 cup water for 5 minutes. Add cinnamon, and raisins or canned fruit if you have it. If the weather's cold and the power's out, a hot bowl of oats is genuinely comforting.

## Mistakes to Avoid

**Too much salt.** Canned soups, meats and vegetables can be salty. Choose lower-sodium options where possible, rinse beans, and season with herbs and spices rather than extra salt.

**Too little fiber.** Lean on beans, lentils, oats, barley and whole-grain pasta instead of white bread and crackers.

**Buying food you won't eat.** A stockpile only helps if you'd happily cook from it. Start with meals your family already likes.

**Forgetting the practical gear.** Check you have a manual can opener, a way to heat food if the power's out, and water for cooking as well as drinking.

## Keep It Rotating

The most useful habit is the simplest. Once a week, cook one dinner entirely from the pantry and replace what you used. You'll learn what your family actually likes, keep everything fresh, and find any gaps while the stores are still open.

You don't need to prepare for every possible disaster. Start this week by adding three cans of beans, one bag of whole grains, and a can opener to your shopping list.
