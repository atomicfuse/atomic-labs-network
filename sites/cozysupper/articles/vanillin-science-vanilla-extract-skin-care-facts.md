You may have seen the headline: the flavor in your vanilla extract could help heal wounds. Here's the useful version. Researchers are interested in vanillin, the compound behind vanilla's flavor and scent, for medical wound-healing materials. That does not mean your baking bottle belongs in the first-aid kit. Keep it in the pantry, where it does its best work.

## What the research actually suggests

Per Women's Health, a scientific review points to vanillin as having potential in wound-healing materials. The reasons are its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial properties.

Notice the word *materials*. The idea is that vanillin could be built into specially designed products, such as future wound dressings. It is not an idea about dabbing a kitchen ingredient onto a scrape.

## Why vanilla extract isn't a first-aid product

The article stresses that this finding does not mean you should apply vanilla extract to cuts. Any medical use would need formulations that are designed and tested for the job.

That is the gap between a promising compound and a safe treatment. A lab ingredient in a carefully made dressing is one thing. A bottle meant for cookie dough is another. The research is about the first, not the second.

If you get a cut, clean it and cover it the way you normally would, and ask a medical professional if you're unsure how to care for it.

## Vanillin vs. vanilla extract

It helps to know what's in your cupboard. Vanillin is the compound responsible for vanilla's flavor and scent. It is one piece of the story, and the research looks at that compound specifically. Vanilla extract is the cooking ingredient you buy to flavor food. Studies on one don't automatically transfer to the other, and nothing in the review suggests they do.

The broader takeaway is a fun one. Researchers are re-examining familiar food and scent compounds for medical uses as they look for affordable, accessible ways to improve wound care. Everyday ingredients can have surprising biology. That's a reason to be curious, not a reason to experiment on yourself.

## Where vanilla really earns its keep: a cozy small-batch treat

Since the bottle is staying in the kitchen, put it to work. This warm vanilla baked custard is a small-batch dessert for two, built from pantry basics.

**Makes:** 2 servings. **Active time:** 10 minutes. **Bake time:** about 35 to 45 minutes.

### Ingredients

- 1 large egg plus 1 egg yolk
- 1 cup whole milk (2% works in a pinch)
- 3 tablespoons sugar
- 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
- Pinch of salt
- Ground nutmeg or cinnamon for the top

### Steps

1. Heat the oven to 325°F. Set two small oven-safe ramekins or mugs in a baking dish.
2. Warm the milk in a saucepan or the microwave until steaming but not boiling.
3. Whisk the egg, yolk, sugar, and salt in a bowl. Slowly pour in the warm milk while whisking so the eggs don't scramble.
4. Stir in the vanilla. Pour the mixture through a sieve into the ramekins if you want a silky texture.
5. Dust with nutmeg. Pour hot water into the baking dish until it comes about halfway up the ramekins.
6. Bake 35 to 45 minutes, until the edges are set and the center still jiggles slightly. Cool 10 minutes, then serve warm or chilled.

### Affordable swaps

- No whole milk? Use 2% or an unsweetened plant milk, though the custard will be slightly less rich.
- Vanilla extract running low? Imitation vanilla works fine in baked custard. A little goes a long way.
- No ramekins? Oven-safe mugs or small heatproof bowls work.

## Why a good bottle is worth keeping around

Vanilla is one of those pantry staples that quietly lifts everything it touches: oatmeal, rice pudding, pancakes, cookie dough. Store it tightly capped, away from heat and direct light, and it will be ready whenever a chilly evening calls for something sweet.

## The takeaway

Let the scientists test vanillin in the lab, and let your vanilla extract do what it does best. Pull out the bottle tonight and make something warm.
