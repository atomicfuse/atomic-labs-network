A recent New York Times piece, "How to Choose a Restaurant When You're Traveling," tackles a question every traveler hits around 5 p.m. in an unfamiliar city. We can't reproduce its advice here, so this guide builds its own decision framework on the same theme. By the end, you'll have a repeatable way to pick dinner quickly, spot a tourist trap, and decide when to book ahead and when to wander.

The stakes feel higher than they used to. Social media hype, online reviews, and hard-to-get reservations have turned a simple meal into a planning task. The fix is not more research. It is a better filter.

## Start With the Meal You Want, Not the Restaurant

Before you open a single app, decide what this meal is for. Most travel meals fall into one of three types:

- **The anchor meal:** a planned, possibly pricier dinner that showcases the region.
- **The local staple:** an everyday place where residents eat the dishes the destination is known for.
- **The quick bite:** street food, a market stall, a bakery counter.

A trip of several days works well with one anchor meal, a few staples, and plenty of quick bites. Naming the type first stops you from judging a $9 noodle shop against a tasting menu.

## Use Reviews as a Filter, Not a Verdict

Reviews help most when you read them for patterns instead of scores. A few habits keep this from eating your afternoon:

1. **Read the middle.** Skim a handful of three- and four-star comments. They tend to describe what the place is actually like.
2. **Look for specifics.** Mentions of particular dishes carry more weight than "amazing!" repeated twenty times.
3. **Check who is writing.** If reviews are mostly in the language of visitors and none in the local language, treat that as a signal to dig further.
4. **Set a limit.** Pick a shortlist of two or three places per meal and stop.

This is our own rule of thumb rather than a finding from the Times piece, but it works: once you have two good options, more research rarely improves the outcome.

## Read the Local Cues

You can learn a lot by standing outside for a minute.

### The menu
A short menu built around a few regional dishes often suggests a kitchen that does a handful of things well. A giant laminated menu with photos in five languages and every cuisine imaginable is a common sign of a place aimed at passersby.

### The crowd
Look for a mix of ages and groups who seem to know the routine: regulars chatting with staff, families, coworkers on a break. Empty tables at peak local mealtime deserve a question, though they may just mean you are early. Dining hours differ by region, so check when locals typically eat.

### The street
Restaurants directly beside major landmarks often cater to foot traffic. Walking a few blocks into a residential neighborhood frequently changes the picture, and so does a visit to a food market, where stalls specialize and turnover is quick.

### The touts
If someone on the sidewalk is working hard to pull you in, consider it a red flag. Good places with steady business rarely need to.

## Splurge or Casual Gem? A Simple Decision Rule

Choose the **splurge** when:
- The dish or style of cooking is central to the destination's identity.
- The restaurant takes reservations, and you can book before you leave.
- You have a specific occasion or a flexible budget.

Choose the **casual gem** when:
- You are short on time or tired.
- You want to try several regional specialties in one day.
- The best local version of a dish is traditionally sold at a stall, counter, or market instead of a formal dining room.

In many places, the most representative food is the cheapest. Treat a high price as a choice, not a proxy for quality.

## Balance Famous Spots With Open Slots

Hyped restaurants can be worth it, but reservation scarcity means they eat up planning energy. A workable balance is to book one headline meal if it genuinely excites you, then leave other meals open. Open evenings let you follow your nose, ask a hotel front desk or shop owner where they eat, and fit in a market visit without guilt.

## A Five-Step Checklist for Picking Dinner Fast

1. **Name the meal type:** anchor, staple, or quick bite.
2. **Shortlist two or three places** within walking distance.
3. **Skim reviews** for dish-specific detail and a mix of voices.
4. **Check the cues:** short menu, local crowd, no hard sell.
5. **Decide and commit.** If the first choice has a long wait, go to number two instead of restarting the search.

## A Handy Back-Pocket Move: Ask for the Local Dish

Once seated, ask the staff what the kitchen is known for or what a local would order. It signals curiosity, gets you off the tourist-default items, and often leads to the best plate on the table.

## Final Takeaway

Before your next trip, write down one anchor meal and leave the rest of your dinners open. Then, each evening, run the five-step checklist and eat where it points you.
