Fewer Americans are eating out every week, and the ones who still do are getting pickier. If you're part of that shift, this guide will help you decide which restaurant meals deserve your money and how to cover the rest at home without feeling deprived.

## What the new numbers say

Weekly restaurant dining in the U.S. has fallen to a record-low 39% of adults in 2026, according to a new Gallup poll, as reported by Fox News. That is down sharply from pre-pandemic levels. Middle- and upper-income adults are cutting back the most, per the report.

The takeaway isn't that Americans have given up on restaurants. The reporting frames it as people becoming more selective, driven by value, costs, safety concerns and changing habits. Dining out is moving from a routine family habit to an occasional treat, while restaurants deal with higher food and supply costs.

That shift is useful if you use it deliberately. When a restaurant meal is no longer automatic, you can spend it on the things a restaurant does better than your kitchen.

## Spend on what you can't easily make at home

A good rule for the occasional meal out: pay for skill, equipment and specialty ingredients, not for something you could assemble in 20 minutes.

- **Regional specialties with long prep.** Slow-braised dishes, hand-pulled noodles, fresh-made tortillas, dumplings and anything built on a stock that simmers for hours.
- **High-heat cooking.** Wok-fired dishes, wood-fired pizza and charcoal grilling are hard to match on a home stove.
- **Dishes with many small components.** Dim sum, mezze spreads and tapas give you a wide tasting range that would take a full afternoon of shopping and prep.

If the menu item is pasta with jarred-style sauce or a plain grilled chicken breast, that's the one to make yourself.

## Look for value that feels real

Value isn't only the lowest price. It's the gap between what you paid and how much you enjoyed it. A few ways to spot it:

1. **Check the shareable dishes.** Family-style plates and mezze-style menus let two or three people leave full without each ordering a separate entree.
2. **Look at lunch.** Many restaurants run a smaller, cheaper midday version of their dinner menu. Check the restaurant's own site or posted hours for specifics.
3. **Try food markets and street food.** Stalls and market counters tend to focus on one or two dishes done well, which is exactly what a selective diner wants.
4. **Order the house specialty.** A restaurant's signature dish is usually what its kitchen is practiced at. Ask your server what regulars order.

## Make one meal out the event

If you're dining out less often, turn it into a plan rather than a default. Pick the place ahead of time, look at the menu, and decide what you'll order. Choosing a neighborhood you rarely visit, or a cuisine you haven't tried, makes a smaller number of meals out feel bigger.

This also fits the mood the Gallup numbers suggest: a treat-yourself meal works best when you've chosen it on purpose.

## Fill the gap with a simple home dish

Cooking at home more often doesn't have to mean dull weeknights. Here is an approachable one-pan dish inspired by the shareable, Mediterranean-style plates that restaurants do well.

### Weeknight chickpea and tomato skillet

Serves 2 to 3. About 25 minutes.

**You'll need:**
- 2 tablespoons olive oil
- 1 onion, sliced
- 3 garlic cloves, minced
- 1 teaspoon cumin
- 1 teaspoon paprika
- 1 can (14 oz) diced tomatoes
- 1 can (15 oz) chickpeas, drained
- Salt and pepper
- Yogurt, fresh herbs and bread to serve

**Steps:**
1. Warm the oil in a wide skillet over medium heat. Cook the onion for 6 to 8 minutes until soft.
2. Add garlic, cumin and paprika. Stir for 30 seconds.
3. Pour in the tomatoes and chickpeas. Simmer for 10 to 12 minutes, until thickened.
4. Season with salt and pepper. Top with a spoonful of yogurt and herbs, and serve with warm bread.

Pantry staples do most of the work here, and the cost per serving stays low. I haven't run a cost comparison against restaurant prices, so treat the savings as a likely benefit rather than a measured one.

## Is the weekly dinner out really finished?

Not quite. Roughly four in ten adults still eat out at least once a week, per Gallup. What's changing is the habit behind it. Less routine, more intention.

## The one move to make this week

Pick one restaurant meal you'll actually look forward to, choose a dish you couldn't easily cook yourself, and make the other dinners at home. Fewer meals out can mean better ones.
