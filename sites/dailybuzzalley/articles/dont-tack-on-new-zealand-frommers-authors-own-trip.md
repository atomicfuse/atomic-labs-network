Pauline Frommer recently sat down with Naomi Arnold and Kate Evans, the authors of the newly published *Frommer's New Zealand*, to talk all things Kiwi, per a write-up carried by Yahoo. The headline takeaway, as it comes through in the coverage: New Zealand isn't a side quest. Stop treating it like one.

## The Australia Add-On Problem

If you've ever stared at a map of the Southern Hemisphere and thought, "Sydney's right there, what's another few hours?", the authors' advice is essentially to put the flight search down. The piece focuses on how New Zealand differs from Australia and why it deserves its own dedicated trip rather than a rushed extension.

It's hard not to see the logic. Tacking a country onto another one is the travel equivalent of watching the final season of a show on 2x speed while scrolling your phone. You technically saw it. You will not be able to discuss it.

## Small Country, Slow Roads

The guidance on planning centers on a deceptively simple idea: New Zealand is compact, but it's slow to drive. According to the coverage, the country's landscapes make distances that look tiny on a map feel much bigger behind the wheel.

That's where first-time visitors tend to overpack an itinerary, and it's the most practical thread running through the authors' advice. Our take: the map is the Instagram filter of trip planning. It flatters your ambitions. Build in breathing room, and choose between North Island and South Island highlights rather than trying to swallow both whole in a week.

## Māori Culture, Beyond the Greatest Hits

The other big thread is where and how to experience Māori culture. The piece highlights evolving, modern Māori tourism experiences that go beyond traditional performances. In other words, the experience on offer is no longer just a show you watch from a bench and then a hāngī (the traditional earth-oven meal) you eat afterward.

We'd be guessing if we named specific operators the authors recommend, so we won't. But the direction of travel is clear and welcome. Indigenous cultural depth is a big part of why travelers are looking at New Zealand for meaningful, nature-rich trips, and the framing here is about engaging respectfully and creatively rather than checking a cultural box.

A pop-culture aside, for the fans: many visitors arrive with the landscape already in their heads thanks to screen adaptations filmed there. It's a fair reminder that the country is not a backdrop. It has living cultures and a story that's far bigger than any location scout's shortlist.

## How to Actually Plan It

Pulling the authors' advice together, a sensible approach looks like this (the structure is ours, the underlying themes are theirs):

- **Give it its own trip.** Don't treat it as an Australia bonus round.
- **Respect drive times.** Compact on the map does not mean quick on the road.
- **Pick your island, or at least your priorities.** Trying to cover everything is the classic overpacking trap.
- **Make room for Māori experiences.** Look for modern, creative offerings, not only the traditional performance format.

## The Bottom Line

The best travel advice tends to be a polite version of "slow down," and this is no exception. Arnold and Evans, who wrote the book on it, are making the case that New Zealand rewards the traveler who shows up with fewer stops and more time. The country isn't going anywhere. Neither, if you plan this right, should your itinerary be sprinting.
