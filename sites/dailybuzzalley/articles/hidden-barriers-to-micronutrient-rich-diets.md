Having enough food is not the same as having a nutritionally adequate diet. That's the core finding of a EUFIC-led qualitative study, reported by Medicalxpress, which looked at vulnerable consumers in Italy and Poland. It found overlapping barriers standing between them and micronutrient-rich eating: cost, transport, cooking access, limits on food assistance, cultural fit, and confusing nutrition information.

Price is the barrier everyone expects. It's just one on a list.

## The "Eat Your Greens" Problem

Nutrition advice has a pop-culture quality: it's everywhere, endlessly repeated, and delivered with total confidence. "Eat more fruit and vegetables" is the *Keep Calm and Carry On* poster of public health. Nobody disagrees with it, and it assumes you can simply act on it.

The study's findings suggest that assumption is shaky. Per the Medicalxpress report, the researchers describe a web of obstacles rather than a single one, and the people facing them include low-income households, refugees, and people who rely on food aid.

The report frames this as a matter of "hidden hunger," meaning micronutrient shortfalls that can exist even when calories are not in short supply. It's hard to spot, because a full plate looks like a solved problem.

## Six Roadblocks, One Grocery Trip

The study lists these barriers, and it's easy to picture how they stack up in a single week:

- **Cost.** The obvious one, and the one policy conversations tend to stop at.
- **Transport.** Fresh, varied food does you no good if getting to it is a hurdle.
- **Cooking access.** Produce and raw ingredients assume you have somewhere to prepare them.
- **Food assistance limits.** Aid can fill a stomach without covering nutritional needs.
- **Cultural fit.** Food that doesn't match what people know and eat is less likely to become a regular part of a diet.
- **Confusing nutrition information.** Labels and guidance only help if people can make sense of them.

It's hard not to see the pattern here. Any one of these is a speed bump. Together they form something closer to an obstacle course, and "just eat healthier" is shouted at the runners from the sidelines.

## Food Security Isn't Nutrition Security

This is the study's sharpest distinction, and the one worth taking away. Food security means getting enough to eat. Nutrition security means getting the right mix of nutrients. The study argues that treating the first as proof of the second leaves a gap, and that vulnerable consumers fall into it.

The food-aid piece is especially interesting. Charity food systems exist to solve hunger, and the study points to limits in what assistance can provide. That's not a knock on the people running them. It's a reminder that a system built to prevent empty stomachs may need extra design work to also deliver nutrient variety. How aid could better serve refugees and low-income families is one of the practical questions the findings raise.

## Why Culture Belongs on the Menu

Any food lover knows that a meal is more than a nutrient delivery system. It's memory, routine, comfort, identity. The study's attention to cultural fit points to something public health messaging sometimes skips: people eat what makes sense to them.

For refugees and migrants in particular, a donated box of unfamiliar staples may not translate into meals people recognize or know how to prepare. That's our reading of the finding, not a claim from the study, but it's a natural place for the cultural-fit barrier to lead. Support that respects what people actually cook and eat has a better shot at being used.

## Why It Matters Right Now

Per the report, food prices, migration pressures, and inequality continue to strain European households. In that climate, hidden hunger is increasingly a public health issue rather than a niche concern. The study's conclusion, as reported, is that awareness campaigns aren't enough without practical, culturally sensitive support systems.

That's a pointed message in an era where wellness content tells people to optimize everything from breakfast smoothies to micronutrient stacks. The advice isn't wrong. It just quietly presumes a kitchen, a car or a nearby market, spare money, spare time, and a pantry that matches your tastes.

## The Takeaway

The most useful reading of this study is not a to-do list for individual shoppers. It's a nudge to stop treating a healthy diet as a matter of willpower. When the barriers are transport, cooking access, aid limits, and cultural fit, a slogan about vegetables isn't a plan. The people with the least room to maneuver are the ones most often handed the advice, and the study suggests the fix has to come from the systems around them.
