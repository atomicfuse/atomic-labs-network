Holiday party season is here, and so is the question that apparently needs to retire: "Why aren't you drinking?" According to Fox News Digital, etiquette expert Lisa Burdette says asking someone that is very impolite, and she offered a graceful way for non-drinkers to deflect it at gatherings.

The piece also cites survey data on the topic, though the details of that data aren't something we can break down here. What we can say is the framing: the question is out, and connection is in.

## Why This Question Keeps Landing Badly

On paper, it sounds friendly. In practice, it asks a guest to explain a personal choice to a room of people holding stemware.

The reasons someone might skip a drink are varied. Per the Fox News report, people abstain for health, religious, personal, or wellness reasons. Some of those are things a person may not want to discuss next to the cheese board. It's hard not to wonder how many awkward pauses at office parties trace back to one well-meaning interrogation.

The point from the etiquette side is simple: a guest who declines a drink has already answered. No follow-up needed.

## The Bigger Shift Behind the Etiquette

This isn't happening in a vacuum. The Fox News piece points to the rise of sober-curious culture and changing dining habits as reasons alcohol norms are shifting. That makes inclusive hosting more relevant than it used to be.

Our read: the old party script assumed a glass of something alcoholic in every hand, and a no-thank-you was treated as news. That script is getting rewritten. A holiday party where the bar is the only organizing principle is starting to look dated.

## What to Say Instead (If You're the Guest)

Burdette shared a graceful way to deflect the question, per Fox News Digital. The specific wording isn't something we can reproduce here, so rather than put words in her mouth, here are general principles in the same spirit, clearly our own suggestions:

- **Keep it short.** A brief answer closes the topic faster than an explanation opens it.
- **Redirect warmly.** Pivot to the food, the host's playlist, or how someone's year went.
- **Hold a drink anyway.** A festive glass of something nonalcoholic tends to stop the question before it starts. That's practical advice, not an etiquette rule.

You don't owe anyone a reason. A pleasant tone does the work.

## What to Do Instead (If You're the Asker)

If curiosity strikes, the better move is to ask about something else. Per the Fox News report, the underlying message for hosts is that connection matters more than alcohol. So lean into that.

Try asking what they're excited about this season, or what's good at the buffet. Same warmth, no spotlight.

## Hosting Without the Awkward Moments

If you're throwing the party, the fix happens before anyone arrives. The goal, as the report frames it, is making sure everyone feels included regardless of what's in their glass.

Some ideas from us, not from the report:

- Give nonalcoholic options real effort rather than leaving a lone can of soda at the back of the cooler.
- Put those drinks out in the same spot and with the same presentation as the alcoholic ones, so nobody has to ask for the "other" menu.
- Plan toasts that work with any beverage. A raised glass is a raised glass.

## The Toast Problem, Solved

There's an old superstition-adjacent notion that you shouldn't toast with water. Whether or not anyone at your party believes it, a host can defuse it by making toasts about the people in the room rather than the contents of the cup. Again, that's our suggestion, but it fits the report's theme that the gathering is about people.

## The Takeaway

The best holiday party question was never really about the drink. It's "How are you?" and, more specifically, "What's been the best part of your year?" Ask that, and nobody has to explain their glass to anyone.
