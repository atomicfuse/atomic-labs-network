Vogue recently caught up with Vicky Bennison, the author and creator of Pasta Grannies, about the older Italian women who keep handmade pasta traditions alive. The conversation centers on her new cookbook, *Eat Everything*, and it comes with a recipe: Rosa's cicatelli con ciambutella.

The timing is neat. Per the Vogue piece, the project arrives as interest grows in slow living, heritage cooking, longevity and low-waste food culture. It's hard not to see Pasta Grannies as the cheerfully floury answer to every hyper-optimized wellness routine that wants you to track, biohack and supplement your way to a good life.

## What Pasta Grannies Actually Is

Bennison's project documents Italian nonne preserving handmade pasta traditions, and, according to Vogue, sharing life wisdom along the way. That pairing is the whole appeal. You come for the hands shaping dough into forms most of us couldn't pronounce, and you stay because the women making them are, in effect, quietly explaining how to live.

The grannies are the point. Nobody here is a celebrity chef with a branded cookware line. These are older women whose knowledge lives in their hands and their kitchens, which is also exactly why preserving it matters. Vogue's piece touches on the broader worry that handmade food traditions are fading, and a project that records them is doing something more urgent than most lifestyle content ever attempts.

## La Dolce Vita, Minus the Filter

Here's my angle: "la dolce vita" has been so thoroughly claimed by travel aesthetics that it's easy to forget it's meant to be a way of living. Think spritz o'clock on a sun-drenched terrace, a linen shirt, a Vespa. Pop culture has been selling that postcard since Fellini's film of the same name gave the phrase its global fame.

Pasta Grannies offers a more practical translation. Vogue frames the project as a timely antidote to optimized wellness, and the everyday habits that emerge are refreshingly unglamorous:

- **Cook with what you have.** No special trip to a specialty store required.
- **Stay social.** Food made by hand tends to be food made together.
- **Keep moving.** Rolling, shaping and cutting dough is its own kind of daily activity.
- **Preserve the recipes.** Pass them on before they're lost.

Those are my takeaways from the themes Vogue highlights, not a checklist handed down by the grannies themselves. But they add up to something far more doable than a retreat in Tuscany.

## The "Eat Everything" Mindset

Bennison's new book title does a lot of work. *Eat Everything* points toward the no-waste cooking ethos that runs through Italian home kitchens, where using what's on hand is less a trend than a habit. In a moment when low-waste food culture is having its day, there's something funny about how long the nonne have been ahead of it. They didn't need a hashtag. They had leftovers and a rolling pin.

It's also a quiet rebuke to the idea that good eating requires a perfect pantry. If anything, the philosophy suggests the opposite: the pleasure comes from making something satisfying out of whatever's in front of you.

## Rosa's Cicatelli con Ciambutella

Vogue's feature includes Rosa's recipe for cicatelli con ciambutella, a handmade pasta dish that fits the book's spirit. I can't reproduce the full method here, so head to Vogue for the ingredients and steps. What's worth saying is that choosing a recipe like this one is the whole philosophy in miniature: a specific woman, a specific dish, and a technique that survives because someone bothered to show it on camera and write it down.

If you try it, treat it the way the grannies would likely want you to: a little patiently, with company if you can manage it, and without worrying about perfection.

## The Sweetest Part Is the Slowest

The most appealing thing about this whole project is how little it asks of you. No new gadgets, no subscription, no supplement stack. Just flour, time, other people and a willingness to pay attention to women who've been doing this far longer than any wellness influencer has been alive.

That's the real joke at the heart of the trend cycle: after years of chasing the secret to a good life, one of the most convincing answers is a granny at a wooden board, shaping dinner by hand and probably telling you to sit down and eat.
