InStyle has pulled together a 15-photo retrospective tracing the three children of Prince William and Kate Middleton, from their christenings through early public appearances and school days to boarding school. The facts at the foundation: Prince George, second in line to the British throne, was born July 22, 2013. Princess Charlotte arrived May 2, 2015. Prince Louis followed on April 23, 2018.

That's a lot of childhood to compress into one gallery. Here's the angle worth taking: the royal childhood is basically a series of set pieces, and the fun is watching the same script get handed to three different kids.

## The Same Three Chapters, Three Times

Per InStyle's rundown, the retrospective is organized less like a timeline and more like a checklist of royal milestones, and each child gets the full treatment:

- A christening
- Early public appearances
- Starting school
- Boarding school

Other entries cover family moments over the years, official portrait moments and recent photos at public events.

It's hard not to notice how much that structure says. Most families have a shoebox of photos that wanders wherever life does. For the Cambridge-era royals (the Waleses, as the family is now styled, though that's outside what InStyle's rundown covers), the milestones come pre-scheduled and pre-photographed. The christening, the first school morning and the move to boarding school are all moments the public gets to see, whether the kid is ready for a close-up or not.

## Christenings: Where the Lineup Begins

The first three entries are the christenings of George, Charlotte and Louis. They're the starting gun for each child's public life, and a good reminder that for royals the first photo op arrives before the first words.

The interesting part is the repetition. Three babies, three ceremonies, and, going by the way the gallery is built, three sets of photos that fans can line up side by side. That side-by-side game is the real engine of a retrospective like this. You aren't just looking at one baby in a christening gown, you're comparing three.

## Early Appearances: Learning the Job in Public

Entries four through six cover each child's early public appearances. Here the three-kid structure pays off, because the gallery gives each child their own beat rather than treating them as one blob of "the royal kids."

There's a pop-culture truth buried in that. Audiences have long treated royal children as an ongoing serial, with fans tracking who's grown taller, who's wearing what and who steals the scene at a big event. A gallery that splits the siblings into separate timelines is feeding exactly that habit.

## School Days, Then Boarding School

The back half of the gallery follows each child through starting school (entries seven to nine) and then boarding school (entries ten to twelve). That's the part that gives the retrospective its news hook.

InStyle's framing is that the children's transition to boarding school is a growth milestone that keeps public interest in the next generation of royals humming. That's the outlet's read, and it's a reasonable one. A first-day-of-school photo has universal appeal, because every parent with a phone has taken one. Boarding school is the point where the royal version diverges from the relatable one, and it's where the gallery shifts from baby photos to something closer to a coming-of-age story.

## Why These Galleries Never Get Old

There's a reason "look how they've grown" content is evergreen. It combines two things readers reliably love: a long-running storyline and the quiet shock of time passing. Anyone who remembers George's birth in 2013 can do the math on how long ago that was.

Some of that is just human. Seeing kids age in photographs makes the reader feel their own calendar flipping. Some of it is specific to royalty, where the institution depends on the public caring about the next generation. Whether that's charming or a little relentless is a matter of taste, and it's my take rather than anything InStyle states.

## The Gallery's Last Frames

The retrospective closes with family moments, official portrait moments and recent photos at public events, so it ends in the present rather than in a christening gown. That's a smart choice. The most satisfying part of any then-and-now is the "now," where you can hold the newborn photos up against the current ones.

It's the quiet punchline of the whole exercise. The first photo in a royal childhood is of a baby who has no idea he's being documented. By the last, the kids are old enough to be in on it.
