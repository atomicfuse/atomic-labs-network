Afar has updated its roundup of 17 tree house hotels around the world, and the headline takeaway is that the category has changed a lot. Per the outlet, tree house stays have evolved from rustic retreats into high-design destinations, with amenities like spas, restaurants, and panoramic suites. The childhood fort now has a concierge.

The list spans Costa Rican rainforests, Swedish Lapland, and African savannas, and Afar reports nightly rates running from $255 to $2,100 and up. That range is the story here, so let's follow it.

## From Backyard Hideout to Five-Star Address

If you ever nailed a few planks into a backyard oak and called it a clubhouse, you were an early adopter of a trend that has since hired an architect. Afar's framing is that tree houses have moved well beyond the budget-camping end of the travel spectrum and into proper hospitality.

It's hard not to see why the idea holds up. The appeal is built into the premise: you sleep at canopy level, and the landscape becomes the main attraction. What has changed, according to Afar, is how much comfort and design now sit on top of that premise.

## A Few Stops on the Tour

Afar's full list has 17 properties. We can only vouch for the handful named in the excerpt we have, so consider these a sampler rather than the whole menu:

- **Acre** in San José del Cabo, Mexico
- **andBeyond Lake Manyara Tree Lodge** in Lake Manyara National Park, Tanzania
- **Arctic TreeHouse Hotel** in Rovaniemi, Finland
- **The Green O** in Greenough, Montana
- **Hårtwood** in Minden, Ontario, Canada

The other dozen are in Afar's piece, and it's worth clicking through for them.

### The Geography Is the Point

Look at that sampler and you get a neat argument for the category. A safari lodge in Tanzania, a hotel in Rovaniemi, a Montana property, an Ontario retreat, and a stay in Baja's San José del Cabo have almost nothing in common climate-wise. The tree house is the constant; everything around it changes. Afar's wider list stretches that range to Costa Rica and Swedish Lapland.

That makes this one of the rare travel trends where you can pick your weather. Want savannas? Done. Want Arctic? Also done. Want something drivable from Toronto, perhaps? That's my guess for why Minden is on there, and it's only a guess, since Afar's excerpt doesn't say.

## Why the Timing Makes Sense

Afar's October 2026 update suggests there's fresh inventory and plenty of interest in the category. My read, and it's only a read: travelers have been gravitating toward stays that feel immersive and look good in a photo, and a room suspended among trees checks both boxes better than most hotels can.

There's also a pop-culture undertone. The tree house is one of those images that works across generations, from storybook cabins to Saturday-morning cartoons to every kid's imagined hideaway. Hotels have figured out that nostalgia is a pretty good amenity, and that adults will pay for it, especially at the higher end of that $255-to-$2,100-plus range.

## How to Use a List Like This

Since the price spread is so wide, a sensible way to read Afar's roundup is to sort by what you want from the trip rather than where it is:

1. **Wildlife:** The Tanzania lodge is the obvious pick if the goal is a safari with a canopy view.
2. **Cold-weather magic:** Rovaniemi is the one to look at if winter is your idea of an adventure.
3. **Closer to home:** Montana and Ontario put the tree house within reach of North American road trips.

Those groupings are my own shorthand, not Afar's. Check each property's own listing for what's included, because a nightly rate at the top of the range presumably buys a very different experience from one at the bottom.

## The Takeaway

The best thing about the tree house boom is how modest its starting point was. Somebody once decided that a few boards in a tree were worth sleeping in, and the hospitality industry spent the following years adding spas, restaurants, and panoramic suites to that idea. The fort never really changed. We just stopped needing a parent's permission to stay out overnight.
