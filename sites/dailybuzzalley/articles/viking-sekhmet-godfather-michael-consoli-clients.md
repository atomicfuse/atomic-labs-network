Most people who get a ship named in their honor would keep the champagne bottle for themselves. Travel advisor Michael Consoli, per a report from TravelPulse, did something more interesting: he made his clients part of the moment.

Consoli, described as a top Viking Cruises advisor, was named godfather of the Viking Sekhmet, a new ship sailing the Nile. According to the report, he shared the honor with his clients through a video, highlighting their support. That detail is the whole story, and it's the part worth a closer look.

## What a Ship Godfather Actually Is

Cruise lines have long tapped a godparent for new vessels, a ceremonial role that traditionally comes with a naming ceremony. The TravelPulse piece doesn't go into the ceremony's mechanics for the Sekhmet, so we'll stick to what's reported: Viking chose a travel advisor for the role, not a celebrity or a dignitary.

It's hard not to read that as a signal. In an industry where the face on the ship's christening is often a famous one, handing the honor to someone whose job is booking trips for other people says something about where Viking thinks its customers come from.

## The Video Move

Here's where Consoli's approach stands out. Rather than treating the title as a personal trophy, he used a video to share it with the clients who, in his telling, backed him along the way. That's the reported fact. The take is ours: it's a smart bit of relationship-building disguised as gratitude, or maybe gratitude that happens to be great relationship-building. Either way, it works.

Think about the dynamic. A travel advisor's entire business runs on trust. Clients hand over big-ticket vacations, often to places they've never been, based on one person's judgment. A video that says "this honor is partly yours" turns a transaction into a shared story. The next time those clients consider a Nile itinerary, they're not just booking a cabin. They're booking a ship their advisor helped christen, at least in spirit.

## Why the Nile, and Why Now

The Sekhmet's name is a nod to the Egyptian lioness goddess, though the TravelPulse summary doesn't elaborate on that, so we'll leave the mythology to the history buffs. What the report does say is that Consoli praised Viking for its luxury offerings and its strong partnership with advisors, and noted demand for culturally immersive experiences.

That last point lines up with a wider trend in premium travel: travelers who want more than a pretty view. Egypt is about as culturally loaded as destinations get, and a river ship lets you unpack once while the temples and tombs come to you. Consoli's comments suggest advisors are hearing the same thing from clients: they want depth, not just décor.

## The Advisor Comeback Narrative

There's a quiet storyline here about who holds influence in luxury travel. For years, the pitch was that apps and booking sites would make advisors obsolete. A cruise line putting an advisor in the godfather seat suggests the opposite, at least for the high-end market. When a trip costs real money and involves a destination with logistical complexity, a human who knows your preferences still carries weight.

To be clear, the report doesn't make that argument itself. It's our read of the situation, based on Viking naming an advisor to the role and Consoli's praise for the partnership. But the pieces fit.

## The Takeaway for Travelers

If you're eyeing a Nile trip, the practical lesson from this story is small but useful: the person who plans your journey matters as much as the ship that carries you. Consoli's move shows what a good advisor relationship looks like when it works, with credit shared, clients remembered and a sense that the trip starts well before boarding.

And for the rest of us, there's a gentle lesson in the video itself. When you get handed a shiny title, the savviest move may be to turn around and point at the people who got you there. Ship godfather is a great line on a résumé. Making your clients feel like part of the crew is better.
