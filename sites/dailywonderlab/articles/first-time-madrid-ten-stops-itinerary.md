Madrid can feel like too much at once: three famous art museums, a royal palace, a giant park, and a food scene that keeps going late into the night. By the end of this guide, you'll have ten stops, from a list Forbes put together, sorted into a route that makes sense for a first visit.

Forbes's roundup pitches Madrid as a city that blends world-class culture, royal history, food, nightlife and iconic public spaces. The trick is not to treat the list as ten equal boxes to tick. Group them by type, and the city starts to feel manageable.

## Start with the Golden Triangle of Art

Three museums form what's often called the Golden Triangle of Art. Each has its own focus, so you don't have to see all of them at full speed.

### Museo Nacional del Prado

The Prado is the headline museum on the list. If you only have time for one major art stop, this is the obvious candidate.

### Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofía

The Reina Sofía is the second corner of the triangle. Pair it with the Prado if you want to see more than one side of Spain's art story in the same trip.

### Thyssen-Bornemisza National Museum

The Thyssen completes the trio. A practical approach: pick one museum as your deep dive, and give the other two a shorter visit rather than trying to cover everything.

## Add royal history and a sunset view

### Royal Palace of Madrid

The Royal Palace anchors the city's royal-history side. Forbes places it alongside the museums as an essential stop, so it earns a slot on any first itinerary.

### Temple of Debod

The Temple of Debod is a good counterweight to a museum-heavy day. It's the kind of stop that fits a lighter, outdoors-oriented part of your schedule, and a sunset visit is a natural way to wind down.

## Give yourself open-air time

### El Retiro Park

El Retiro is Madrid's great public green space. After a morning of galleries, a stroll here resets your attention before the next stop.

### Gran Vía

Gran Vía is the city's iconic boulevard. Walk it rather than plan around it; it works well as a connector between other parts of your day.

## Eat and see flamenco

### Mercado de San Miguel

The market is the easy, flexible food stop. It suits a grazing-style lunch or snack between sights, when you don't want a full sit-down meal.

### Sobrino de Botín

Botín is the traditional restaurant pick. Treat it as a planned meal rather than a drop-in, and consider reserving ahead.

### Corral de la Morería

The flamenco stop closes the list. Evening performances pair naturally with dinner plans, and like Botín, this is one to book rather than leave to chance.

## A simple way to combine them

Forbes presents the list as ten attractions, not a schedule, so the pacing below is my suggestion, not theirs:

- **Day one:** Prado, then El Retiro for a breather, then Gran Vía.
- **Day two:** Royal Palace in the morning, Mercado de San Miguel for lunch, Temple of Debod near sunset.
- **Day three (if you have it):** Reina Sofía or Thyssen, then dinner at Botín or flamenco at Corral de la Morería.

With only two days, drop one of the museums and keep the evening anchors. Specific opening hours and ticket prices aren't covered here, so check each venue's official site before you lock in plans.

## The one thing to do

Book your dinner and flamenco slots first, then build the daytime stops around them. Those are the ones with fixed times; the parks, boulevards and market bend to fit.
