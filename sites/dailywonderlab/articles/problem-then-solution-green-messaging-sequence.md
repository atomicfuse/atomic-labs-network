If you write sustainability messages for a living, or just want to talk friends into greener habits, the order of your sentences may matter as much as the sentences themselves. Research reported by Phys finds that green messages are more persuasive when they move from a negative environmental problem to a positive, achievable solution. By the end of this article, you'll know how that sequence works and how to use it in your own messaging.

## What the research found

Per Phys, the study looked at sustainability communication in tourism. Messages that began with an environmental problem and then turned to a positive, achievable solution did better than messages that skipped the sequence.

According to the report, the effect unfolds in stages:

1. **Attention rises first.** The problem grabs the reader.
2. **Emotional evaluation improves.** The turn toward a solution changes how people feel about the message.
3. **Willingness to act increases.** People become more ready to do something.

That last step is the point. A message that gets noticed but leaves people feeling helpless hasn't finished its job.

## Why the order matters

The summary of the research suggests a simple reading: the problem earns attention, and the solution gives that attention somewhere to go. Reverse the order, or leave out the problem, and you lose the pull at the start. Leave out the solution, and you leave the reader holding a worry with no outlet.

That's my interpretation, not a quote from the study, but it fits the staged effect Phys describes: attention first, feelings second, action third.

The findings also point away from relying on fear alone. Fear can get a glance. The research suggests the shift to something achievable is what carries people toward acting.

## How to build a problem-then-solution message

### Step 1: Open with one clear problem

Name a single environmental issue, not a list. Keep it specific to your audience's situation. A travel example might be a concern tied to the destination the reader is considering.

### Step 2: Turn quickly to something doable

The solution has to be positive and achievable. If the action feels huge or vague, the sequence loses its power. Make the next step small enough that someone could take it today.

### Step 3: Make the action the final line

End on what the reader can do, not on the problem. The last thing people read should point forward.

## Before and after: a quick example

These two sample messages are my own illustrations of the idea, not examples from the study.

**Weak:** "We care about the planet. Choose our eco-friendly tours!"

There is no problem to catch attention, and the claim is generic.

**Sequenced:** "Busy trails are wearing down this landscape. You can help protect it: pick a small-group departure and stay on marked paths."

The problem comes first, the solution is concrete, and the action closes the message.

## Where this applies

Phys notes the study sits within tourism, where travel brands, destinations, and platforms are pushing greener choices. The same structure should travel well to other places you ask people to change a habit, such as a newsletter, a booking prompt, or a campaign. That's a reasonable extension, though the research reported covers tourism specifically, so test it with your own audience before assuming the same results.

## What to avoid

- **Fear with no exit.** A problem that never turns into a solution leaves people stuck.
- **Feel-good with no stakes.** Upbeat messaging that never names a problem misses the attention boost.
- **Solutions that feel out of reach.** The research stresses achievable ones.

## The takeaway

Before you publish your next green message, check its order: problem first, then a solution the reader can act on right away. If your draft ends on the problem, add one more sentence that tells people what to do.
