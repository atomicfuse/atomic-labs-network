New Yorkers have had decades of practice making tiny homes work. A recent HomeDecor roundup, republished by Yahoo, collects five tricks that stylish New York designers use in compact apartments. By the end of this article, you'll know what each trick is, why it works, and how to try it in your own place, whether you rent or own.

The common thread is simple: a small home doesn't have to feel crowded. It needs smart choices about storage, flow, seating, color, and lighting. HomeDecor's description puts it this way: with a creative approach, space-restricted apartments can be just as functional and stylish as larger homes.

A note on what follows: the source gives the five tricks and their general purpose. The step-by-step suggestions below are my own practical takes on how to apply them, not quotes or claims from the designers.

## 1. Use Vertical Storage

When floor space is scarce, the walls are your biggest unused asset. Vertical storage moves your belongings up and off the floor, so the room keeps its open feel.

**How to try it:**
- Look for blank wall space above doors, desks, and sofas, and picture shelves there.
- Choose tall, narrow furniture over wide, low pieces. A tall bookcase takes up the same floor footprint as a short one but holds far more.
- Renters can pick freestanding tall shelving instead of drilling, if the lease is strict.

Store the things you use daily at arm's reach and the occasional items higher up.

## 2. Add Banquette Seating

A banquette is a built-in or bench-style seat, usually tucked along a wall or into a corner. It's on the list because it handles seating without the bulk of several separate chairs, and it keeps the flow of the room clear.

**How to try it:**
- Put a bench along one wall of your dining or eating area and pull the table up against it.
- Choose a bench with storage inside if you can. That's my suggestion, and it ties neatly into trick one.
- Add cushions so it's comfortable enough to linger on.

## 3. Choose Light Colors in Open-Plan Spaces

In an open-plan apartment, where the living, eating, and cooking areas share one space, light colors help the whole area read as one calm, connected room. Busy or dark contrasts can chop a small space into awkward pieces.

**How to try it:**
- Keep walls, large furniture, and big textiles in a related light palette.
- Use accents (pillows, art, a throw) for variety rather than painting each zone a different shade.
- Repeat one or two colors across the space so your eye travels smoothly.

## 4. Embrace Bold Color in Small Rooms

This one seems to contradict the last, and that's the fun of it. The designers' point is that small rooms can handle bold color, and it adds personality rather than shrinking the space. The distinction is one of use: light tones for shared open areas, bold choices in a room that stands on its own.

**How to try it:**
- Pick a single enclosed room, such as a bedroom, bathroom, or entry, and give it a saturated color.
- Test a paint sample on the wall and look at it in morning and evening light before committing.
- Renters can get a taste with removable options like a bold bedspread, curtains, or a large piece of art, if painting isn't allowed.

## 5. Go for Wall Lights in Bedrooms

Bedside tables eat floor space. Wall lights lift the lighting off surfaces and onto the wall, which frees up the nightstand for what you actually need there, or lets you use a smaller one.

**How to try it:**
- Mount a light on each side of the bed, or one on the side you read on.
- If you can't hardwire, look for plug-in styles, which work with a cord and a wall hook. Check your lease first.
- Place them at a height where you can reach the switch from bed.

## Why This Matters

Per HomeDecor, the appeal here is compact living that still feels elevated and personal. Housing costs keep many people in smaller places, so making every inch work is a skill worth having.

You don't need to do all five. Pick the trick that solves your most annoying problem. If clutter bothers you, go vertical. If you have nowhere to sit, try a banquette. Start with one change this weekend and see how the room feels.
