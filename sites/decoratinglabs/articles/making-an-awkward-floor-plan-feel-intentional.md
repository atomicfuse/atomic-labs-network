If your home has a strange layout, a curve, a long bend, or two spaces that never quite met, you can stop treating it as a flaw. A 4,000-square-foot apartment in Mumbai, designed by Studio PKA and profiled by Architectural Digest, shows how. Its floor plan is shaped like a horseshoe, and the architects built the design around that shape instead of fighting it.

## The Project: Two Apartments, One Horseshoe

Per Architectural Digest, the home began as a pandemic purchase: two apartments joined into one, leaving a horseshoe-shaped plan. It belongs to a couple in Mumbai and their daughter, who regularly flies in from the US. The result is described as a calm, honey-toned home.

The takeaway is practical. A layout that looks awkward on paper can become the home's signature if you decide early to work with it.

## Lesson 1: Embrace the Curve

According to the report, the architects embraced the curve rather than trying to hide or square it off. You can apply the same thinking at any scale:

- **Name the quirk.** Decide what your odd feature is (a bend, an angled wall, a long corridor) before you plan anything else.
- **Let it set the rhythm.** Arrange furniture and circulation so the shape feels like the point of the room, not something you're working around.
- **Stop correcting it.** Fighting an angle with boxy furniture usually makes it look more awkward.

## Lesson 2: Plan Privacy for Two Generations

The home has to work for parents who live there full time and a daughter who visits from abroad. Architectural Digest highlights that the plan was organized for privacy across both generations. A horseshoe suits this well, since its two arms can naturally separate different parts of daily life.

If you host adult children, in-laws, or long-stay guests, ask these questions of your own layout:

1. Where does each person retire to when they want quiet?
2. Which spaces are shared, and which are clearly personal?
3. Can a visitor come and go without crossing the whole house?

You don't need 4,000 square feet to answer them. Even a door, a corridor turn, or a change in floor finish can mark a boundary.

## Lesson 3: Use One Design Language

Joining two apartments risks a home that feels like two homes. Per the profile, the answer was a single, cohesive design language, with a honey-toned palette running through the interiors. Large or oddly shaped homes feel calmer when materials and colors repeat.

To borrow the idea:

- Pick a small palette, such as one warm tone, one neutral, and one accent.
- Repeat a few finishes room to room instead of introducing something new in each.
- Let the connecting spaces, like halls and transitions, carry the same look as the main rooms.

## Lesson 4: Curate From Scratch

The report notes that every object, artwork, and finish was curated from scratch, treated as part of the architecture rather than an afterthought. Few of us can commission everything, but the principle scales down well: decide on the room's character first, then buy to fit it.

A budget-friendly version:

- Hold off on buying filler pieces just to cover empty space.
- Choose one or two meaningful items per room, such as a piece of art or a standout object, and build around them.
- Edit anything that doesn't match your palette or mood.

A home with fewer, chosen pieces reads as more considered than one full of matching sets.

## Lesson 5: Give the Shape a Story

The horseshoe is a traditional symbol of good luck, and the project leans on that metaphor, per the coverage. That's a useful reminder: a layout feels more intentional when you can describe what it means to you. A story helps you make decisions. If the shape suggests shelter, you might favor soft lighting and enclosed seating. If it suggests flow, you might keep sightlines open.

It doesn't have to be a symbol of luck. It just has to be a clear idea you can check each choice against.
