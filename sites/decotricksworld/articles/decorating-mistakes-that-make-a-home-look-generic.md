Most rooms don't look off because of one big blunder. They look off because of small choices that feel automatic: the art you hung without thinking, the pillows that came with the sofa, the shelf you styled the way everyone else did. Per a roundup originally reported by Yahoo, interior designers tend to notice five of these mistakes almost immediately. The good news is that every one is fixable, and most fixes can come from things you already own, from thrift stores, or from secondhand finds rather than a big shopping trip.

The common thread in that roundup is that a home looks better when choices feel intentional, personal, and properly scaled. Here is how to apply that, with a lean toward reuse over buying new.

## 1. Hanging Too Many Small Pieces of Art

A wall dotted with lots of little frames can read as scattered rather than curated. The issue is scale: small pieces get lost on a large wall.

Here's how to fix it without spending anything:

- Take the small pieces down and lay them out on the floor. Group them tightly so they read as one larger shape.
- Or choose a single bigger piece for the wall and move the small ones to a shelf or a smaller wall.
- Reuse mismatched frames you already have by painting them one color, which makes a cluster feel deliberate.

The goal is for the art to feel proportional to the wall it lives on.

## 2. Relying on So-So Pillows

Limp, forgettable throw pillows are one of the quickest ways a sofa looks unfinished. Designers notice pillows that are flat, ill-fitting, or simply bland.

You don't need to buy a whole new set. Try these instead:

- Swap in fuller inserts so covers actually hold their shape. Stuffing a cover with a slightly larger insert is a common trick for a plumper look.
- Replace just the covers. Vintage textiles, a worn-in linen tablecloth, or fabric from a thrifted garment can be sewn or wrapped into fresh covers.
- Mix textures and patterns rather than buying a matching set.

This is upcycling at its easiest: the sofa looks more expensive, and nothing new has to be manufactured.

## 3. Choosing Mass-Market Art Only

The roundup flags art that comes entirely from big-box, mass-market sources. There's nothing wrong with a poster or print on its own, but a home filled only with the same pieces everyone else has can feel generic.

This is where secondhand really pays off:

- Browse thrift shops, estate sales, and flea markets for original paintings, old prints, or interesting frames.
- Frame something personal, like a child's drawing, a map of a place you love, or a fabric scrap.
- Mix one or two mass-market pieces with something one-of-a-kind so the whole wall feels chosen rather than copied.

Vintage finds also keep existing objects in circulation, which is the most eco-friendly kind of decor.

## 4. Turning Books Backwards

Books turned spine-in, so the pages face out, are a styling habit that has spread widely. It's meant to create a neutral look, but designers reportedly spot it right away, and the downside is practical too: you can't tell what the books are.

Here's a simple correction:

- Turn the spines out. Titles and worn covers show personality.
- Stack some books horizontally and prop others upright for variety.
- If the colors clash, you can wrap a few in paper or fabric you already have, rather than flipping them around.

Books you actually read are a quiet way to make a space feel lived in.

## 5. Treating Every Shelf the Same with Trendy Items

The last mistake is styling every shelf with the same trendy objects in the same arrangement. It looks like a catalog page rather than your home.

Try a shelf reset:

1. Clear the shelf completely.
2. Put back only things that mean something to you: a ceramic from a trip, a family photo, a plant in a repurposed jar.
3. Vary heights and leave some breathing room. Not every inch needs filling.
4. Give each shelf a slightly different feel instead of repeating one formula.

Before buying anything for a shelf, shop your own home first. A bowl from the kitchen or a candlestick from another room can work well.

## Why This Matters Now

Many homes are styled with social media in mind, and the risk is spaces that look the same as everyone else's. The roundup points to a broader move toward personality-driven interiors, vintage finds, and meaningful decor instead of copy-and-paste trends. That's good for your wallet and for the planet, since personal and secondhand items are usually the ones that already exist.

## Your Five-Minute Designer Audit

Walk through your main room and ask one question of each wall, pillow, book, and shelf: did I choose this, or did it just land here? Fix the first thing that answers "just landed," using something you already own.
