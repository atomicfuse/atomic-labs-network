Got a layout that refuses to behave? A long hallway, a strange curve, two spaces that never quite became one? A recent Mumbai project shows what to do with it: stop fighting the shape and design around it.

Architectural Digest profiled a 4,000-square-foot apartment in Mumbai by Studio PKA. Per the report, it began as a pandemic purchase, with two apartments joined into one, leaving a floor plan shaped like a horseshoe. The home is for a couple and their daughter, who regularly flies in from the US. The result is described as calm and honey-toned. Here's what you can borrow from it, even if your home is a fraction of the size.

## Embrace the curve instead of hiding it

According to the profile, the architects embraced the horseshoe rather than trying to straighten it out. That's the core lesson, and it scales down well.

If your home has an odd feature, try this:

- **Name the quirk.** Is it a curve, a long corridor, an awkward bend? Decide it's the main feature, not a flaw.
- **Repeat it.** An echoed shape in a mirror, rug, or arched shelf makes the quirk look deliberate.
- **Plan around it.** Place furniture and circulation to follow the shape instead of working against it.

The horseshoe also carries a nice bit of symbolism, since it's widely seen as a good-luck emblem. That's a reminder that a home's story can be part of its design.

## Plan privacy for more than one generation

This home has to work for a couple living there day to day and a daughter who visits from abroad. The profile highlights that the architects planned privacy for two generations. A horseshoe layout lends itself to this, since the two ends of the shape can naturally sit apart.

You can apply the same thinking without a horseshoe:

### Think in zones, not rooms

Group the spaces used by each person or generation, and keep shared areas in the middle. Guests or visiting family then have somewhere that feels like theirs, and the household keeps its own rhythm.

### Decide who crosses whose path

Before buying anything, trace the route from each bedroom to the kitchen and the main living area. If one person's route cuts through someone else's space, that's the problem to solve first.

## Use one design language throughout

A large or oddly joined home can feel like several separate apartments stitched together. This one was two apartments combined, which is exactly that risk. The profile points to a single, cohesive design approach, and the calm, honey-toned palette is a big part of how it reads.

To get that effect at home:

1. **Pick a narrow palette.** Choose a warm base tone and a few supporting colors, then repeat them in every room.
2. **Repeat materials.** The same wood tone or finish appearing in different spaces ties them together.
3. **Keep transitions quiet.** Flooring and wall colors that change abruptly at every doorway break the flow.

This works in a one-bedroom as well as a 4,000-square-foot home. Consistency is what makes a space feel calm.

## Curate everything as part of the architecture

Per the profile, objects, artwork, and finishes were curated from scratch. Nothing was inherited from a previous layout to be worked around. That's a luxury most of us don't have, but there's a usable principle inside it: treat furnishings as part of the plan, not an afterthought.

You can borrow it in small ways:

- **Decide on the big pieces first,** then choose art and accessories to suit them.
- **Buy slowly.** Leave a wall or corner empty until the right piece turns up rather than filling it to fill it.
- **Reuse what fits the language.** If an existing piece doesn't match your palette or materials, refinishing or relocating it beats forcing it in.

This is also where eco-friendly choices fit naturally. Curating fewer, better pieces and giving existing furniture a new finish means less waste than a full replacement.

## Why this matters for ordinary homes

Most of us live with some compromise: a rented layout, a joined-up space, a room that's the wrong shape. The profile makes a quiet argument that hybrid living, visiting family, and entertaining all put new demands on a home, and that unconventional layouts can handle them well when they're planned for. That framing is the Architectural Digest piece's, and it's a useful way to look at your own space.

## Try this weekend

Walk through your home and find the one feature you've been trying to hide. Then write down a single way to repeat, frame, or highlight it. Start there, and let the awkward part set the design.
