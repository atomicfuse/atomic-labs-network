Fiber is having a moment, and "fibermaxxing," the habit of piling on as much as possible, is the latest version of it. Here's the useful part: you can get more fiber into your dinners without the bloating, so you'll know the signs you've gone too far and how to build up at the table in a way your gut can handle.

The message from Women's Health, which reported on the trend, is a balanced one. Fiber is healthy, and most people need more of it. But raising your intake too aggressively can upset your digestion. Overdoing it is uncommon, per the outlet, but possible.

## Signs You May Be Eating Too Much Fiber

Per Women's Health, the signals that you may be overshooting include:

- **Diarrhea**
- **Constipation**
- **Bloating**
- **Loss of appetite**

The list looks contradictory, since diarrhea and constipation are opposites, but both can show up when your gut is being pushed harder than it's ready for. If you notice any of these after a recent change in how you eat, the fiber ramp-up is a reasonable suspect. I can't diagnose you, and persistent or severe symptoms are worth raising with your doctor or a dietitian.

## Why It's Easier to Overdo Than You'd Think

Women's Health points to the wellness-culture appeal of fiber: gut health, feeling full, and better metabolic markers. Meanwhile, high-fiber packaged foods and low-net-carb products are becoming more common. That means fiber can sneak into your day from places you're not counting, like a bar at lunch, a fortified snack, or a "high-protein, low-carb" wrap.

A practical habit: glance at the fiber line on labels for anything packaged you eat regularly. You're not trying to ban those foods, just to know what's stacking up.

## Build Fiber Up Gradually, One Dinner at a Time

The core fix is the one the trend tends to skip: don't change everything at once. Here is a gentle way to approach a week of dinners. These are my suggestions as a cook, not figures from the report, so adjust to how you feel.

### Change one thing per dinner

Pick a single swap per meal rather than overhauling the plate:

- **Monday:** Stir a can of rinsed beans into a pot of tomato soup or pasta sauce.
- **Wednesday:** Swap half the white rice in a stir-fry for brown rice.
- **Friday:** Add a big handful of vegetables to a sheet-pan dinner.

If Monday leaves you bloated, hold there for a few days before moving on to the next change.

### Keep portions of the new ingredient modest

Start with a few spoonfuls of beans or lentils, not a full cup. You can always add more next time. Taking it slowly lets you see which food is responsible if something doesn't sit right.

### Spread fiber across the day

Rather than loading everything into dinner, let breakfast and lunch carry some of it, so no single meal is doing all the work. This also keeps dinner from becoming a heavy, hard-to-digest plate.

## An Easy One-Pan Dinner to Try

This is a flexible template, serving 4, that adds fiber without going extreme. Total time is about 35 minutes.

**You'll need:** 1 pound chicken thighs or firm tofu, 1 can (15 ounces) chickpeas, drained and rinsed, 3 cups broccoli florets, 1 sweet potato cut into small cubes, 2 tablespoons olive oil, salt, pepper, and your favorite spice blend.

1. Heat the oven to 425°F.
2. Toss the sweet potato cubes with 1 tablespoon oil and roast alone on a sheet pan for 10 minutes.
3. Add the chicken or tofu, chickpeas, and broccoli, drizzled with the remaining oil and seasoned well.
4. Roast 20 to 25 minutes more, until the chicken reaches 165°F and the vegetables are browned at the edges.

**Substitutions:** Swap cauliflower for broccoli, white beans for chickpeas, or regular potatoes for sweet potato. If beans are new for you, start with half the can and save the rest for tomorrow's lunch.

## Check Your Pantry for Hidden Fiber

Before you add more, take stock of what's already there. High-fiber snack bars, low-net-carb tortillas, and fortified cereals can all quietly raise your total. If you're already feeling off, cutting back on those packaged items may be easier than changing your dinners.

## Treat Fiber Like a Dial, Not a Goal

Fiber is good for most people, and Women's Health is clear that most of us need more of it. The aim isn't the biggest number; it's the amount that leaves you comfortable. This week, make one swap at dinner, pay attention to how you feel for a few days, and only then add the next.
