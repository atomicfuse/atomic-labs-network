New Haven, Connecticut, has an official "pizza guru," and his job is to walk visitors through the city's pizza culture. Colin Kaplan, a pizza historian and tour guide, is the subject of a recent Fox News feature on the Elm City's growing food tourism scene. Per Fox, the piece takes viewers through the city's beloved culinary traditions and its legendary local pizzerias. It also covers an unusual overnight stay designed for devoted food enthusiasts.

You may not be booking a train to Connecticut this weekend. You can still steal the best idea from that tour: eat several versions of the same food side by side and talk about what makes each one different. Here is how to turn that into an easy dinner party at home.

## Why a pizza crawl works as a dinner party

A tour like Kaplan's is built on comparison. You taste one place's pizza, then another's, and you start noticing differences you would have missed eating one pie on its own. That format suits a casual gathering because it gives guests something to do and something to argue about, and the host does almost no cooking.

I can't tell you from the Fox report exactly which pizzerias Kaplan visits or how he describes them, so I'll leave those to the source. The format is the part that travels.

## Step 1: Pick your lineup (3 pizzerias, 6 to 8 guests)

Three different pizzerias is the sweet spot. Fewer than that and there's nothing to compare; more and people get full before the last stop.

Choose places that make different styles if your town allows it: a thin, charred pie from one spot, something thicker from another, a neighborhood slice shop for the third. Order a plain cheese or simple tomato pie from each, so the crust, sauce, and cheese are what you're comparing, not the toppings.

### How much to order

This is a tasting, not a full dinner. As a working guideline:

- Plan on 1 to 2 slices per person per pizzeria.
- For 6 to 8 guests and three pizzerias, that usually means one large pie from each place.
- Add one extra pie of your most crowd-friendly style for seconds.

If your guests are big eaters or the pizza is the only food, add a fourth pie.

## Step 2: Stage it like stops on a tour

A crawl moves from place to place. At home, you recreate that with rounds.

1. **Round one:** Serve the first pizza on its own. Ask everyone to say one thing about the crust.
2. **Round two:** Bring out the second pie. Now compare it with the first.
3. **Round three:** Serve the third and let people vote on a favorite.

Space the rounds about 20 to 25 minutes apart. That leaves time to talk, and it keeps the pizza from sitting out too long.

### Keep the pizza hot

Pick up or have the pies delivered close to serving time, and stagger the orders if you can. If you need to hold a pie, a 200°F oven keeps it warm for a short stretch. To revive a slice that has cooled, a hot skillet for a few minutes crisps the bottom better than a microwave does.

## Step 3: Add two easy sides

Pizza tastings need something to reset the palate. Keep sides simple and cold so you aren't cooking while guests eat.

- **A sharp green salad:** Arugula, thinly sliced red onion, lemon juice, olive oil, salt. Toss it right before serving. Baby spinach or any peppery green works in place of arugula.
- **Marinated vegetables or a relish tray:** Jarred pickled peppers, olives, and carrot sticks need no prep beyond opening the jars.

Put out water and something light to drink. Strongly flavored drinks make it harder to taste the differences between pies.

## Step 4: Give people a scorecard

This sounds fussy, but it makes the night. Hand out a small card or a sticky note with four categories:

- Crust
- Sauce
- Cheese
- Overall

Score each from 1 to 5. Tally at the end, announce a winner, and settle the debates. If someone's favorite wins, they will talk about it for weeks.

## Make it a bigger weekend

The Fox report mentions an overnight stay designed for devoted food enthusiasts, which shows how far the pizza-tourism idea can go. You can borrow a smaller version of that spirit. Invite guests to bring a dessert or a drink, and have breakfast ready for anyone who lingers. Leftover cold pizza has its fans, and a skillet with a fried egg on top does a decent job with the rest.

## The real appeal of food tourism

Kaplan's role suggests something simple. A city's food identity gets stronger when someone tells its story and shows people where to eat. The same is true at your own table. When you give your guests a framework, whether a tour, a scorecard, or three pies to compare, an ordinary pizza night turns into an event.
