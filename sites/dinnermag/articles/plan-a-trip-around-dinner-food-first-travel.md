Real Simple recently ran a first-person case for planning every vacation around food, paired with nine tricks for making trips more delicious and memorable. The nine aren't spelled out in the material I have, so I won't pretend to list them. What the piece argues is simple, and it's worth borrowing: let meals be the spine of your trip, and the sightseeing will arrange itself around them.

Here is a practical way to do that without turning your vacation into a spreadsheet or a budget disaster. By the end, you'll have a loose framework for choosing a few anchor meals, finding places locals actually eat, and carrying the trip home with you.

## Start With Dinner, Not the Attractions

Most itineraries begin with landmarks and squeeze meals into the gaps. Flip that. Before you book anything, ask what this place is known for on a plate. A regional specialty, a famous market, a bakery with a line out the door: these give each day a shape.

Then build outward. If the dish you want is served at dinner, keep the afternoon light. If a market is best in the morning, put it first. Travelers increasingly want trips that feel personal and local rather than like a checklist, and food is one of the easiest ways to get there.

## Pick Two or Three Anchor Meals, Not Twenty

The fastest way to make food-first travel feel exhausting is to plan every bite. Instead:

- Choose **one anchor meal per day at most**, and fewer on busy days.
- Pick **two or three "must-try" foods** for the whole trip. These are the dishes you'd be sorry to miss.
- Leave the other meals open for wandering, snacking, and whatever smells good.

Reserve only the anchors that truly need it, such as a popular dinner spot. Everything else can be decided that morning.

## Use Markets and Bakeries as Your Flexible Meals

Markets, bakeries, and casual counters are where a destination's everyday cooking shows up, and they rarely need a reservation. They're also easier on the wallet than a string of sit-down dinners.

A simple rhythm that works:

1. **Morning:** a bakery or café near where you're staying.
2. **Midday:** a market lunch, eaten standing up or on a bench.
3. **Evening:** your anchor dinner, or a lighter spread of snacks if you've had a big day.

This keeps the trip from feeling over-scheduled, and it gives you room to say yes to something unplanned.

## How to Find Where Locals Actually Eat

You don't need insider contacts. A few habits go a long way:

- **Walk a few blocks away** from the main sights before choosing a place. Restaurants right beside major attractions are the likeliest tourist traps.
- **Ask people who live there.** A hotel front desk, a shopkeeper, or a market vendor will often point you somewhere better than a generic list will. Ask "where would you eat tonight?" rather than "what's good?"
- **Look at the menu.** A short menu focused on regional dishes is usually a better sign than a long one with something for everyone.
- **Notice who's eating there.** A room full of residents at a normal mealtime tells you plenty.

## Avoid Tourist-Trap Meals

The signs are fairly consistent: hosts pulling you in off the sidewalk, photo-heavy menus in many languages, and a menu that tries to cover every cuisine. None of these is proof on its own, but together they're a cue to keep walking.

The fix isn't snobbery. It's a little research ahead of time. Pick a few backup options near each day's plans, so when you're hungry and tired you aren't settling for the first open door.

## Keep It Affordable

Food-led travel doesn't have to mean splurging nightly. Spend where it counts and save elsewhere:

- Make **one dinner a splurge**, and keep the rest casual.
- Treat markets, bakeries, and street-level counters as full meals.
- Share dishes so you can taste more for less.

## Bring the Trip Home to Your Table

This is where a trip stays memorable. Before you leave, decide on one thing to carry back: a spice blend from a market, a local pantry staple, or simply a note about a dish you loved. Jot down what was in it and how it was served.

Back home, recreate it on a weeknight. Keep the version simple: one main, one side, and the one ingredient you brought back. Substitute freely. If you can't find a regional herb or cheese, a close cousin from your own grocery store will still get you the spirit of the meal, and the memory with it.

## Your Next Step

Before your next trip, write down just three foods you want to eat there. Plan the days around those three, leave everything else open, and let the rest of the meals surprise you.
