A curved, horseshoe-shaped floor plan sounds like a headache. Long arcs, odd corners, rooms that seem to wander away from each other. But a 4,000-square-foot apartment in Mumbai, designed by Studio PKA and profiled by Architectural Digest, shows how that kind of layout can become the best thing about a home. Here is what you can borrow from it, whether your own plan is curved, chopped up, or just stubbornly strange.

## The Project in Brief

Per Architectural Digest, the home began as a pandemic purchase: two apartments joined into one, with a floor plan that bends into a horseshoe. It belongs to a couple in Mumbai and their daughter, who regularly flies in from the US. Studio PKA's task was to make the space work for two generations while keeping it calm and cohesive.

The reported approach comes down to a few decisions: embrace the curve instead of fighting it, plan privacy deliberately, and curate every object, artwork, and finish from scratch. Each one translates to smaller homes.

## Embrace the Quirk Instead of Hiding It

The instinct with an awkward plan is to disguise it: add walls, square off corners, force the space into a standard box. Studio PKA, according to the profile, took the opposite route and leaned into the curve.

You can apply the same thinking at any scale:

- **Name the oddity.** Is it a bend, an angled wall, a long narrow hall, a corner nobody uses? Decide whether it is a feature before you decide how to fix it.
- **Let it set the rhythm.** A curve naturally pulls you from one space to the next. Place the things you want people to notice, such as art or a favorite piece of furniture, along that path.
- **Resist over-correcting.** Every wall you add to "normalize" a plan costs you the character that made it distinctive.

There is also a nice bit of symbolism here. A horseshoe is traditionally seen as a good luck charm, and the home's shape gives its story a built-in metaphor. Whether or not you care about luck, finding a story in your layout helps you make confident choices.

## Plan Privacy for Every Household Member

This home needs to serve parents who live there daily and a daughter who visits from abroad. That is a familiar situation for many households now: adult children returning, relatives staying for weeks, guests, or hybrid work schedules that put several people at home at once.

The lesson is to plan privacy before you plan style. Try this on your own floor plan:

1. **Map who needs what.** List each person or group and when they are home. A visitor who stays a month needs different things than a weekend guest.
2. **Separate quiet from social.** Keep sleeping areas away from the spaces where you entertain, using distance, doors, or a change in level of activity.
3. **Give visitors a base.** Even a single room with its own storage and a door that closes makes a long stay easier for everyone.
4. **Check the sightlines.** From the main seating area, what can you see into? Fix the views you don't want before choosing furniture.

A horseshoe plan helps by nature, since the two arms of the shape can sit far apart. If your home is a more conventional box, you can create the same effect with rugs, furniture placement, and door choices that signal where the private zone begins.

## Use One Design Language Throughout

A large home made from two joined apartments risks feeling like two different homes. The profile describes the result as calm and honey-toned, a cohesive palette carried through the space. That consistency is what lets a big, curved plan read as one idea.

You don't need 4,000 square feet to use this:

- **Pick a limited palette.** Choose a dominant tone, in this case warm honey, and a couple of supporting ones. Repeat them room to room.
- **Repeat materials.** The same wood, stone, or metal finish appearing in several places ties separate spaces together.
- **Vary the shapes, not the language.** Rooms can have different functions and still feel related if finishes and proportions echo one another.

Cohesion is especially useful in awkward layouts. When the architecture is doing something unusual, a quiet, consistent surface treatment keeps it from feeling chaotic.

## Curate From Scratch, and Treat Objects as Architecture

Per Architectural Digest, the interiors here were curated from scratch: objects, artwork, and finishes chosen to suit the home rather than carried in from elsewhere. That is a luxury most of us can't fully replicate, but the principle scales down well.

If you are furnishing a difficult space, try this order of operations:

1. **Fix the big decisions first:** walls, flooring, built-ins, and main finishes.
2. **Choose major furniture next,** testing each piece against the shape of the room, especially along curves and corners.
3. **Add art and objects last,** and only where they have a reason to be there.

Buying pieces to fit the space, instead of squeezing existing pieces into it, is how a home ends up feeling designed rather than assembled. If you already own a lot, edit hard. Keep what suits the palette and the plan, and let the rest go.

## What to Try in Your Own Home

You probably don't have a horseshoe. You likely do have something that annoys you: an off-angle wall, a long hallway, a room that never quite works. Start with a simple exercise. Stand in that spot, decide what it could be if it were on purpose, and then choose one finish, one piece of furniture, or one piece of art that supports that idea.

The takeaway from this Mumbai apartment is simple: stop apologizing for the awkward part of your plan, and design around it as if it were the point.
