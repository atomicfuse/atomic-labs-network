Halloween packs a doorbell storm, a bowl of candy, strange costumes, and spooky decor into a few hours. For a dog, each of those is a stress trigger or a safety risk. A short plan made before the first trick-or-treater arrives will cover most of it. Here is how to build one, based on guidance reported by WFTV.

The core idea from that guide: Halloween can still be fun with your dog around, as long as you plan for what stresses them and what could hurt them.

## Start with the door, because that's where the chaos is

The doorbell rings again and again, strangers in odd outfits stand on your porch, and the door keeps opening. That is a lot for any dog, and it's also an escape opportunity.

A few doable ways to handle it:

- **Put your dog in a separate, closed-off room** before trick-or-treating starts, away from the front door and the noise.
- **Give them something to do in there**, like a chew or a favorite toy, so the quiet room feels like a good place rather than a punishment.
- **Consider setting a bowl outside** or meeting kids at the end of the driveway, so the door stays shut.
- **Check that the ID tag and collar are current** in case your dog does slip out.

If your dog normally barks at every knock, assume Halloween will be several times worse and plan for that.

## Lock down the candy

The simplest rule: candy lives where your dog can't reach it. That means the bowl by the door, kids' trick-or-treat bags left on the floor, and wrappers in low trash cans.

The guide flags toxic treats as a major risk. Rather than guess which sweets are safest, treat all of it as off-limits and store it up high or behind a closed door. If kids are collecting candy, tell them to keep their bags out of the dog's reach too.

## Check costumes for red flags

Not every dog wants to be a pumpkin. Before you commit, try the costume on a day or two ahead and watch your dog's reaction. Signs it's a bad idea include:

- Your dog freezes, hides, or keeps trying to pull it off.
- It restricts movement, vision, or breathing.
- It has small pieces your dog could chew off and swallow.

If the outfit isn't working, skip it. A bandana or nothing at all still makes a fine photo, and your dog will be happier.

## Watch the decorations

Spooky decor is another hazard. Dogs chew and tug on things, so keep decorations, cords, and anything small or breakable out of reach, and be wary of new objects placed at nose level.

## Know when to call the vet

If your dog does get into candy or chews on a decoration, don't wait to see what happens. Contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic promptly, and have the details ready: what your dog ate, roughly how much, and when.

It helps to find your nearest emergency vet's number and hours before the night starts, since you won't want to be searching while a trick-or-treater is at the door and your dog is in trouble. Save it in your phone now.

## A quick pre-Halloween checklist

1. Pick the quiet room and stock it with a chew.
2. Test any costume a day or two early.
3. Move candy and decor out of reach.
4. Confirm your dog's tag and collar are secure.
5. Save your vet and emergency clinic numbers.

Do the first item today: choose the room where your dog will spend the evening, and set it up before the doorbell starts.
