A cat who bolts under the bed at every knock, or won't come out when guests visit, can be helped. You won't turn a timid cat into a social butterfly overnight, but you can give her a plan for feeling safer. Here is a simple, gradual approach you can start today.

The idea comes from a CBS News piece on helping scaredy cats become confident, which draws on advice from animal advocate Carol Erickson. We're working from a summary of that piece, not her full list of tips, so the steps below are a general framework rather than her exact words. Check the original for her specific guidance.

## Start by Making Home Feel Safe

Confidence grows from security. Before you try to change how your cat reacts to anything, make sure she has places where she can relax.

- **Give her a retreat.** A covered bed, a cardboard box, or a cat tree perch lets her hide and watch without being cornered.
- **Offer height.** Many cats feel safer when they can see the room from above.
- **Keep the basics predictable.** Feed, scoop, and play at roughly the same times each day.

Don't pull a hiding cat out. Hiding is how she copes, and forcing her out usually makes the fear worse.

## Identify Exactly What Scares Her

"Scared of everything" is rarely true. Spend a few days noting what sets her off: the vacuum, visitors, the doorbell, a certain room, sudden movement. Write down what happens and how she reacts.

A short list gives you something to work with. You can tackle one trigger at a time instead of trying to fix a whole personality.

## Expose Her to Triggers in Tiny Doses

This is the core of helping an anxious cat: start with a version of the trigger so mild she barely notices it, then pair it with something she loves.

Take a cat who fears the vacuum. A gradual version might look like this:

1. Leave the vacuum parked in the room, switched off, while she eats a treat.
2. Move it a little, still off, while she gets a treat.
3. Turn it on in another room at a distance, with treats or a favorite toy.
4. Slowly bring the sound closer over many days.

The rule is simple: if she freezes, flattens her ears, or runs, you went too fast. Back up a step. Sessions of a few minutes are plenty.

## Use Play to Build Courage

Play lets a nervous cat practice stalking, pouncing, and succeeding. A wand toy is a good choice because it keeps your hands away from her and lets her chase from a comfortable distance.

- Aim for a few short sessions a day.
- Let her "win" and catch the toy often.
- Finish with a treat or meal so the session ends on a high note.

## Let Her Set the Pace With People

If visitors are the problem, ask them to ignore her. No reaching, no staring, no calling her over. Have guests sit quietly and let her approach on her own terms. A treat tossed gently near (not at) her can help her link strangers with good things.

If you live with children, teach them the same rules. Quiet voices and no chasing go a long way.

## Reward Small Wins, and Know What Progress Looks Like

Progress with a fearful cat is usually quiet. Watch for:

- Coming out of hiding a little faster than last week
- Eating or playing with a mild trigger in the room
- Relaxed body language, such as a loose tail and soft eyes
- Choosing to sit in the same room as you

Praise softly and reward these moments with a treat. Noticing small wins helps you stay patient, which matters because this process takes weeks or months, not days.

## A Simple 30-Day Shape You Can Follow

This is our own way of organizing the work, not a program from the CBS piece.

- **Week 1:** Set up hiding spots and perches, keep routines steady, and log her triggers.
- **Week 2:** Add daily wand-toy play and begin the mildest version of one trigger exercise.
- **Week 3:** Repeat, moving up a step only if she stays relaxed.
- **Week 4:** Introduce a calm visitor or a slightly harder step, and note how far she's come.

Expect setbacks. A bad day doesn't erase progress; just drop back to an easier step.

## When to Call Your Vet

Sudden fearfulness in a cat who used to be confident can have a medical cause, such as pain or illness. Hiding, loss of appetite, or litter box changes are all worth a vet visit. Rule out health issues first, and ask your vet or a qualified behavior professional for help if the fear is severe or isn't improving.

## The One Thing to Do Today

Pick a single trigger, find its mildest version, and pair it with a treat for two minutes. Then stop and repeat tomorrow. Small, calm, repeatable sessions are what turn a scaredy cat into a braver one.
