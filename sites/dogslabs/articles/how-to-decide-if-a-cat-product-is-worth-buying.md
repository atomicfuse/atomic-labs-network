BuzzFeed Shopping recently published a roundup of 27 Chewy products for cat owners, pitched as practical buys that make life cleaner, easier, and more fun. We can't walk you through the individual picks here, because the full list isn't something we can reproduce accurately. What we can do is give you something that outlasts any single list: a quick way to judge whether a cat product is worth your money, whether it comes from that roundup or your own late-night scrolling.

By the end, you'll have four questions to ask before you click "add to cart," plus a few habits that keep you from buying the same useless thing twice.

## Why a Curated List Helps (and Where It Stops)

Per BuzzFeed, the pitch is that these products are worth the investment because your cat is. That's a nice sentiment, and curated lists do save time when you're overwhelmed by thousands of options on a retailer like Chewy.

But a list can't know your cat. A product that's perfect for a bored young indoor cat may be pointless for a senior who sleeps 16 hours a day. So treat any roundup as a shortlist, then run each candidate through your own filter.

## The Four-Question Test

The BuzzFeed piece frames its picks around making life easier, cleaner, and more fun. That maps neatly onto a test you can use on anything.

### 1. Does it fix a problem you actually have?

Write down your three most annoying cat-related chores or complaints. Litter tracked across the floor? Scratched sofa arm? A cat who yowls at 5 a.m. out of boredom? Only shop against that list. If you can't name the problem a product solves, it's a want, not a need. Wants are fine, but price them as wants.

### 2. Will your cat actually use it?

This is where most pet purchases fail. Look at what your cat already does for free: climbs, scratches, hides in boxes, stares out windows. Products that support a behavior your cat already shows have a much better hit rate than ones that ask your cat to learn something new.

If your cat ignores every toy you own but loves cardboard, a fancy gadget is a gamble. A sturdy scratcher or a window perch might not be.

### 3. Does it make your side of the deal easier?

Convenience is a legitimate reason to spend. If a product cuts a daily five-minute chore down to one minute, that adds up over a year. Ask how often you'd use it, and whether it's easy to clean. Anything that's a hassle to wash tends to stop getting washed, and then stops getting used.

### 4. Is the price fair for how long it will last?

Divide the price by how many months you realistically expect to use it. A sturdy item that lasts two years can beat a cheap one you replace every few weeks. A consumable, like food or litter, is judged differently: compare cost per serving or per use rather than the sticker price.

## Spend More On These, Less On Those

A useful rule of thumb from our side, not from BuzzFeed: put your money where your cat spends the most time and where your home takes the most wear.

- **Worth spending more on:** things your cat uses daily, like the litter setup, the main scratcher, and the bed or perch they favor.
- **Fine to go cheap on:** novelty toys. Cats often lose interest quickly, so buy one, watch, and only then buy more.

## How to Test Before You Commit

You don't need a lab. Try these low-effort habits:

1. **Buy one, not a bundle.** Unless it's a consumable you've used before, start with a single item.
2. **Give it a week.** Put it where your cat already hangs out and note whether they investigate it in the first few days.
3. **Watch the rotation.** Swapping a toy out for a few days and bringing it back can renew interest before you decide it's a dud.
4. **Check return terms.** Before buying, glance at the retailer's return policy so a miss doesn't become a loss.

## Special Cases

### New cat parents

You'll be tempted to buy everything at once. Resist. Start with the basics your cat needs for daily life, then add extras once you see how your cat behaves. Your cat's habits will tell you what to buy next.

### Multi-cat households

Resources matter more when there are several cats. Consider whether one item can realistically serve everyone or whether you need duplicates in different spots. Shared items that cause squabbles aren't a bargain, however cheap.

### Indoor cats

Indoor cats depend on you for stimulation. Climbing spots, scratching surfaces, and things to watch are all reasonable places to put enrichment money, because they replace what the outdoors would otherwise supply.

## Read Lists Like a Skeptic, Gently

Shopping roundups are written to help you buy, and many retail links can earn the publisher a commission. That doesn't make the picks bad, but it's a reason to cross-check a product against reviews from people with cats like yours before spending.

## The Takeaway

Before your next cat purchase, name the exact problem it solves and the behavior your cat already has that it supports. If you can do both in one sentence, buy it. If you can't, leave it in the cart for a day.
