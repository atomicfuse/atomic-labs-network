Disney, the studio that yanked its content from Netflix in 2017 to launch its own streaming service, is now handing some of it back. Per Vulture, Disney is licensing a selection of its library to Netflix, with the Ice Age films and *Percy Jackson and the Olympians* heading to the service this weekend and more titles to follow soon.

The reported list also includes legacy shows like *Felicity* and *Revenge*. What's conspicuously absent: Marvel, Star Wars and Pixar films, according to Vulture's report.

That absence is the whole story. This looks like a two-tier library strategy, and it's hard not to read the deal through that lens.

## The Velvet Rope Around the Crown Jewels

Per Vulture, the licensed batch is a curated one. Ice Age, Percy Jackson and a handful of network-era dramas are going out the door. The franchises that arguably define Disney+ are staying home.

That's our read, not a confirmed Disney explanation, but the pattern is tidy: license what's useful for revenue, keep what drives subscriptions. Nobody subscribes to Disney+ for the sake of *Revenge*. Plenty of people subscribe for Marvel, Star Wars and Pixar. Renting out the first category while guarding the second is the kind of move that lets a company have its cake and sell slices of it too.

It's also a notable shift from 2017, when Disney pulled its films from Netflix to build Disney+. The reported context for the new deal is that Disney+ faces subscriber pressure and competition, which makes licensing secondary content a revenue play. On Netflix's side, it's reportedly continuing to acquire licensed content to broaden a catalog that once leaned heavily on its own originals.

## Timing Is Doing a Lot of Work

The schedule doesn't look accidental. Two Disney properties in the batch have fresh releases on the horizon: *Ice Age: Boiling Point* is slated for theaters in February, and *Percy Jackson* season three is due in November.

Putting earlier installments in front of Netflix's audience beforehand is a classic warm-up act. Viewers who binge the earlier Ice Age films this fall are, in theory, primed to buy a theater ticket in February. Anyone who finishes the first seasons of Percy Jackson on Netflix might be primed for season three in November. Whether that actually plays out is speculation on our part, but the calendar lines up almost suspiciously well.

One wrinkle worth wondering about: if the audience that catches up on Netflix is satisfied there, does it ever migrate to Disney+ for the new season? Disney presumably thinks the marketing value outweighs that risk, though we can't say how the company weighed it.

## The Nostalgia Play

The most fun part of the reported lineup is the throwback TV. *Felicity* and *Revenge* are legacy titles, and Vulture's report also points to *This Is Us* in the nostalgia conversation. Network-era dramas have a particular appeal for streaming: lots of episodes, familiar faces, and a built-in audience that remembers watching them live.

There's precedent for this kind of revival. The so-called "Netflix bump" is the phenomenon that revived *Suits*, a show whose licensed arrival on the service brought it a fresh wave of viewers. If a similar surge happens here, one of these older shows could become an unlikely breakout. Our bet, and it is only a bet, is that the long-running dramas have the better shot than the animated sequels, since binge-ready volume is exactly what that effect tends to reward.

## What the Deal Reveals

Strip away the nostalgia and the picture is about economics. Streaming's early years were a land grab, with studios hoarding libraries to launch their own platforms. Now the pendulum seems to be swinging back toward pragmatism: why let a back catalog gather dust when a rival will pay to put it in front of millions of subscribers?

For viewers, it means a little less fragmentation, at least for these titles. For Disney, it's a hedge. For Netflix, it's more shelf space with recognizable names on it.

Disney spent years building a walled garden. It now appears willing to open a side gate, as long as Marvel, Star Wars and Pixar stay locked inside. The sharpest takeaway may be that the streaming wars aren't ending so much as developing trade agreements.
