Jason Sudeikis showed up to the *Ted Lasso* Season 4 red carpet in Los Angeles this week with the full squad in tow — Hannah Waddingham, Juno Temple, Brendan Hunt — and, per Deadline's coverage of the event, Apple CEO Tim Cook made the trip out too. That's a lot of institutional faith in one mustache.

The premiere event wasn't just a cast reunion photo op, though those are always worth a scroll. It was the first public confirmation of something that sounds either like a brilliant creative swing or the most ambitious pivot since Ted moved to England in the first place: Season 4 sends Ted Lasso to coach a second-division women's football team.

## The Premise Shift That Changes Everything

For three seasons, *Ted Lasso* lived and died on the dynamics of AFC Richmond — the locker room culture, the Diamond Dogs, Roy Kent's perpetual scowl, the biscuits. The show's emotional architecture was built around that specific club, those specific men, and the particular flavor of toxic masculinity that Ted was quietly dismantling with kindness and Midwestern patience.

So dropping Ted into a women's second-division squad isn't just a new set of characters. It's a structural reset. The same fish-out-of-water engine runs, but the context it's operating in is genuinely different — women's football carries its own cultural weight, its own real-world battles for recognition and resources, and the show has a chance to be as pointed about that world as it was about the men's game without simply repainting the same walls.

Whether the writers thread that needle well is the question Season 4 will have to answer. But as a creative choice, it's at least an interesting one — a show refusing to simply replay its own greatest hits.

## The Cast: Who's Back, Who's New

The red carpet confirmed the returning core: Sudeikis leading the charge, with Waddingham, Temple, and Hunt all back in the fold. That the original ensemble is still largely intact matters, because *Ted Lasso* without its ensemble isn't really *Ted Lasso* — the show was never really about football, and it was only partly about Ted. It was about a community of people getting better at being people.

New cast members are joining the Season 4 lineup alongside the returnees, though the specific names weren't detailed in premiere coverage. The arrival of the women's team means fresh faces are essentially baked into the premise — which is a tidier way to expand a cast than the usual maneuver of just adding characters to an already crowded room.

## Apple TV Has a Lot Riding on This

Tim Cook at a red carpet is not nothing. Apple TV has had a genuinely strong slate — *Severance*, *The Morning Show*, *Slow Horses* — but *Ted Lasso* was the show that made the streaming service feel like a real destination rather than an expensive experiment. Seasons 1 and 2 were phenomena. Season 3, which dropped in 2023, had a more complicated reception and ended in a way that made a fourth season feel both unlikely and necessary.

The fact that it's here at all is a small triumph. That Cook showed up to the premiere suggests Apple is treating this as a flagship moment, not a quiet content drop.

## The Schedule: Weekly, Starting August 5

Season 4 launches on Apple TV on August 5, with episodes rolling out weekly through October 7. That old-school rollout model — one episode at a time, every week — is worth noting. In an era when Netflix drops entire seasons like they're clearing inventory, the weekly format keeps the conversation alive longer and lets moments breathe. *Ted Lasso* has always been a show that rewards the kind of slow, communal watching that weekly releases encourage. The fan forums after each episode of Season 2 were genuinely alive in a way that binge-drops rarely sustain.

Eight-ish weeks of episodes means the show runs through the heart of summer and into early fall, a stretch when streaming competition is real but appointment viewing is still possible.

## What the Women's Team Premise Actually Promises

It's hard not to wonder whether *Ted Lasso* going to the women's game is partly a response to the real-world explosion of women's football. The NWSL has grown dramatically, the Women's World Cup has become an actual cultural event, and the Lionesses' story has kept English women's football in the public conversation in a way that feels different from even five years ago.

If the show is paying attention to that context — and there's reason to believe it is, given the care the original seasons took with the sport's actual culture — Season 4 could feel plugged in rather than tacked on.

The second-division detail is also interesting. Not the top flight, not a glamour club. Second division means scrapping for promotion, limited resources, and the kind of underdog energy the show runs on. Ted's never coached from a position of institutional power, and there's no reason to start now.

## The Longer Bet

The red carpet in Los Angeles this week was, at its core, a statement: the show is back, the people who made it what it was are still here, and the story has somewhere new to go. Whether Season 4 delivers on that promise won't be settled by premiere photos or a well-attended event — it'll be settled episode by episode, starting August 5.

But for a series that many assumed had said its goodbye, showing up at all with this much talent and this much genuine creative ambition is, as a certain coach might say, a good sign.
