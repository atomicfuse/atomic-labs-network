## Why Canned Foods Deserve a Second Look

Health describes canned foods as shelf-stable, cost-effective, and convenient. For a beginner cook, that adds up to a pantry that doesn't spoil while you figure out what's for dinner. With grocery prices still high and many people trying to waste less, a few cans on the shelf are a practical safety net.

## Read the Label Before You Buy

Canned doesn't automatically mean healthy, so a ten-second label check matters. Two things to look at:

- **Sodium.** Savory cans like beans and lentils are often packed with salt. Look for a lower-sodium or no-salt-added version when it's available.
- **Added sugar.** For fruit, pick cans packed in water or juice rather than heavy syrup.

A simple habit also helps with beans and lentils: drain them and give them a good rinse in a colander before cooking. This is a common cook's trick, and it takes about 30 seconds.

## Lentils: Your Fastest Soup Base

Canned lentils skip the simmering step, so they're ready as soon as you open the can. That makes them ideal for weeknights.

**Quick warm lentil salad (about 10 minutes, serves 2):**

1. Drain and rinse one 15-ounce can of lentils.
2. Warm them in a skillet with 1 tablespoon olive oil for 2 to 3 minutes.
3. Stir in 1 tablespoon lemon juice or red wine vinegar, a pinch of salt, and black pepper.
4. Top with chopped tomato, a handful of herbs if you have them, and a spoonful of yogurt.

Swap idea: no lemon? Any vinegar works. No fresh herbs? Dried oregano or cumin changes the flavor in a pleasant way.

## Beans: The Most Flexible Can on the Shelf

Beans are the pantry workhorse. Black beans, chickpeas, white beans, and kidney beans all do different jobs, and one can usually serves two people as a main component.

**Spiced chickpeas over rice (about 15 minutes, serves 2):**

1. Drain and rinse one 15-ounce can of chickpeas.
2. Heat 1 tablespoon oil in a pan over medium heat. Add half a diced onion and cook 4 minutes.
3. Add 1 teaspoon cumin, 1/2 teaspoon paprika, and the chickpeas. Stir for 3 minutes.
4. Add a small can of diced tomatoes (or half a regular can) and simmer 5 minutes.
5. Serve over rice or with flatbread.

This leans on flavors common in Middle Eastern and North African cooking, and it's a gentle introduction to spice if you're new to it.

Other easy moves: mash white beans on toast with olive oil and garlic, or add black beans to quesadillas.

## Sardines: Small Fish, Big Flavor

Sardines are the item people hesitate over, and Health lists them among the surprisingly good choices. If you've never tried them, start gently. Their flavor mellows when you pair them with something acidic and crunchy.

**Sardine toast (about 5 minutes, serves 1):**

1. Toast a thick slice of bread.
2. Spread on a little mustard or mashed avocado.
3. Lay on 3 to 4 drained sardines and press them lightly with a fork.
4. Finish with a squeeze of lemon and black pepper.

They also work stirred into hot pasta with garlic, olive oil, and chili flakes, a pantry dinner that echoes classic Mediterranean cooking.

## Canned Fruit: Dessert and Breakfast Helper

Canned fruit is easy to overlook, but it's useful when fresh fruit is out of season or has gone soft. Choose fruit packed in water or juice to keep added sugar down.

**Two-minute ideas:**

- Spoon drained peaches or pears over plain yogurt or oatmeal.
- Chop and stir into pancake batter.
- Warm with a pinch of cinnamon for a simple topping.

## Build a Small Starter Shelf

You don't need to buy everything at once. If you're starting from zero, here's a modest plan:

- Two cans of beans (one chickpea, one black or white)
- One can of lentils
- One or two cans of sardines
- One can of fruit in water or juice

With that, you can make every recipe above, and each takes 15 minutes or less.
