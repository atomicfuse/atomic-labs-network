Japan is one of the most food-focused travel destinations around, and a little planning goes a long way. Tasting Table recently rounded up nine tips for finding the best food while traveling there. Here they are, with practical ways to put each one to use, so you can eat like a local rather than just follow the crowd.

## 1. Research Regional Specialties Before You Go

Japanese food varies by region, so the best meal in one city may not exist in the next. Before you book anything, look up what each place on your route is known for. Write a short list per destination, even just two or three dishes, and let it guide where you eat.

This also saves you from the default option of eating the same famous dishes everywhere. Matching your meals to the place is the simplest way to taste something that feels specific.

## 2. Follow Locals Instead of Tourist Crowds

A line of locals is a better signal than a menu with photos in five languages. When you're choosing between two spots, look at who is eating there. If the room is full of people who clearly live nearby, that's a good sign.

This tip pairs well with the next ones, because locals tend to eat in the places described below.

## 3. Explore Train Stations and Department-Store Food Halls

These are easy to overlook, and they're often right on your route. Train stations and department-store food halls pack a wide range of food into one place, which makes them ideal when your group can't agree or you're short on time.

Try this: when you arrive in a new city, walk through the station or the nearest department-store food hall before choosing lunch. You'll see what's on offer and can pick something you wouldn't have found by searching online.

## 4. Try Small Neighborhood Restaurants

Smaller restaurants away from the main sightseeing areas are where many travelers find their most memorable meals. Pick a residential street, walk a few blocks beyond the landmarks, and look for modest places with a short, focused offering.

If you're a beginner at ordering abroad, a small place with a limited menu is actually easier. There's less to decode, and you can ask about the one or two things they do best.

## 5. Use Convenience Stores Strategically

Convenience stores are a useful part of an eating plan, not just a last resort. They work well for a quick breakfast, a snack between sights, or a light meal when you don't want a full sit-down.

The strategy is to treat them as a supplement. Grab something simple there, then save your appetite and budget for the meals you really want to remember. It's also a low-cost way to sample unfamiliar items.

## 6. Visit Markets for Fresh, Casual Bites

Markets are built for grazing. Rather than committing to one big meal, you can try several small things, which suits a curious eater and lets you taste more in less time.

Go hungry, bring a bit of cash and a light touch, and share what you buy with your travel companions so you can sample more.

## 7. Learn Basic Food Phrases and Etiquette

You don't need fluency. A few food phrases and a basic understanding of dining etiquette make ordering smoother and show respect for the place you're visiting. Before your trip, learn how to say a few things such as greetings, thank-you, and how to ask for a recommendation, and read up on basic table manners.

It's a small effort that tends to make interactions friendlier, especially in small restaurants where staff may not have much English.

## 8. Book Popular Restaurants in Advance

If there's a restaurant you really want to try, don't leave it to chance. Popular places can fill up, so reserve ahead of time. Pick your one or two must-visit spots, book them first, and build the rest of your itinerary around them.

Leave the other meals flexible. That balance gives you a few guaranteed highlights and room for the spontaneous finds described above.

## 9. Stay Open to Unfamiliar Dishes

The final tip may matter most: be willing to order something you can't identify. Some of the best discoveries come from dishes you wouldn't have picked at home.

A simple rule helps. Once per day, order one thing you've never tried. Ask the staff what it is, or what they'd suggest, and see where it leads.

## Putting It Together: A Simple Eating Plan

You don't need to use all nine tips on every day. Here's an easy way to combine them:

- **Before the trip:** Research regional specialties, learn a few phrases, and book your must-visit restaurants.
- **Each morning:** Grab something simple from a convenience store.
- **Midday:** Check out a station or department-store food hall, or a market.
- **Evening:** Walk a few blocks off the main path and choose a small neighborhood restaurant where locals are eating.

This mix keeps costs reasonable, keeps you moving, and gives you a mix of planned and unplanned meals.

## Your Next Step

Open your itinerary and write one regional dish next to each city you're visiting. That single list will point you toward better meals than any viral spot.
