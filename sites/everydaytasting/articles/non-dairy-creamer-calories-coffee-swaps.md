Americans aren't drinking much more coffee than they used to, but they're getting nearly 40% more calories from it. That's the finding of a new study, as reported by Healthline, and the main driver is the growing use of non-dairy creamer. If you pour a splash into your mug every morning, this article shows you how to see what's actually in your cup and how to swap in cheaper, simpler options without making your coffee taste sad.

## What the study found

Per Healthline's report, coffee consumption among U.S. adults stayed stable between 2003 and 2018. The calories coming from coffee beverages, though, climbed by nearly 40%.

Non-dairy creamer was the primary culprit. Its caloric contribution doubled, from 7.7 calories per day to 18.5 calories per day on average.

That number may look small. Eleven extra calories a day is not a crisis. But it's an average across all coffee drinkers, and it shows a slow shift: the same habit, quietly becoming a more caloric one. Healthline also notes that over 60% of Americans drink coffee daily, so small changes add up across a lot of mugs.

## Why this sneaks up on you

Black coffee has very few calories, and many people think of coffee as a basically healthy drink. Creamers change that picture, and they're easy to underestimate because the serving is small and poured by eye.

Here's the practical problem: the label's serving size may be much smaller than what you actually pour. If you glug creamer into a big mug, you may be using two or three servings without noticing. (This is general label-reading advice, not a finding from the study.)

## How to read a creamer label in 30 seconds

Stand in the grocery aisle and check three things:

1. **Serving size.** Compare it with how much you really pour. Be honest.
2. **Calories per serving.** Multiply by your real number of servings.
3. **Ingredient list.** Look at what comes first. If sugar or oil leads the list, you're buying something closer to dessert than a splash of milk.

This takes half a minute and costs nothing. Do it once, and you'll know whether your usual bottle deserves its spot in the fridge.

## Simple swaps that keep coffee tasting good

You don't have to go black. Try these, starting with the easiest.

### Swap 1: Plain milk

A splash of regular milk is cheap, you probably already have it, and it gives coffee a rounder, softer taste. Start with about 2 tablespoons in a standard 8 to 12 ounce mug and adjust.

### Swap 2: Cut your creamer gradually

If you love your current creamer, don't quit cold turkey. Use a little less each week. For example, if you pour 3 tablespoons now, drop to 2½ for a week, then 2. Your taste buds adjust faster than you'd expect.

### Swap 3: Add spice instead of sweetness

A small shake of cinnamon, about ¼ teaspoon, stirred into the grounds before brewing adds warmth that makes coffee taste a little sweeter without sugar. It's a trick used in many coffee traditions, and it takes five seconds.

### Swap 4: Try chicory

Chicory root is a roasted, slightly bitter, slightly nutty ingredient often blended with coffee, famously in New Orleans-style brews. Replace a small portion of your grounds, say 1 tablespoon of chicory per 4 tablespoons of coffee, for a deeper flavor that stands up with less milk. Start small, since it's a strong taste.

### Swap 5: Make a better coffee

Sometimes we load coffee with creamer to cover harsh flavors. Fresher beans, a coarser grind for a drip machine, and water that's hot but not boiling can make a cup that needs far less doctoring. Try one change at a time.

## A quick weekday plan

If you want a no-fuss way to test this, spend two weeks:

- **Week 1:** Read your creamer label, measure what you actually pour with a tablespoon, and write it down.
- **Week 2:** Switch to plain milk or cut your creamer by a third, and add cinnamon to the grounds.

The whole experiment takes a few minutes of effort and might save you money, since a carton of milk usually stretches further than a specialty creamer bottle (check your own prices to confirm).

## What about black coffee?

Healthline's coverage points to black coffee's documented health benefits, and that's part of why the add-ins matter. The report's concern is that heavy use of caloric extras can undercut a drink many people assume is healthy. I'd add my own take: you don't need to be a purist. A modest splash of milk is a perfectly reasonable middle ground.

## The one thing to do

Tomorrow morning, measure your creamer with a real tablespoon before it hits the mug. Once you see the actual amount next to the label, you'll know exactly where to start trimming.
