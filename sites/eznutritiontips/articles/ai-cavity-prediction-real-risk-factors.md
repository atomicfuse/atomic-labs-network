Brushing matters, but it's only part of the cavity story. A new study suggests that where you live, what you earn, and whether you can see a dentist may shape your risk just as strongly. Here's what the research found and how to use it to protect your own teeth, starting with what ends up on your plate and in your glass.

## The Study: Machine Learning Meets a National Survey

An international team of researchers used artificial intelligence to predict who is at high risk for cavities, per Medicalxpress. The findings were published in the journal Caries Research. The team applied machine learning to Brazil's national oral health survey, aiming to predict both cavity risk and severity.

The goal is population-level: helping health systems spot which groups need prevention most, rather than waiting for people to show up in pain. Cavities affect billions of people worldwide, and public health systems have limited resources, so targeting matters.

## What the Model Flagged as Strong Signals

The researchers found that several factors strongly shaped outcomes:

- Wealth
- Household density
- Race
- Dental insurance
- Recent dental visits
- Access to fluoridated water

Notice what's missing from that list: nothing about how many times a day someone brushes. That doesn't mean brushing is pointless. The summary of the study doesn't say what the survey measured on that front, so we can't draw conclusions either way. It does mean the biggest predictors were tied to income, living conditions, and access to care and infrastructure.

## Why Fluoridated Water Still Deserves Attention

Of everything on the list, water is the one you can check in about five minutes. Access to fluoridated water showed up as a meaningful factor in the findings. If you don't know whether your tap water is fluoridated, your local water utility can tell you, and many publish the information on their website or in an annual water quality report.

If your water isn't fluoridated, ask your dentist what they recommend for your situation. Don't start supplementing on your own. Fluoride products have recommended amounts that vary by age.

## Turn the Findings Into a Food and Drink Checklist

The study itself didn't analyze diet in the material reported, so treat this section as practical common sense rather than a study result. Still, if you want to lower your own cavity risk, your kitchen is the place you control most.

### Make water your default drink

If your tap water is fluoridated, drinking it throughout the day gives your teeth regular exposure. Keep a bottle on your desk and refill it rather than reaching for something sweet.

### Watch the sipping, not just the sugar

Sugary or acidic drinks sipped slowly over hours keep your mouth in a constant state of attack. If you enjoy juice, soda, or a sweet coffee drink, have it with a meal and finish it, rather than nursing it all afternoon.

### Build snacks around crunchy, low-sugar foods

Raw vegetables, cheese, nuts, and plain yogurt make easy swaps for sticky candy and crackers that cling to teeth. Pair them with a glass of water.

### Rinse when you can't brush

After a sweet or acidic meal, a quick swish of water helps clear leftovers until you can brush.

## The Access Gap: Book the Visit

Recent dental visits and dental insurance both appeared among the strong signals. That points to a simple, boring truth: regular checkups catch problems early, and not everyone can get them.

If you can, schedule your next cleaning rather than waiting for a toothache. If cost is the barrier, community health centers and dental school clinics often offer lower-cost care in many areas. Call and ask what's available near you.

## What AI Could Change in Dentistry

The appeal of this kind of model is a shift from reactive to targeted prevention. Instead of treating cavities after they form, health systems could use risk mapping to direct resources, such as fluoride programs and outreach, toward the communities most likely to need them.

That's the promise, and it's still a promise. This was one analysis of one country's survey, and a prediction model identifies patterns rather than proving cause. Still, it adds to the case that cavity risk is shaped by far more than personal discipline.

## Your Move

Check whether your tap water is fluoridated this week, make water your main drink, and book the dental visit you've been putting off. Those three steps cover the biggest levers the research highlights that you can actually pull.
