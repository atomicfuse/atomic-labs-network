Ask a Pilates instructor to pick a single move to do for the rest of her life, and you might expect something dramatic. Nicole Hernandez's answer, as reported by Fitandwell, is the pelvic curl. It's simple, it needs no equipment, and by her account it brings together spinal mobility with glute, hamstring, and core strength in one movement.

Here's how to make it work for you at home: what it's meant to do, how to check your form, and how to make it easier or harder.

## Why the Pelvic Curl Earns a Lifetime Spot

Per Fitandwell, Hernandez's case rests on efficiency. One slow movement asks your spine to move, one vertebra at a time, while your glutes, hamstrings, and core do the work. That's a lot of payoff for a move you can do on a mat in your living room.

That fits a broader appeal many people feel right now: low-impact workouts that are accessible and sustainable. If you want a routine you'll actually keep doing, a "minimum effective dose" move like this is a good place to start. (That framing is our read on the trend, not a claim from Hernandez.)

## How to Do a Pelvic Curl

The source summary doesn't spell out step-by-step cues, so what follows is a general description of the standard version of this exercise. Treat it as a starting point, and consider a class or instructor for personal feedback.

1. **Set up.** Lie on your back with knees bent and feet on the floor, roughly hip-width apart. Arms rest by your sides.
2. **Settle your pelvis.** Breathe in to prepare.
3. **Curl up.** As you exhale, tilt your pelvis and peel your spine off the mat slowly, one segment at a time, until your hips are lifted.
4. **Curl down.** Lower with the same control, rolling back down through the spine until your pelvis returns to the mat.

The word to remember is *slow*. The spinal mobility benefit comes from articulating through the back, not from launching your hips upward.

## A Quick Form Check

Use these self-checks, which are general technique guidance rather than quotes from Hernandez:

- **Feel it in the right places.** The work should show up in your glutes, hamstrings, and core. If your lower back is doing most of the effort, ease the height and slow down.
- **Move in a sequence.** If you lift in one rigid piece, like a plank tipping up, you're skipping the spinal mobility part. Imagine unpeeling tape from the mat.
- **Keep your feet steady.** Notice whether your feet grip evenly or roll to the edges.
- **Control the way down.** Lowering tends to be rushed. Make it as deliberate as the way up.

A useful before-and-after cue: before, your back may feel stiff and your glutes quiet. After a few controlled reps, you should feel a gentle warmth through the backs of your legs and hips and a sense that your spine moved more freely.

## Three Ways to Adjust It

These are common-sense progressions and regressions for the same movement, not Hernandez's prescriptions.

### Make it easier
Reduce the range. Lift only as high as you can while keeping the movement smooth, and build height over time. Fewer, slower reps beat many sloppy ones.

### Keep it as is, but add pauses
Hold briefly at the top and again partway down. Pauses make you control the movement rather than rely on momentum.

### Make it harder
Shift your weight toward one foot or slow the tempo further. Any change that challenges balance and control without compromising your spine's smooth motion keeps the move honest.

## Fit It Into Your Week

Because the pelvic curl needs only a mat, it slots easily into a morning routine, a warm-up before a walk, or a short break from desk work. Start with a handful of slow reps, notice how your back and hips feel, and increase gradually. If you have back pain or a medical condition, check with a healthcare professional before adding new exercises.

## The Takeaway

Pick one quiet five-minute window this week, lie down, and do a few pelvic curls as slowly as you can. If a seasoned instructor would keep only one move for life, it's worth finding out what it feels like in your own body.
