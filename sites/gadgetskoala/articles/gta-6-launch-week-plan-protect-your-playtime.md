Painter and decorator Sean Little has booked a week off work to play the new Grand Theft Auto game. According to the BBC, he has also jokingly had his wife and daughter sign a "non-interference" contract so he can play uninterrupted.

It's funny, but there's a useful idea buried in it: if you want a clear run at a huge release, you need to settle the deal with your household before launch day, not during it. Here's how to do that without needing a lawyer.

## Why a Week Off Makes Sense to Fans Like Sean

Per the BBC's profile, Little is a long-time GTA superfan, and the release is framed as a major event for gamers who have waited 13 years since GTA 5. That context matters. When a game arrives once in a decade or so, wanting to experience it properly rather than in 40-minute scraps is a reasonable instinct.

Whether that counts as self-care is a fair debate, and I'd say it does, as long as the people around you aren't paying for it. Which brings us to the contract.

## Step 1: Pick Your Window and Say It Out Loud

The single most useful thing Little did was make the plan visible. He booked the time off and told his family. You can copy that:

- Decide how many days you actually want to play. Be honest rather than ambitious.
- Request the time off early, if you need it.
- Tell everyone at home the dates, in writing if you like. A note on the fridge counts.

Surprises are where resentment starts. A clearly announced week is just a schedule.

## Step 2: Write Your Own Mock Contract

The "non-interference" contract is a joke, per the BBC, but the format works because it makes everyone laugh while setting expectations. Keep yours short. A simple template:

1. **Dates:** The launch week, start to finish.
2. **Play hours:** The stretches you're claiming, for example evenings or a set block each day.
3. **Exceptions:** What's allowed to interrupt you. Emergencies, meals, school pickups.
4. **What you owe:** The part most people skip.
5. **Signatures:** Everyone signs. Silly pens encouraged.

That fourth point is what turns it from a gag into a fair trade.

## Step 3: Make It a Two-Way Deal

A contract where only one person gets something won't last past day two. Offer something back. Ideas:

- Take on a chore or two the week before, so the household is ahead.
- Plan a family outing for the weekend after launch week.
- Agree that the console or PC is off during dinner.
- Offer a swap: your gaming week now, their equivalent protected time later.

Think about which chores you'd happily pay to dodge, and which your family would. Trading those is the easiest way to buy goodwill.

## Step 4: Prep the Practical Stuff Before Launch

None of this is in the BBC piece, so treat it as general common sense rather than anything Little did. The less you have to sort out mid-week, the less likely you are to break your own contract:

- **Storage and downloads:** Clear space and pre-load if the platform allows it.
- **Controllers and headsets:** Charge them and keep spare batteries or a cable nearby.
- **Food and drink:** Stock easy meals so you aren't raiding the kitchen at awkward moments.
- **Notifications:** Mute work email and group chats, so the week off is actually off.

## Step 5: Know When to Pause

A week of heavy playing is a lot. Build in breaks: stand up, step outside, eat a proper meal with your family. The contract protects your playtime, but it shouldn't make you disappear. If you're a parent, a short stretch each day spent offline together keeps goodwill high and makes the whole arrangement sustainable.

## The Bigger Picture

What makes Little's story land isn't really the contract. It's that he's an adult unapologetically taking a hobby seriously, and doing it with humor and his family's buy-in. That's a healthier model than sneaking in sessions and hoping nobody notices.

It also reflects how major releases now work. A big game launch can feel like a blockbuster film opening, a shared moment people plan around. Wanting to be part of it is normal.

So here's the takeaway: before launch day, sit down with your household, agree the dates, and offer something in return. Whether you actually print a contract is up to you, but the conversation is the part that protects your week.
