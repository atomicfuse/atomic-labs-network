If you've ever been told games rot your brain, here's a counterpoint worth knowing about. An opinion piece in the Pepperdine Graphic, written by Perspectives Staff Writer Abby Samuel, argues that video games may help counter neurocognitive decline, including in people already experiencing early memory loss. This is coverage of an argument, not a hands-on review or a medical guide. Below: what the piece says, what it means for what you play, and how to keep it healthy.

## What the piece argues

Per the Pepperdine Graphic, the column pushes back on the idea that all electronic entertainment harms the brain equally. Samuel's case is that video games stand apart from other screen habits, and that they can improve brain function in people already dealing with early memory loss.

The column cites studies that link gaming, 3D platformers and exergames to several outcomes: better memory, stronger executive function, gray matter growth and reduced dementia risk. We haven't reviewed those studies ourselves, so treat these as the column's claims about the research, not settled medical fact. It's also an opinion piece, so it's making an argument rather than offering a clinical review.

## The genres the column points to

Two types of games get specific attention, and both give you something concrete to try.

### 3D platformers

The column cites studies connecting 3D platformers to memory benefits. Think of games where you move through three-dimensional spaces, jump between platforms and keep track of where you are. That kind of play asks you to remember layouts and plan routes, which is a plausible reason it would exercise memory. That reasoning is our own read, not something we can attribute to the studies.

If you want to test the idea, a 3D platformer is an easy place to start. Pick one with a gentle difficulty curve so you're engaged but not frustrated.

### Exergames

Exergames combine physical movement with gameplay, and the column groups them among the formats tied to better brain outcomes. They're a good fit if you'd rather not sit still, and they work on a console or handheld setup that tracks your movement. Check which platforms a specific title supports before you buy, since availability varies.

## Why this conversation is happening now

As populations age, dementia concerns are rising, and gaming is increasingly discussed as something older adults might use, not just a youth pastime. The column also reflects a broader move toward evidence-based talk about screen time instead of blanket panic. That's a healthy shift whichever side you land on.

## How to use games without overdoing it

The column's claims are encouraging, but none of this means more hours always equals a sharper brain. Here's a practical way to approach it.

- **Pick games that make you think.** Look for ones that ask you to remember, plan and adapt, rather than games you can play on autopilot.
- **Keep sessions bounded.** Set a time limit before you start. Moderation is how you get benefits without sliding into unhealthy overuse.
- **Mix in movement.** If you're curious about exergames, try one alongside your usual play.
- **Play for enjoyment.** A game you actually like is one you'll keep up, and that's the real advantage of any routine.

## What this doesn't tell you

Games are not a treatment. If you or someone you know is noticing memory loss, a doctor is the right first stop. The column argues games may help people with early memory loss, but it's an opinion piece, and a few cited studies shouldn't replace medical advice. We also can't say how large any benefit is, how much play it takes, or which specific titles performed best, since those details aren't something we can confirm here.

## Your next move

The next time someone says games turn your brain to mush, you can point to an argument that says otherwise. Then test it yourself: pick one 3D platformer or exergame, set a time limit, and see how you feel after a few weeks of mindful play.
