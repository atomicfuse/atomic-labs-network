If you're thinking about taking a day off to play *GTA 6*, you've now got a cable news segment to argue with. Per HuffPost, Fox News host Harris Faulkner scolded Americans for taking time away from work to play the game, framing it as lost productivity. Here's what was reported, why it set off a backlash, and a simple way to decide for yourself.

**Coverage label: Gaming News.** This is based on HuffPost's reporting, not hands-on play. We haven't played the game, and this piece makes no claims about its release date, platforms, or price. Check Rockstar's announcements or your platform's official storefront for those details before you plan anything.

## What was reported

According to HuffPost, Faulkner objected to the idea that American workers might skip their duties to stay home and play a video game for a day. HuffPost's account frames her complaint as one about lost productivity, and as hypocrisy given ongoing complaints about affordability. HuffPost's own take on the segment is pointed: it describes her demand as churlish and sarcastically casts workers as shirking duties to their "corporate overlords." That's the outlet's editorial slant, not a neutral summary.

HuffPost also reports a wave of backlash. Critics accused conservatives of reviving the old "lazy young people" and "video games are ruining society" talking points.

## Why a video game became a flashpoint

Per the HuffPost report, *GTA 6* has become a massive cultural event after a 13-year wait. That scale is why reactions to it land harder than a typical release. When millions of people are anticipating the same game, anything said about those players gets amplified.

The timing matters too. HuffPost ties the controversy to ongoing frustration over affordability, paid time off, corporate power, and conservatives' struggles with younger voters. Our read: when a host tells people to stop playing a game, the argument quickly becomes about who gets to decide how people spend their own time.

## The angle worth keeping: earned time off is yours

Setting the politics aside, the practical question for you is simple. If you have paid time off, using it for a game you've waited years for is a normal use of it. The backlash HuffPost describes centers on that same point: leave you've earned versus a productivity panic.

That's our analysis, not a reported fact, and your workplace may have its own rules. Here's a sensible way to handle it.

### Plan it like any other day off

1. **Confirm the release details first.** Get the date and your platform's availability from official sources, not from social posts.
2. **Request leave through normal channels.** Ask early, the way you would for any appointment or trip.
3. **Check your policy.** If your employer requires a reason, you don't need to be more specific than "personal day."
4. **Don't call in sick.** It's the easiest way to turn a harmless day off into a work problem.
5. **Protect the day.** Handle chores and errands beforehand so the time is actually yours.

### If you can't take the day

Plenty of people can't. A weekend, an evening after work, or a staggered plan across several days works fine. Nothing about a story like this requires you to burn leave.

## Is this a new kind of panic?

Critics quoted in HuffPost's coverage see a familiar pattern: blaming leisure habits, especially younger people's, for bigger economic frustrations. The "video games are ruining society" line is decades old, and HuffPost's coverage suggests it still gets a reaction. Whether it persuades anyone is another matter. HuffPost's framing is that the stance may backfire with younger voters, which is a view of the outlet and its sources rather than a settled fact.

## The takeaway

Decide how you'll play before launch, not after the commentary starts. Verify the date and platform through official channels, put in your time-off request early, and spend the day the way you planned. Your PTO is yours to use.
