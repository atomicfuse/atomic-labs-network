**Gaming News.** This is a report on another outlet's interview, not a hands-on piece. If you've ever assumed North Korea sits entirely outside global gaming culture, a recent installment of NK News' "Ask a North Korean" series offers a more complicated picture, and it's worth a few minutes of your time.

## What happened

NK News, which runs "Ask a North Korean" as a series of interviews with and columns by defectors, took on a reader question: do North Koreans play video games like *Grand Theft Auto*? The series features people who, per NK News, mostly left the DPRK within the last few years. Readers can submit their own questions by emailing ask@nknews.org with their first name and city of residence.

In this installment, a defector explains how video gaming evolved in the DPRK, from consoles to smartphones, and describes exposure to foreign games, *Grand Theft Auto* among them. We're summarizing NK News' framing here, so for the specifics of who played what and where, read the original interview.

## The main takeaway: players wanted fun, not messaging

The thread running through NK News' coverage is that entertainment often mattered more to players than propaganda did, even with government controls in place.

That's a useful correction to a common stereotype. The picture of a country that is simply digitally sealed off misses something familiar to anyone who games: people go where the fun is. A game's politics, origin or official approval can matter less to a player than whether it's engaging.

## Gaming didn't stand still

According to the defector's account as summarized by NK News, gaming in the DPRK moved from consoles to smartphones over time. If that sounds like the arc in most of the world, that's the point. The hardware shifted toward the phone in your pocket, and the habits of players shifted with it.

This is also why the topic keeps drawing interest. NK News frames smartphones, gaming centers and foreign media as things that continue to complicate state information control. A game is a small thing, but it travels the same routes as other media.

## Why *Grand Theft Auto* is a revealing example

It's striking that the question centers on *Grand Theft Auto*, a series built around the look and feel of an open, consumer-driven society. Our read, and it is only our read: a game like that can offer a glimpse of how a different world looks and sounds, even through a fictional lens. NK News' piece positions GTA as one example of foreign games reaching players, not as a measure of how widely it's played, and we can't say more than that.

## What to take from it

- **Gaming is a human constant.** Even in one of the world's most restricted societies, players found games and made them part of their lives, according to the defector's account.
- **Technology shifts habits.** The move from consoles to smartphones is part of this story, not a footnote.
- **Entertainment can outpace control.** Per NK News, foreign media and gaming spaces complicate the state's grip on information.

## What we don't know

The source material we have doesn't cover which specific consoles or phones were common, how games were obtained, or how often *Grand Theft Auto* was played. We aren't going to guess at any of that. A single defector's account is also one person's experience, not a survey of a whole country, so treat it as a window rather than a census.
