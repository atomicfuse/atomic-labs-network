## What the argument is, and what it isn't

Per the video's title and description, Asmongold is reacting to someone else's argument that GTA 6 discourse says something about gaming media as a whole. That is commentary, not reporting. A reaction video is one person's take on another person's take.

That doesn't make it worthless. Reaction content often captures what a big part of the audience is feeling. But a feeling about critics is not evidence about any specific critic. If someone says critics have been inconsistent, the useful follow-up is: which critics, which games, which scores, and compared to what?

## Step 1: Check what the writer has actually played

Before a game is out, there is a ladder of coverage, and each rung deserves a different level of trust:

- **First Look:** based on trailers or announcements. Good for knowing what's been shown, not how it plays.
- **Preview:** early hands-on impressions, usually in a controlled setting.
- **Review:** based on finished or near-finished play, ideally with disclosure about how the copy was obtained.

If a piece about GTA 6 is making sweeping claims about quality, ask which rung it's standing on. Trailer-based opinions can be fun, but they can't tell you how a game handles over 30 hours.

## Step 2: Separate the critic from the crowd

The thread in the video, as described, is a gap between professional critics and the people who play games. That gap is real in some cases and imagined in others. Two things get blended together all the time:

1. **Disagreement.** A critic scores a game lower than you would. That's normal. Taste varies.
2. **Bad faith.** A critic is allegedly pushing an agenda. That's a serious accusation and needs specifics.

Treat the first as a reason to find a critic whose tastes match yours. Treat the second as unproven until someone shows the receipts, not just a spicy video title.

## Step 3: Discount for hype in both directions

Huge anticipation distorts coverage. Excitement can inflate praise, and backlash to excitement can produce harsher takes than a game deserves. Games like Cyberpunk 2077 and Starfield are often cited in these debates as examples of big launches that split opinion, though we aren't relying on a source here for the details of how either was received, so look up the actual reviews and player feedback yourself rather than leaning on anyone's summary.

A useful habit: when a take feels very certain about a game nobody has finished, apply a mental discount. The more certain the tone, the more evidence you should want.

## Step 4: Build your own panel of sources

Instead of trusting or distrusting "critics" as a bloc, pick three to five voices and track them over time:

- One outlet with written reviews that explain scoring.
- One or two critics whose past recommendations matched your experience.
- Player impressions from the platform you use (PC, console, or handheld), since performance can differ.

When all three types agree, you can be fairly confident. When they split, that's your signal to wait a week after launch and read more.

## Step 5: Decide before the launch-day rush

The cheapest protection against hype is a rule you set in advance. Try this:

- Wait for reviews and player reports on your platform before pre-ordering.
- Check refund policies on your storefront.
- Confirm the edition and price on the official store page, not a thumbnail or a clip.
- Set a hard budget, including any season passes or add-ons.

## Is the trust gap real?

Some of it likely is. Plenty of players feel that scores don't reflect what they enjoy, and that feeling is why reaction channels like Asmongold's find an audience. But "exposed every game critic" is a headline-sized conclusion, and our read is that a single reaction video can't carry that weight. It's an opinion about patterns, and patterns need examples.

The healthiest takeaway isn't to pick a side between critics and the crowd. It's to stop outsourcing the decision. Critics give you information, players give you information, and trailers give you a sales pitch. You weigh them.

## Your move

When GTA 6 coverage floods your feed, ask one question of every take: what has this person actually played? Then wait for the answer before you spend a cent.
