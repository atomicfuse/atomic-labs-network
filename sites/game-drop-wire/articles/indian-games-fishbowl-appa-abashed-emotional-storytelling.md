*First Look: this piece is based on reporting from the Hindustan Times, not our own hands-on play. We haven't verified platforms, prices or release dates for these games, so check the official storefront page for your region before you buy.*

If you want a game that treats grief as the main event rather than a plot twist, a small group of Indian indie developers is making them. Per the Hindustan Times, titles like *Fishbowl*, *Appa* and *Abashed* tackle loss, trauma and mental health, and the paper describes them as a gut punch that also helps players cope. Here is what the reporting tells us, and how to decide whether they're right for you.

## What these games are about

The Hindustan Times sums up the premises in a single line: a dead father, a missing partner, a grandmother you miss. These are personal, everyday losses, not world-ending stakes. The paper says the games use storytelling and gameplay mechanics to help players process loss and psychological struggles, and describes the experience as cathartic and healing.

That is the outlet's characterization, not a clinical claim. None of this suggests a game replaces professional support. Treat these as stories that may give you room to feel something, not as treatment.

## How the games are built to carry emotion

Per the report, the design tools include mood meters, daily check-ins and puzzle-solving. We can't tell you which game uses which, and we haven't played them. But the list says something useful about what to expect.

- **Mood meters and daily check-ins** suggest a slower, reflective rhythm. These sound closer to checking in with yourself than to clearing a level.
- **Puzzle-solving** suggests you're asked to do something with your hands while the story unfolds, rather than just reading.

If you usually play for speed and reflexes, expect a different pace.

## Why they stand out in India's market

The Hindustan Times frames these games against a market long dominated by FPS and action titles. Story-driven games, it reports, are gaining legitimacy there, which it reads as a cultural shift toward more introspective play.

The path isn't smooth. According to the report, developers face resistance from Indian gamers and from publishers who are hesitant to fund games about death that don't center combat. The same report says developers are finding international audiences hungry for these narratives.

In our view, that gap is the practical hook for you. These are small projects built by people betting on a niche, and wherever you live, you're likely part of the audience they're hoping to reach.

## Is this the right time for you to play one?

This is our take, not the report's. Games built around grief can be a good fit if you:

- Like short, story-first experiences more than competitive or action-heavy play.
- Want a game that sits with sadness instead of rushing past it.
- Are in a steady enough place to engage with themes of death and loss.

They may be a poor fit if a recent loss is still raw and you'd rather not be reminded of it. There's no shame in waiting. Skim the store page description first, since it should tell you what the story covers.
