Both Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery step into the earnings spotlight this week, reporting second-quarter results while a legal fight over their planned $110 billion merger hangs unresolved. For anyone watching where Hollywood is headed — and that includes gamers who care about the entertainment companies funding big IP adaptations — the numbers deserve a closer look.

The story the brief doesn't immediately tell you is this: streaming subscriber counts are climbing at both companies, but that growth is paper-thin cover for what's happening underneath in cable television. The Q2 results will force both companies to answer whether their streaming businesses can actually carry the weight — and what that means for a merger that regulators are actively fighting.

## Streaming Is Growing, But Read the Fine Print

Paramount+ sits at 79.6 million subscribers. HBO Max has crossed 140 million. Those are real numbers, and neither company should be embarrassed by them. In raw subscriber terms, both platforms have built meaningful audiences.

But subscriber count and financial sustainability are different conversations. Linear TV — cable channels, traditional broadcast — still generates a substantial portion of both companies' revenue, and that business is shrinking. Every quarter, more households cut the cord. Ad revenue tied to traditional TV follows those viewers out the door.

The paradox here is sharp: Paramount and WBD are growing where it's culturally cool to grow (streaming), while slowly bleeding out where they historically made the most reliable money (cable). Q2 earnings will reveal just how wide that gap has gotten, and whether streaming revenue is growing fast enough to actually replace what linear TV is losing — or just growing fast enough to make the slide look more gradual.

Investors aren't patient people. That gap is exactly what's been weighing on both companies' stock prices.

## The Merger Math and Why Antitrust Complicates It

The $110 billion merger between Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery was supposed to be the answer to that problem. The logic is straightforward: combine catalogs, cut duplicate costs, and create a streaming platform with enough scale to compete with Netflix and Disney+. Two struggling giants becoming one more competitive one.

But antitrust challenges have slowed that calculation considerably, per the reporting from Deadline. Regulators examining the deal are raising questions about what a combined entity of this size means for competition in media — who controls what content, what it costs consumers, and how much leverage one company ends up holding over distribution.

The legal uncertainty has a direct effect on both stocks. When a merger's outcome is unclear, investors price in risk. Neither company can fully execute on the integration strategy while the legal cloud sits overhead, which creates an operational limbo that's genuinely bad for business.

Meanwhile, Comcast is reportedly exploring a spin-off of NBCUniversal — a separate but related sign that the entire legacy media industry is trying to figure out its next structural move. The Paramount-WBD situation isn't happening in a vacuum; it's part of an industry-wide reshuffling that doesn't have a clean ending yet.

## Studio Performance: A Mixed Bag Worth Noticing

Aside from the streaming and merger angles, the Q2 calls will also reflect how both companies' film studios actually performed at the box office — and the brief suggests it's an uneven picture.

Warner Bros.' *Supergirl* reportedly stumbled, which is notable given how much DC's cinematic rebuild has been positioned as a tent-pole priority under James Gunn's stewardship. One miss doesn't define a strategy, but it's a data point that will come up.

On the Paramount side, *Scary Movie* apparently landed as a success — a franchise revival that found its audience. That contrast between the two studios' recent film performance isn't just box office trivia. For a potential combined company, understanding whose creative pipeline is actually generating returns matters enormously when you're trying to argue a merger creates value rather than just cuts costs.

## One Year of Paramount-Skydance as Context

This week's earnings also land near the one-year mark of the Paramount-Skydance deal closing — a useful reminder of how quickly priorities shift in Hollywood. A year ago, the conversation was all about what Skydance's David Ellison would do with the keys to Paramount. Now the conversation is about whether Paramount survives as an independent entity at all or gets folded into WBD.

That's not a slow drift. That's the industry moving fast, under financial pressure, with no consensus on what the right structure looks like.

## What to Actually Watch for in the Earnings Calls

If you're following this story — whether as an investor, an industry observer, or someone who just cares about what happens to beloved IP — a few specific signals will tell you more than the headline numbers:

**Streaming revenue per subscriber, not just subscriber count.** Adding subscribers at a loss is a growth story that eventually runs out of road. The per-subscriber economics matter more than the raw totals.

**Cable ad revenue trajectory.** How fast is it declining? A managed descent is survivable. An accelerating one changes the math on the whole enterprise.

**Any merger timeline language.** Executives on earnings calls choose their words carefully. If they're confident about the antitrust process, you'll hear it. If they're hedging, that hedging is the story.

**Studio slate commentary.** Both companies need reliable theatrical performers to feed streaming libraries. How executives talk about their upcoming film slates — especially DC's next moves after the *Supergirl* stumble — signals whether creative recovery is on track.

The Q2 reports won't resolve the merger's legal fight. But they will draw a clearer picture of whether Paramount and WBD are strong enough to wait it out — or whether the financial pressure is quietly accelerating the need for a resolution, one way or another. Watch the numbers, but listen to the framing. That's where the real answer lives.
