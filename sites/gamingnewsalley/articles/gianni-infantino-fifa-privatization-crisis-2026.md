Just days before the 2026 World Cup final, Gianni Infantino looked like a man nobody could touch. Then, in roughly seven days, that image fell apart completely — and the fallout is still reshaping global football.

Here is what actually happened, what it reveals about how FIFA operates behind closed doors, and why the consequences extend well beyond one man's political future.

## The Plan That Started It All

According to reporting by the New York Times, Infantino had been advancing a scheme to sell a stake in FIFA competitions to private investors, with Joshua Kushner among those reportedly involved. The details of the arrangement were not publicly announced or put to a broad vote — they emerged through leaks and internal channels, which is precisely where the trouble began.

The core idea — bringing outside capital into FIFA's commercial structure, potentially including the World Cup itself — is not inherently outlandish. Private investment has reshaped other sports properties. But the *way* this was being handled set off alarm bells almost immediately.

## The Backlash Was Fast and Came From Multiple Directions

Within days of the plan becoming known, UEFA and Concacaf — two of FIFA's most powerful regional confederations — pushed back hard. This was not a quiet disagreement expressed through diplomatic channels. Per the New York Times report, it amounted to fierce backlash that qualified as open rebellion from major confederations.

That kind of coordinated resistance matters enormously in FIFA's political structure. The confederations are not simply regional administrators; they deliver the votes that elect the president. When UEFA and Concacaf signal displeasure simultaneously, a sitting president has a real problem.

The resistance was not limited to confederation leaders, either. Internal FIFA staff also pushed back, and the departures of key figures including Kevin Lamour and Carlos Cordeiro became visible markers of Infantino's growing isolation. Staff departures at that level do not happen quietly — they signal a credibility problem that goes beyond policy disagreement.

## Why the Privatization Scheme Hit a Nerve

To understand why this particular plan triggered such a strong reaction, it helps to think about what selling a stake in the World Cup would actually mean.

The World Cup is the financial engine that funds FIFA's entire development structure — the money that flows down to smaller national associations, youth programs, and football infrastructure in countries that will never host a major tournament. Any arrangement that redirected a share of that revenue to private investors would, in effect, take from that pool.

For smaller member associations, that is not an abstract concern. It is the difference between having resources and not having them. For UEFA — which runs its own highly profitable competitions and has long been wary of FIFA overreach — it represented exactly the kind of unilateral move that makes Infantino difficult to work with.

The secrecy around the deal compounded the problem. Governance failures in football have a long history, and FIFA's own past is not exactly clean. When a major commercial restructuring is advanced without transparent process, the instinct among member associations is to assume the worst about who benefits and who loses.

## From Invincible to Vulnerable: The Political Math Has Shifted

Before this episode, Infantino's path to re-election in 2027 looked straightforward. He had accumulated support across confederations, particularly in Africa and parts of Asia, and there was no obvious challenger with the standing to mount a serious campaign.

That calculus has changed. Per the New York Times reporting, there is now active discussion about a unity candidate to replace him, with Concacaf president Victor Montagliani among those being positioned as a potential challenger. The fact that this conversation is happening openly — rather than in whispers — is itself a measure of how quickly the ground has shifted.

This is how FIFA power transitions have historically worked: not through sudden coups, but through the gradual withdrawal of support from confederations that previously stayed neutral or broadly supportive. Once that process starts, it is difficult to reverse.

## What Infantino Does Next Is the Real Story

The collapse of the privatization plan is the immediate news. But the more consequential question is whether Infantino can rebuild the coalition he needs to remain in office past 2027 — and what he might offer or concede to do it.

His options are constrained. Walking back the privatization scheme is already done; that deal is finished. But the confederations that pushed back are going to want more than a retreat on a single proposal. They will want structural assurances about how future commercial decisions are made, and they will want to see those assurances in a form they can point to.

The challenge for Infantino is that his governing style — centralized, relationship-driven, resistant to institutional constraint — is precisely what made this crisis possible in the first place. Changing that style credibly, while also managing a re-election campaign, is a genuinely difficult needle to thread.

## What This Means for the Sport's Commercial Future

FIFA's commercial model is not going away, and the underlying question the privatization plan was trying to address — how to maximize and distribute the value of football's biggest property — remains live. The failure of this specific scheme does not resolve that question; it just means the next attempt will have to be structured very differently.

For fans, the practical concern is straightforward: any commercial arrangement that dilutes the revenue flowing back into football's development infrastructure ultimately weakens the sport at its base. The World Cup's value is inseparable from football being genuinely global — and that globalism requires sustained investment in places where the game is still growing.

Whoever runs FIFA after 2027, that tension will still be sitting on their desk on day one.
