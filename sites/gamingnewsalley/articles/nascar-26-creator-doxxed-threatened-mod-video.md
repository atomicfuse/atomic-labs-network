A creator who goes by Dustin, known online as DLBTV, says he was doxxed, threatened, and harassed after posting videos related to NASCAR 26 mods — and according to Dailydownforce, a police report has been filed.

Whatever you think about mod coverage, public videos, or niche racing game drama, nothing about any of it leads to a legitimate outcome where someone's home address gets posted online. That's where this one ended up.

## What Dustin Says Happened

Per reporting from Dailydownforce, Dustin's address and phone number were first posted inside his own Discord server before spreading to other platforms. It didn't stop with him — one of his admins was also targeted, with threats that reportedly extended to that admin's family.

Dustin has confirmed a police report was filed. That's not a content creator escalating drama for views. That's someone who felt genuinely unsafe enough to involve law enforcement.

## The Mod Controversy Behind It

The harassment is connected to the NASCAR 26 modding scene, which has been running hot as the game expands its paid paint scheme DLC offerings. When you have a passionate fanbase accustomed to deep customization — and a game increasingly selling visual content at a price — friction between official DLC and fan-made mods was always going to build.

Dustin posted videos touching on NASCAR 26 mods, and some in the community blamed him when a particular mod method stopped working after a game update. The accusation: his coverage exposed the method, prompting the developers to patch it.

Dailydownforce pushes back on that conclusion directly, noting there's no clear evidence that Dustin's video actually caused the patch. Game updates patch things for all kinds of reasons. Blaming a specific creator for a developer's internal decision — without proof — is a stretch. Acting on that assumption with doxxing and threats is something else entirely.

## Where Gaming Communities Go Wrong

This situation is an example of a pattern that shows up across gaming communities with uncomfortable regularity. A creator covers something. A patch drops. Someone connects the dots in the worst possible way and decides that gives them license to retaliate.

The NASCAR 26 community is small and passionate, which is usually a strength. Fans who care that much about accuracy, paint schemes, and modding tools are the backbone of simulation-adjacent racing games. But tight communities can also turn fast when they feel like something they love has been threatened — and misdirected anger has a way of landing on the most visible target.

Dustin was visible. He had a Discord with an active community. That made him easy to find and, in the worst-case scenario, made his personal information accessible to people who shouldn't have had it.

## The Ethics of Covering Mod Tools

There's a real conversation to be had about how creators handle coverage of modding tools — particularly ones that exist in legal or community grey areas, or ones that depend on staying under the radar to function. If a mod method works because it's not widely known, a video reaching thousands of viewers does change the calculus.

That's a legitimate discussion. Creators navigating that space should think carefully about what they cover and how. But "think carefully about your coverage choices" is advice delivered through comment sections and direct messages, not through posting someone's home address.

Criticism of content is normal. Disagreement is normal. Even being loud and angry in a community space is, within limits, normal. Doxxing is a line that sits completely apart from all of that — it introduces real-world consequences and physical risk into what should be an entirely digital argument.

## Paid DLC and Mod Tensions Aren't Going Away

It's worth understanding the broader context here because it's going to keep producing friction. As NASCAR 26 sells paid paint schemes, fans who have historically relied on community mods for that kind of customization feel the squeeze. The free, fan-made version of something is getting harder to access right as the official paid version gets more prominent.

That tension is real and worth talking about. Publishers locking down modding capabilities while selling DLC that overlaps with what mods used to provide is a genuine point of criticism — of developers and publishers, not of individual creators covering the scene. Redirecting that frustration at someone like Dustin doesn't address the underlying issue. It just hurts a person who was, by most accounts, doing what gaming creators do: covering the game his audience cares about.

## A Police Report Changes the Conversation

The fact that law enforcement is now involved should signal to anyone still treating this as mod drama that it stopped being mod drama a while ago. Filing a police report is not a content play. It's a step people take when they're worried about what might happen next.

Dustin's admin being targeted — with threats involving that person's family — makes it clear this wasn't one bad actor sending one angry message. It spread, it escalated, and real people outside the original dispute got pulled into it.

If you're part of the NASCAR 26 community and you're frustrated about mods, patches, or creator coverage, that frustration has legitimate outlets. Write a detailed comment. Post your take. Have the argument in public. The moment you're looking up someone's address, you've left the conversation entirely and entered territory with serious legal consequences.

The mod method may or may not have been patched because of a video. That question is genuinely unresolved, per Dailydownforce's reporting. But Dustin's address being posted online is not unresolved — it happened, a report has been filed, and no patch controversy justifies it.
