Masahiro Sakurai, the creator of Smash Bros., has warned that AI could speed up the flood of games arriving on Steam, according to a report from Shattered. The reported expectation: annual Steam releases, already above 19,000 a year, will keep growing, and most AI-made games won't turn a profit.

That's the news. The more useful question is what it means for you as a player and as someone who might build a game. The angle worth committing to is this: when making a game gets easier, getting noticed becomes the hard part.

## What Sakurai Is Reportedly Warning About

Per Shattered's breakdown, Sakurai's concern isn't only that AI is a production tool. He frames it as a marketplace and attention problem. If AI accelerates output, the result could be more copycat, low-quality and commercially weak titles landing on a storefront that is already strained by record release volume.

Two points from the report matter most:

- Steam releases are already above 19,000 a year, and Sakurai expects that number to grow.
- Most AI games are expected to be unprofitable.

I'm only working from the reported summary here, so I won't put words in his mouth beyond that. But the shape of the argument is clear: more supply doesn't mean more audience.

## Why Discovery Is the Real Bottleneck

There are only so many hours in a player's week. A store can list 19,000 games a year, but your attention doesn't scale with the catalog. Every new release, good or bad, competes for the same finite pool of wishlists, reviews and recommendations.

This is why the AI debate and the Steam visibility debate overlap. AI doesn't create the discoverability problem, since Steam was crowded before. What it could do, going by Sakurai's reported warning, is make the crowd bigger and noisier.

My read, and it's an opinion rather than something from the report: the games most hurt by that noise won't be the obvious junk. They'll be the modest, handmade indie releases that were already fighting to be seen.

## What It Means for Indie Developers

If you make games, the reported warning points toward a few practical priorities.

### Originality becomes the edge

If copycats get cheaper to produce, a clone has less and less reason to exist. A distinct hook, art style or mechanic is something a flood of lookalikes can't easily dilute. This is the strongest competitive advantage in the scenario Sakurai describes.

### Don't assume shipping equals selling

The prediction that most AI games will be unprofitable is a reminder that finishing a game is only half the job. Budget real time for marketing, community building and wishlist growth, not just development.

### Quality signals matter more

When anyone can ship, players lean harder on trust: demos, clear trailers, honest store pages and early reviews. Those are things you can control today.

## Is This the Next "Content Slop" Debate?

AI-generated creative work is already a flashpoint across games, music, film and publishing, per the report. Games are following the same pattern: a new tool lowers the cost of making things, volume surges, and audiences start asking what's worth their time.

It's worth separating two arguments that often get mashed together. One is about how games are made. The other is about what happens to a marketplace when output explodes. Sakurai's reported warning leans toward the second, which is why it lands even with people who have mixed feelings about AI tools themselves.

## How Players Can Find Worthwhile Games Anyway

You don't have to wait for Steam to fix discovery. A few habits help cut through the volume:

1. **Wishlist selectively.** Treat your wishlist as a shortlist, not a bookmark pile. If you wouldn't buy it at launch, skip it.
2. **Play demos first.** A short demo tells you more than any trailer.
3. **Read the negative reviews.** They reveal recurring problems quickly, including a game that feels like a copy of something else.
4. **Follow curators and developers you trust.** A handful of reliable voices beats scrolling the new releases list.
5. **Check what makes it different.** If you can't say what a game does that others don't, that's a signal.

None of these are new, but they matter more as the catalog grows.

## The Bigger Picture

Sakurai's comments matter partly because of who is making them. As the creator of Smash Bros., he has spent a career thinking about what makes games feel polished and worth returning to. Seeing him frame AI as a storefront problem rather than a purely technical one shifts the conversation toward the questions players and developers face right now.

There's also a fair counterpoint: a prediction is not an outcome. The report describes an expectation that releases will grow and most AI games will struggle commercially. Whether that plays out is something Steam's numbers will show over time.

## The Takeaway

Whether you play or build, act as if attention is the scarce resource. Players: curate your own shortlist and trust demos over hype. Developers: give people a reason to remember your game, because in a market of 19,000-plus releases a year, being different is the only reliable way to be seen.
