Games have spent decades as the punching bag of screen-time debates. An opinion piece in the Pepperdine Graphic, written by Perspectives Staff Writer Abby Samuel, flips that script: it argues video games can improve brain function in people already experiencing early memory loss. Here is what the piece claims, how to read it sensibly, and how you can play with your brain in mind.

## What the Pepperdine Graphic is arguing

Samuel's piece opens from a familiar place: several forms of electronic entertainment are notorious for harming the brain, but video games, she argues, stand apart. Per the article, studies link gaming, 3D platformers and exergames to better memory, executive function, gray matter growth and reduced dementia risk.

This is an opinion column, not a clinical trial. I can only relay what the piece says it cites, and I haven't verified the underlying studies here. Treat it as a strong argument worth following up on, not medical advice.

## Why the timing makes sense

As populations age and dementia concerns rise, games are being reframed from a youth pastime into a possible cognitive wellness tool. It also fits a wider shift toward evidence-based conversations about screen time instead of blanket moral panic. Not all screens are the same, and the column's pitch is that games deserve their own line on that chart.

## The game types the piece points to

Three categories come up, and each suggests a different way to play.

### 3D platformers

The column cites 3D platformers in connection with memory benefits. These games ask you to track space, remember routes and plan jumps. If you want to test the idea, a classic like Super Mario 64 is the kind of title this research conversation tends to center on. Note that the specific game is a natural example, not a prescription from the article.

### Exergames

Exergames combine movement with play. The column groups them with the games tied to brain benefits. The appeal is obvious: you get physical activity and mental engagement in one session, which is a good fit if sitting still for long stretches isn't your thing.

### Games in general

The piece also cites links between gaming broadly and better executive function, the mental toolkit for planning, switching tasks and focusing. Even if you never touch a platformer, games that make you juggle goals and adapt on the fly may fit that description.

## How to play for your brain without overdoing it

The column's strength is its reframing, but the practical side is on you. Some sensible ways to apply it:

- **Pick games that make you think.** Favor titles that demand memory, navigation or planning over ones you can play on autopilot.
- **Keep sessions bounded.** Set a timer. Healthy gaming means getting the benefits without sliding into the overuse critics worry about.
- **Mix in movement.** Try an exergame a few times a week alongside your usual sit-down play.
- **Play something new.** Learning fresh controls and maps asks more of you than replaying a game you know by heart. This is my suggestion, not a claim from the column.
- **Treat it as one habit among several.** Games aren't a substitute for medical care if you or someone you love notices memory changes.

## Starting points for older players

The column's audience includes older adults, and games can be a low-pressure entry point. A simple 3D platformer on a console you already own costs little to try. Pair it with a friend or grandchild and you add a social layer, which is a bonus I'd argue for, though the piece itself doesn't claim it.

## The caveat worth keeping

It's an opinion column making a case. The most responsible reading is that games look like a promising, enjoyable thing to include in a brain-healthy routine, not a cure. If memory loss is a real concern, talk to a doctor and bring the games along as a supplement.

The next time someone says games rot your brain, you now have a counterargument. Better yet, pick one thinking-heavy game this week, cap your session at an hour, and see how it feels.
