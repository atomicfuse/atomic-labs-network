Mathematics is having an identity crisis, and 2026 is the year it showed. According to a piece originally reported by Phys, frontier AI models from OpenAI and Anthropic have made what it calls stunning progress on big open math problems, with alleged breakthroughs on heavyweights like Navier-Stokes and the Riemann hypothesis. Those claims have provoked backlash from leading mathematicians.

Note the word "alleged." The coverage describes these as solved or claimed progress, and the fight over which is which is part of the story. But the deeper argument isn't really about whether any single proof holds up. It's about what happens to a field when the answers stop being the hard part.

## The homework, the lesson, and the gap between them

Here's the angle worth sitting with: AI may have solved the homework, but did anyone learn the lesson?

To outsiders, mathematics looks like a machine for producing true statements. Prove the theorem, collect the result, move on. The Phys-reported argument suggests that picture misses what mathematicians themselves value: understanding. A proof, in that view, isn't just a certificate stamped "correct." It's a story about why something is true, and the story is what lets the next person build something new.

That gives us three things that are easy to blur together:

- **Proof:** an argument that a statement is true.
- **Verification:** confirming the argument actually holds, which is where formal verification tools come in.
- **Understanding:** knowing why it holds, and what it opens up.

AI can plausibly deliver the first two at scale. Whether it delivers the third is the question haunting the field. If a model hands you a verified result that no human can intuit, you have an answer in the way a vending machine has a snack: satisfying, but you didn't learn to cook.

## From theory-building to results factory

The article's sharper claim concerns structure, not philosophy. It points to AI agents, formal verification and massive compute as a combination that could shift mathematics from human-led theory-building to industrial-scale result production.

It's hard not to hear echoes of how other fields changed once automation arrived: the craft becomes throughput. That comparison is my gloss, not the article's, but it captures the worry. Human mathematicians traditionally work slowly, build conceptual frameworks, and publish the fruits. An agent farm with a big compute budget and a formal checker doesn't need a framework. It needs a problem list and electricity.

Formal verification matters here because it addresses an obvious objection, namely that AI output can be confidently wrong. If a proof can be machine-checked, trust shifts from "do we believe the author?" to "does the checker pass it?" That's a real change in how the field might decide what counts as established.

## Why the pushback is so loud

The backlash from leading mathematicians, as described by Phys, isn't simply guild protectionism, though the stakes for the guild are real. The article frames this as more than AI assisting research. It may be reshaping the incentives, training pathways and culture of an entire scientific field.

That last phrase is the heart of it. Consider the incentives. Mathematicians are rewarded for solving problems and for the ideas that come with the solutions. If the famous problems fall to machines, what is the prestige currency? And consider training: Ph.D. students traditionally cut their teeth on hard, tractable problems. If AI is picking off breakthroughs, what exactly is the apprenticeship? It's fair to wonder whether a generation of students will learn the craft by watching a model do it.

The article's framing suggests this is where the crisis really lives: not in any one proof, but in the pipeline that produces mathematicians in the first place.

## A skeptic's checklist for the headlines

Because the claims are dramatic and some are disputed, a few habits help when you see "AI solves [famous problem]" scroll by:

1. **Check the verb.** Solved, claimed progress and assisted are different things, and the coverage itself separates them.
2. **Ask who verified it.** A formal, machine-checked proof carries different weight than an announcement.
3. **Look for the explanation.** Does anyone, human or otherwise, say why it works?
4. **Watch the reaction.** When leading mathematicians push back, the dispute is usually informative about what's actually at stake.

## The part no benchmark measures

Science enthusiasts tend to love a clean scoreboard: problem list, checkmarks, done. Mathematics has always been a little different, because the scoreboard was never the point. The point was the shared, human-legible understanding that made the next discovery possible.

If 2026 is the year AI got good at producing answers, the real test for the field is whether it can keep producing understanding, and whether it decides that still matters. A world with a thousand verified theorems nobody can explain would be a strange kind of progress: a very long answer key with no textbook.

Which is, in the end, the worry mathematicians seem to be voicing. Not that the homework got done, but that nobody stayed in class.
