Forbes Media chairman and editor Steve Forbes weighed in on whether college is losing its value, citing New York Fed data showing that 42% of recent college graduates are underemployed, per Fox News. His prescription: colleges should cut what he calls "garbage courses" and consider three-year degrees to bring tuition down.

That is a tidy two-part pitch, and it lands at a moment when rising tuition, student debt worries, and shaky early-career outcomes are putting higher education's return on investment under a microscope. Families are asking a question that used to feel almost rude: is the four-year degree still worth the price?

## The number doing the heavy lifting

The 42% figure is the headline, and it deserves a careful read. Underemployed, as the term is commonly used, generally means working in a job that doesn't require a degree. The summary of Forbes's remarks doesn't spell out how the New York Fed defines it or break the data down by major, so treat the number as a signal, not a verdict. It tells you something is off for a big slice of graduates. It doesn't tell you which slice.

That gap is the real lesson for anyone who likes data. An average hides a distribution. A single stat about "college graduates" lumps together very different degrees, schools, debt loads, and job markets. If you're a science-minded reader, your instinct should be to ask what's underneath the mean.

## Forbes's two fixes, tested like hypotheses

### Hypothesis one: trim the filler

Forbes argues colleges should cut "garbage courses." He frames it as a way to lower costs and, implicitly, to sharpen what a degree delivers. The reporting doesn't list which courses he means, so I won't guess on his behalf. But the underlying logic is easy to test on your own: if a course doesn't build a skill, a prerequisite, or a clear credential, you're paying tuition for it anyway.

The practical move is to pull the degree requirements for any program you're considering and sort each course into one of three buckets: core to the field, transferable skill, or elective filler. If the third pile is large, that's a cost you can price out.

### Hypothesis two: the three-year degree

The other proposal is to shorten the degree. Forbes suggests three-year programs as a way to lower tuition. The math is intuitive: fewer terms, fewer bills, and a year of earlier paychecks. It's hard not to see the appeal, especially for students already weighing debt against salary.

What the summary doesn't tell us is how such a program would handle labs, sequenced coursework, or internships. For science and engineering students in particular, those pieces are often where the learning (and the hiring pipeline) lives. A compressed degree that squeezes those out might save money and cost value. That's my read, not Forbes's, and it's the question I'd want any three-year program to answer up front.

## A quick ROI checklist for students and parents

The debate is more useful if you turn it into homework. Here are the questions worth asking before you commit to a school and major:

- **What do graduates of this specific program actually do?** Ask for placement data by major, not a campus-wide brag number.
- **How much debt will you carry versus the realistic starting pay in that field?** Put the two side by side.
- **How much of the degree is core versus filler?** Use the bucket test above.
- **Is there a shorter or cheaper route?** Ask whether the school offers an accelerated path.
- **Do you need a degree for the job you want?** Some careers do. Others may be reachable through alternatives like trade school, apprenticeships, or certifications.

That last item is where the conversation gets honest. A degree is one tool, not a default setting. For some careers it's essential; for others it may be an expensive detour. The point of the exercise is to know which kind you're buying.

## The take

Forbes is a business-media veteran making a business argument: treat a degree like a product and ask what you get for the price. You can agree or not with his specific fixes, but the framing is useful. A 42% underemployment figure is a prompt to run the numbers, not a reason to panic or to wave the stat away.

The most scientific response is the least dramatic one. Don't ask "is college worth it?" in the abstract. Ask whether this program, at this price, for this career, clears the bar. If the answer is yes, great. If nobody can show you the data, that silence is the data.
