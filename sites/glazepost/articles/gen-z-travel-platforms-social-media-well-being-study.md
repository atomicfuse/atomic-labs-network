A new study of 210 Gen Z users in Greater Jakarta suggests that social media communication on online travel platforms can improve customer well-being, as long as it encourages participation, value co-creation, and positive emotional experiences. The research was reported by Phys, and it lands on a point anyone who has fallen down a late-night "hidden gems of Lisbon" rabbit hole already suspects: how a platform makes you feel may matter as much as whether you click.

## The Finding, Plainly

Per Phys, the study looked at social media communication on travel platforms and its link to customer well-being among Generation Z users. The takeaway: well-being benefits show up when that communication drives people to take part, to help shape the content (the "value co-creation" part), and to feel good while doing it.

Two caveats. This is a study of 210 people in one region, Greater Jakarta, so it's a focused snapshot, not a verdict on every Gen Z traveler on Earth. And the summary available here doesn't spell out the study's methods or specific numbers, so I won't invent any.

## Why This Is Getting Attention

Travel platforms increasingly work like social networks. Per the reporting, Gen Z users lean on reviews, comments, recommendations, and shared experiences before they book anything. That's not a booking engine in the old sense. That's a comment section with a boarding pass.

The study's framing shifts attention away from engagement metrics like clicks and toward the emotional quality of digital travel interactions. It's hard not to read that as a gentle jab at the attention economy's favorite scoreboard. A click tells you someone showed up. It doesn't tell you whether they left feeling excited or vaguely behind on life.

## The Angle: Emotional Quality Beats Click Count

Here's my take, and it's labeled as such: the most interesting part of this research is what it implies about the difference between engagement and *good* engagement.

Anyone online in the last decade knows the feeling of scrolling something designed to keep you scrolling. Travel content has a particular version of this: the perfectly lit infinity pool, the itinerary that makes your long weekend look like a failure of imagination. The study points toward a different model, where users participate, contribute, and feel positively connected.

### Participation is the point

The "value co-creation" language is academic-speak for something you've seen a thousand times: users posting reviews, answering questions, sharing tips, and building the platform's content themselves. Think of the traveler who leaves a detailed note that the "ten-minute walk" to the beach is actually uphill. That's co-creation, and per the study's framing, it's part of the route to feeling better about the whole experience.

### Informed, excited, connected

The takeaway for brands and creators, adapting the study's spirit, is to make users feel informed, excited, and emotionally connected rather than overwhelmed or pressured. Anyone who has been told a room is "almost sold out!" at 2 a.m. will recognize the pressure side of that equation. It's a fair guess, though only a guess, that urgency banners and countdown timers sit on the wrong side of this finding. The study as reported doesn't test them.

## What It Means for the Trip-Planning Era

The broader link the research highlights is between trip planning, community, and well-being. Planning a trip has always been part of the fun, and now it often happens in public: saving posts, swapping recommendations, reading strangers' honest reviews. If that process feels communal and encouraging, the study suggests it can be good for you before you've even packed.

For influencers, the implication is that the "look at my flawless itinerary" format may be less valuable than content inviting people in. For travel apps, it's a nudge to treat comment threads and user reviews as features that shape how people feel, not just filler between the booking buttons.

## The Bottom Line

A study of 210 young travelers won't rewrite the rules of the internet. But it puts a number-adjacent spotlight on something fans of online culture have long suspected: the platforms that make us feel something good tend to be the ones we actually want to come back to. A trip, it turns out, can start improving your mood at the "saved to wishlist" stage, provided nobody is screaming that only two rooms are left.
