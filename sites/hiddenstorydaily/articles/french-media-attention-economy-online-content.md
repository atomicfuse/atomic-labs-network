## The Case File: What Politico Describes

Per Politico, the episode shows how a fringe narrative from France was amplified by Owens and turned into a lucrative attention engine. The outlet frames it as an example of how fringe ideas can become monetized political media products.

That framing is the key. The story is less about whether a theory is true and more about what happens to a theory once someone builds a business around it.

## Why a Theory Travels Better Than a Fact

A foreign conspiracy theory has a built-in advantage for an audience that cannot easily check it. Few viewers in one country follow another country's local press, courts or politics closely. That gap is hard to fill quickly, and mystery tends to fill it first.

Our read: distance adds intrigue. A story that sounds hidden, foreign and suppressed feels like a secret you have been let in on. Politico's framing suggests that this appeal is exactly what gets turned into content.

## The Incentive Problem

The attention economy rewards outrage and suspicion. Creators, platforms and media figures all compete for the same limited audience attention, and a narrative that keeps people watching, sharing and arguing is valuable whether or not it is correct.

That is the uncomfortable part of the "gold mine" idea. A conspiracy theory does not end when it is answered. Every rebuttal can become new material, and every new twist gives the audience a reason to come back.

As analysis rather than reported fact: when a story pays more the longer it stays unresolved, you should ask what rewards resolution.

## Skepticism, Commentary or Misinformation?

These three get blurred constantly. Here is a way to separate them.

### Skepticism

Healthy skepticism asks for evidence and is willing to change its mind. It can end with "this checks out" or "this does not."

### Commentary

Commentary offers opinion on events that others have established. It is clearly labeled as opinion and does not pretend to be investigation.

### Misinformation

This presents unsupported claims as likely or proven, often through insinuation and "just asking questions," and does not retreat when contradicted.

## A Five-Step Check Before You Share

1. **Find the original claim.** Who first said it, and where? Trace it past the clip or screenshot.
2. **Look for local reporting.** If it began in another country, search for coverage from that country's own outlets, even through translation tools.
3. **Notice what is for sale.** Check for subscriptions, merchandise, sponsors or memberships tied to the content.
4. **Test for falsifiability.** Ask what evidence would change the creator's mind. If the answer is nothing, it is not skepticism.
5. **Wait a day.** Outrage is time sensitive. Facts usually survive a night of sleep.

## What the Documentary Version Leaves Out

The dramatic telling of any conspiracy story features a hidden hand. The less cinematic reality in Politico's framing is a business model: attention in, revenue out. That does not require anyone to be a mastermind. It only requires an audience that rewards the next installment.

That is why these stories matter beyond any one personality. The same incentives shape influencer business models and online politics generally, and they erode audience trust over time.

Before you pass on the next explosive theory, ask one question: who profits if I keep watching? Then read Politico's full piece and judge the evidence for yourself.
