Overnight guests rarely remember your thread count. They remember the small things, like waking up and finding a hot cup of coffee within reach. This guide shows you how to build a simple coffee station in a guest room, using a compact single-serve machine as the centerpiece.

The timing is handy. Per Homes & Gardens, the compact Keurig K-Supreme Single Serve Coffee Maker is $45 off as part of Black Friday sales. That deal is short-lived, but the setup below works with any compact coffee maker you already own or choose to buy.

## Why a coffee station works so well

Homes & Gardens frames in-room coffee as a small but thoughtful upgrade that adds boutique-hotel comfort and convenience for holiday visitors. It's easy to see why. Guests don't have to hunt through your kitchen, wait for you to wake up, or ask for anything.

It also costs far less than a renovation. You are adding one small zone, not redoing a room.

## Step 1: Pick the right spot

You need a surface and an outlet. That's it. Good options:

- A dresser top, if it sits near a plug
- A narrow console or bar cart
- A small table or sturdy tray-topped nightstand
- A shelf in a corner with a wall outlet nearby

Keep the machine away from the edge of the surface and away from the bed. Make sure the cord doesn't stretch across a walkway.

## Step 2: Choose a compact machine

Space is the main constraint in most guest rooms, so size matters. The Keurig K-Supreme is described by Homes & Gardens as a compact, space-saving single-serve model, which suits a dresser or small table. Single-serve also means a guest makes one cup at a time, with no pot to clean or leftover coffee going stale.

Measure your chosen spot before buying. Leave a little room above the machine for lifting the lid and a bit of space beside it for mugs.

## Step 3: Corral everything on a tray

A tray is the quickest way to make a setup look intentional instead of cluttered. It defines the station and makes it easy to move or tidy.

A basic tray setup might include:

- The coffee maker
- Two mugs
- A small basket or bowl of pods
- Sugar and stirrers in a lidded jar
- A folded napkin or two

If you have a tray at home, use it. A thrifted wood or metal tray costs a few dollars and does the same job.

## Step 4: Add a few finishing touches

This is where the hotel feel comes from. Keep it light, though. Too many extras will crowd a small surface.

- **Matching mugs.** Two mugs in the same color look calm and considered.
- **A water bottle or small carafe.** Guests can refill the machine without a trip to the kitchen.
- **A small tray or dish** for used pods.
- **A handwritten card** with the Wi-Fi password and a note about how the machine works.

That last one is worth doing. A two-line instruction note means nobody feels awkward about figuring out your machine.

## Step 5: Think about light and color

Morning coffee is a morning ritual, so check how the spot looks at that hour. A dim corner feels gloomy at 7 a.m. If there's no natural light, a small lamp with a warm bulb makes the station inviting without being harsh.

Stick to a simple palette. If the room is neutral, choose white or gray mugs and a wood or black tray. If the room has a color, pull one shade into the mugs. Repeating a color is a cheap way to make a room feel pulled together.

## Step 6: Do a quick guest test

Before visitors arrive, sleep-test the setup yourself, or at least walk through it:

1. Can you reach the outlet without moving furniture?
2. Is it clear where to put a used pod and a dirty mug?
3. Is there enough surface space to set down a cup and a phone?
4. Is the machine filled and ready to go?

Fix whatever feels fussy. The best hotel touches are the ones guests don't have to think about.

## Does the station have to be a Keurig?

No. The idea works with any compact coffee maker, a kettle with instant coffee and tea, or a pour-over setup if your guests are into that. The Keurig is simply the example in the current deal. If you already own a machine you rarely use, moving it to the guest room costs nothing.

If you're deciding whether to buy, ask yourself how often you host. For a few visits a year, a sale price on a small machine can be a reasonable way to add comfort. If you'd rarely use it, a kettle and a few tea bags is a cheaper start.

## Other small luxuries worth adding

Once the coffee station is in place, a few other low-cost extras keep the hotel theme going:

- A phone charger by the bed
- An extra blanket folded at the foot of the bed
- A small dish for jewelry or keys
- A few bottles of water

None of these need a big budget. Together with the coffee station, they tell guests you planned for their visit.

## The takeaway

Before your next guests arrive, clear one dresser top, grab a tray, and set up a single cup, a few pods, and a note. That small corner will do more to make your guest room feel welcoming than almost anything else you could buy this weekend.
