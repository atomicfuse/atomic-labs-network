Got a room or a whole layout that never quite works? A Mumbai apartment recently featured by Architectural Digest shows that the odd shape you're fighting can become the best thing about your home. You can borrow the thinking without a 4,000-square-foot budget.

The home, designed by Studio PKA, began as two apartments joined into one, bought during the pandemic. The result was a floor plan shaped like a horseshoe. It's now a calm, honey-toned home for a couple and their daughter, who regularly flies in from the US.

Your home is almost certainly smaller. The lessons still scale down.

## Lesson 1: Work with the curve instead of hiding it

Per the report, the architects embraced the horseshoe rather than trying to straighten it out. That's the core idea worth stealing.

At home, that might look like:

- **A long, narrow room:** treat it as two zones (a seating end and a reading or desk end) instead of pushing all the furniture against the walls.
- **A awkward corner or bump-out:** give it one clear job, like a chair and lamp, rather than leaving it empty.
- **An odd hallway:** hang art along it so it feels like a gallery, not leftover space.

The question to ask is not "how do I fix this?" but "what is this shape good for?"

## Lesson 2: Plan privacy for everyone who lives there

This home has to work for two generations, including a daughter who visits from abroad. Privacy was part of the plan from the start, per the profile.

You don't need a second apartment to apply this. Think about who needs what:

1. **List the people** who use the space, including regular guests.
2. **Note when they need quiet** (early calls, late nights, naps).
3. **Give each person one spot that's theirs,** even if it's a chair with a lamp and a small side table.
4. **Use furniture as dividers.** A bookshelf, a rug edge, or a screen can separate zones without construction.

A guest who has a defined corner and a place to put their things feels at home faster.

## Lesson 3: Pick one design language and repeat it

A big or oddly shaped home can feel like a collection of separate rooms. Keeping a single design language helps it read as one place.

The Mumbai home is described as calm and honey-toned. You can do the same with a short, repeatable recipe:

- **Choose 2 or 3 main colors** and let them show up in every room.
- **Pick one or two wood tones** and stick with them.
- **Repeat a material,** such as woven baskets, linen curtains, or brass handles.

This is also the cheapest way to make mismatched thrifted pieces look planned. A can of paint or a few matching knobs can tie them together.

## Lesson 4: Choose every object on purpose

Per Architectural Digest, the objects, artwork and finishes in this home were curated from scratch as part of the architecture, not added at the end. You probably can't commission everything, but you can adopt the habit.

Try this before you buy anything:

1. Decide the feeling you want (calm, cozy, bright).
2. Measure the spot and note what it needs to do.
3. Buy or thrift only what fits both.
4. Leave empty space until something truly right turns up.

Waiting for the right piece costs less than buying three wrong ones.

## Lesson 5: Give the shape a meaning

The horseshoe is traditionally seen as a symbol of good luck, and the profile leans into that idea. You don't need a literal symbol. But a home feels more personal when its layout or decor tells a small story, such as a favorite place, a travel memento, or a family heirloom given a prominent spot.

Pick one meaningful item per room and let it be the focal point.

## Try it this weekend

Walk through your home and find the one space that annoys you most, whether it's a long room, a odd nook or a dead-end hall. Name one job for it, put one lamp and one chair or piece of art in it, and see if the awkward shape starts to feel like a feature.
