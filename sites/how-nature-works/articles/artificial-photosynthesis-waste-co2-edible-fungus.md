Next time you're on a woodland walk, look at a rotting log. The pale threads and mushrooms there are fungi doing what they do best: eating whatever carbon they can find. Now researchers have taken that appetite and pointed it at something unexpected, which is waste carbon dioxide.

Per a report from Phys, a team at Washington University developed an artificial photosynthesis system that turns waste CO₂ into protein-rich, edible fungus in seven days. By the end of this piece, you'll know how the process works, how it compares with soy and beef, and what it tells you about the fungi you can spot close to home.

## The short answer: a catalyst makes food for the fungus

Plants use sunlight to turn CO₂ into sugars. This system mimics that idea without a leaf. According to Phys, the researchers used a tailored copper-gold catalyst to convert CO₂ into feedstocks that a fungus can eat. Then the fungus, *Neurospora intermedia*, grows on them and produces protein-rich biomass.

Think of it as a two-step relay. Step one is chemistry: the catalyst transforms CO₂ into simple compounds. Step two is biology: the fungus eats those compounds and grows. The whole cycle takes seven days, per the report.

The source describes the catalyst as tailored, meaning it was designed to produce feedstocks the fungus finds friendly. That matching of chemistry to biology is the point of the work.

## Why a fungus, and why this one?

Fungi are not plants, and they are not animals. They don't photosynthesize. Instead, they absorb nutrients from their surroundings, which is why you find them on logs, leaf litter, and soil. That flexibility makes them natural candidates for eating a manufactured food source.

*Neurospora intermedia* is the species named in the research. Phys reports it as an edible fungus, and the result is a protein-rich product. I can't tell you from the report how it tastes or what the final product looks like, so treat any promise about flavor with caution until more details are published.

## How it compares with soy and beef

The appeal here is resources. Phys reports the process produces a sustainable protein using far less land and water than soy or beef. Those two foods are a useful comparison because both depend on farmland and, in beef's case, feed crops as well.

The report doesn't give exact figures in the material I have, so I won't invent any. The direction is what matters: a system that runs on CO₂ and electricity doesn't need a field or a pasture in the traditional sense.

## Why researchers are looking for options now

Per the coverage, global protein demand is rising fast. At the same time, agriculture faces pressure from climate change, water scarcity, emissions, and limited land. Those pressures pull in opposite directions, and that's the gap this kind of research aims at.

The larger idea is a future where food production is decoupled from traditional farming and powered by renewable electricity. That's a possibility, not a done deal. Turning a lab result into something on shelves involves questions of cost, scale, and whether people want to eat it, none of which the report settles.

## The question nobody can answer yet: will people eat it?

This is my read, not a finding from the research. Even a very efficient protein has to clear a simple hurdle: it has to be something a person wants for dinner. Fungus-based foods already exist in the world, but a powder or ingredient made from CO₂ will face the same test of taste, texture, and trust.

It's also unclear from the report how soon, or at what scale, this could leave the lab. Keep an eye on those questions rather than on the seven-day headline alone.

## What to notice in your own backyard

You can see the same principle at work without any equipment. Fungi are the planet's recyclers, and a few minutes outdoors will show you how they work.

- **Check a fallen log.** White or pale threads under the bark are fungal tissue feeding on dead wood.
- **Lift a patch of damp leaf litter.** Fine webbing running through the leaves is fungus breaking them down.
- **Look after rain.** Many mushrooms appear when moisture and food are both available, which shows how closely fungi track their food supply.

Don't eat anything you find. Wild mushroom identification takes real expertise, and some look-alikes are dangerous.

## The takeaway

This research takes a basic fungal talent, growing on whatever carbon is on offer, and feeds it with chemistry instead of dead wood. Next time you pass a mossy log full of pale threads, remember that the same hungry organisms are now being tested as a way to turn waste carbon into protein. Then go look closely at what's growing on that log.
