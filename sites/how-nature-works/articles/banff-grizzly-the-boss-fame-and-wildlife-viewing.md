Picture a line of cars pulled over along a mountain road, doors open, phones raised toward a distant shape moving through the trees. That scene plays out in Banff National Park in Alberta, Canada, and one bear is often the reason: a grizzly known as "the Boss," also tracked as Bear 122.

The short answer to why this matters: fame doesn't make a wild animal safer. Per a recent New York Times profile, Bear 122 has survived a train, cars and rival bears, and some people now fear the biggest threat to his life is his own celebrity. Here's what that means, and what you can do differently when you meet wildlife of any kind.

## Who is the Boss?

According to the New York Times, Bear 122 has become a local legend in Banff National Park. His reputation rests on survival. He has reportedly come through encounters with a train, with vehicles, and with other bears that were rivals for territory.

Those are the three big hazards for large animals in a busy mountain park: transportation corridors, roads, and competition from their own kind. Surviving all of them is what turned a numbered bear into a character with a nickname.

I can only report what the Times has laid out, so I won't guess at details of his age, range or injuries here. The core of the story is clear enough: he is a wild grizzly, and people have made him a star.

## Why fame can be a danger

The Times describes the central tension this way: the wilderness hasn't managed to take him down, but admiration might.

Think through how that can happen. When an animal becomes famous, more people go looking for him. More people means more cars slowing or stopping, more phones, and more people edging closer for a better shot. Wildlife managers often call a roadside crowd like this a "bear jam." It puts the animal and the people in the same small space.

This is my analysis, not a claim from the article, but the logic is simple. A bear that is regularly surrounded by people has less room to move, feed and rest on his own terms. And people who feel they know a bear from social media may underestimate how dangerous a grizzly is.

## What a bear jam looks like, and what to do instead

You don't have to be a bad actor to contribute to one. A single car stopping is the start. Then the next stops behind it.

If you see wildlife from the road in a national park:

- **Keep moving if you can't pull off safely.** Stopped traffic is a hazard for people and animals alike.
- **Stay in your vehicle** when a large animal is near the road. A car is a far better barrier than open ground.
- **Use zoom, not footsteps.** If you need a closer view, use binoculars or a long lens rather than approaching.
- **Follow park staff directions.** Rangers set distances and closures for good reasons.
- **Don't call out or try to get the animal's attention.** You want the bear to behave naturally and ignore you.

Check Parks Canada's current guidance for the minimum viewing distance for bears before you go. I'd rather you read the official number than rely on a figure from me.

## Don't share the location

Social media is a big part of how a bear becomes a celebrity. The Times frames this as part of a wider collision between wildlife tourism, online animal fandom and crowded national parks.

It's worth thinking before you post. A tagged location or a "he's here right now" caption can send a crowd to the same spot within the hour. If you want to share a photo, consider waiting until you've left, and skip the precise location.

## The legend versus the real animal

Communities love to build stories around iconic animals, and a nickname like "the Boss" is part of that. The stories are fun. They also tempt us to treat a bear as a mascot or a personality we know.

But Bear 122 is a wild grizzly. His survival says something about how tough he is, and also about how many dangers a park full of roads and rails creates. The conservation question isn't whether he's a good bear. It's whether the park can keep space around him so that he continues to act like a wild one.

## What to notice next time

When you're in a park and a crowd gathers, watch the people for a moment before you watch the animal. Look at where the cars are parked, how close anyone is standing, and whether the animal is changing direction because of them. If the crowd is steering the animal, you're too close, so back off.

The best way to honor a famous bear is to be a boring part of his day: stay far away, stay quiet, take your picture and leave.
