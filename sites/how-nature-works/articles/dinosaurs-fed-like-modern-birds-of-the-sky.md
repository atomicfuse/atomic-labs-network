Picture a hawk on a fence post with a meal pinned under its foot. It doesn't chomp down. It grips the food, hooks its beak into it, and pulls backward, tearing off strips. Now, according to a report from Phys on new experiments, some of the most famous meat-eating dinosaurs may have fed in a similar way: dragging their teeth through flesh like a saw instead of stabbing straight down like a dagger.

By the end of this article, you'll know what the experiments tested, why the answer hinges on tooth shape, and what to look for the next time you watch a bird of prey eat.

## The short answer: pull, don't punch

The study concerns theropods, the group of meat-eating dinosaurs that includes Allosaurus and Velociraptor. Per Phys, the experiments suggest these animals probably fed by pulling their curved, serrated teeth backward through flesh, much like a saw, rather than biting vertically.

Think of the difference between two kitchen moves. Pressing a steak knife straight down onto meat works poorly. Drawing it back and forth works far better. The researchers' results point to the dinosaurs doing the second thing.

## How the DinoSAW test worked

The team used a machine called DinoSAW to compare cutting styles. According to Phys, diagonal pull-cutting needed about one-third less force than vertical puncturing.

That is a meaningful saving. An animal that can slice through flesh with a third less force gets more meal for less effort, and it needs less brute strength behind each bite. This is our reading of the result rather than a claim from the report, but it's easy to see why such a feeding style could pay off over a lifetime of hunting.

## Why tooth shape hints at behavior

Teeth are one of the best clues paleontologists have, because they fossilize well and they're built for a job. Two features matter here, both noted in the Phys report:

- **Curvature.** The teeth of these theropods curve backward.
- **Serrations.** The edges are serrated, like a steak knife.

A smooth, straight spike suits stabbing and holding. A curved, serrated blade suits slicing as it moves through material. The experiments suggest the design matches the drag-and-saw motion, which is why the finding reframes how animals like Allosaurus and Velociraptor may have fed.

## The bird connection

Birds are living theropods' descendants, which is part of why this comparison feels natural. The report links the dinosaurs' likely feeding style to modern birds of prey and also to Komodo dragons.

The analogy isn't perfect. An eagle has a beak, not a mouth full of serrated teeth, and the report doesn't claim the mechanics are identical. What the comparison offers is a picture of the motion: grip, then pull and tear, rather than a single deep stab.

## What to notice next time

You don't need a fossil quarry to see this idea in action. A few things to watch for:

1. **Hawks and eagles at a meal.** If you're lucky enough to see a red-tailed hawk or similar raptor feeding, watch the head. It tends to pull backward and tear rather than crunch straight down.
2. **Where the force goes.** The bird usually braces the food with its feet and pulls against that grip. Holding the food steady while the head moves is part of the technique.
3. **Your own kitchen.** Try cutting a tomato with a serrated knife. Press down and it squashes. Draw it back and forth and it slices cleanly. That is the same physics the DinoSAW work was probing.

An easy way to feel the difference: press a steak knife straight down on a piece of cooked meat, then drag it. The dragging takes noticeably less effort.

## Why this matters beyond one dinosaur

Per Phys, the study also shows how engineering tools can test long-standing assumptions about extinct animals. We can't watch a dinosaur eat, but we can build a machine, measure the force, and see which motion makes sense for the teeth they left behind.

The finding is a suggestion, not a final verdict. The report says the experiments suggest dinosaurs *likely* fed this way, and the sensible reading is that it is a strong clue about behavior, not a video of it.

## The takeaway

Next time you see a bird of prey, or even just a serrated knife, watch the motion rather than the bite. Pulling is the efficient move, and it seems the dinosaurs may have known it first.
