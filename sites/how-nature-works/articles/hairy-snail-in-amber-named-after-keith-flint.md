Picture a garden snail on a damp leaf. Now picture one the size of a speck, its shell covered in fine hairs, stuck in a drop of tree resin while dinosaurs walked nearby. That snail is real, and it has just been given a name.

Researchers have identified a new terrestrial snail, about 99 million years old, preserved in Myanmar amber. They named it *Archaeospirostoma flinti* after Keith Flint, the late frontman of The Prodigy, because the hairs on its shell resemble his iconic hairstyle, per Phys, which reported the study.

The short answer to why a snail has hairs: nobody has pinned that down. But the discovery shows how much a small piece of amber can tell you about life in the age of the dinosaurs, and how scientists now read it without breaking it.

## What the snail is and where it came from

The fossil comes from Myanmar amber, often called "burmite." Per Phys, this amber dates to the Mesozoic Era, the age of the dinosaurs, and is one of the most important sources of information about biodiversity from that time.

The new species is a land snail, not a sea creature. That matters because soft-bodied land animals rarely fossilize well. Resin can capture small, delicate things that ordinary rock fossils usually lose.

## Why amber preserves so much

Amber is fossilized tree resin. When a small animal gets stuck in sticky resin, the resin seals it away from the air and the usual decay. Over millions of years it hardens, and the tiny creature stays inside, often with fine features intact.

That is exactly what happened here. Shell hairs are the kind of fragile detail that would crumble or wear away in many other fossil settings. In amber, they can survive for nearly 100 million years.

## Why scientists scanned it instead of cracking it open

You can't easily cut into a priceless fossil to see what's inside. The study highlights micro-CT scanning, an imaging method that builds a detailed 3D picture of a specimen without damaging it, per the report.

Think of it as a medical CT scan scaled down for something smaller than a grain of rice. Researchers can examine the shell's shape and surface details from every angle on screen while the amber stays whole. That is a big deal for tiny, delicate fossils, which are easy to destroy and impossible to replace.

## The mystery of the hairy shell

Hairy shells aren't unheard of in living snails, but this one is a reminder that shell hairs are a puzzle. The report coverage I'm working from doesn't say what the hairs did for this species, and I won't guess. What the fossil does give researchers is a rare look at a feature that usually vanishes with time.

That is the real value of finds like this: each one adds a data point about what small animals looked like and lived alongside in the Mesozoic.

## Why name a snail after a rave icon?

Scientists are free to name new species almost anything, and pop-culture names tend to stick in people's minds. Here the logic is visual: the shell's bristles echo Flint's spiky hair. The name gets a story about a fossil snail in front of readers who would never open a paleontology journal.

It also does what good names do. *Archaeospirostoma flinti* is now attached to a memorable image, which makes the science easier to share.

## What to notice next time

You can't find Mesozoic snails in your backyard, but you can practice the same close looking.

- **Check living snails.** Look at garden snails on a damp morning. Notice shell texture, ridges, and color bands. Some species have fine hairs on the shell surface, which you may need a hand lens to see.
- **Look at tree resin.** Pine, spruce, and other conifers ooze sticky resin on trunks. You may spot a trapped gnat or bit of bark. It's a small-scale version of how amber begins.
- **Visit a museum.** Natural history museums often display amber with insects or other creatures inside. Look for the tiniest details, since those are what amber does best.

## The takeaway

A fossil can be tiny, fragile, and nearly 100 million years old, and still be read in 3D without a single crack. Next time you pass a snail on a wet path or a bead of resin on a pine, take a closer look: that is where the same story starts.
