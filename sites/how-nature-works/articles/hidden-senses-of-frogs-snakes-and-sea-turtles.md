Picture a frog sitting perfectly still at the edge of a pond. It looks like it is doing nothing. In reality, it may be taking in a lot of information that you cannot detect at all.

The short answer is that many amphibians and reptiles have senses that go well beyond the familiar five. Some feel vibrations through their skin, some detect heat, and some appear to read Earth's magnetic field. Here is how a few of them work, and what you can look for the next time you spot one outdoors.

This article draws on a feature originally reported by Phys, which explores these unusual senses. I have kept to the details that report supports.

## The Surinam toad: hunting with its fingertips

Start with one of the strangest frogs on Earth. The Surinam toad lives in the murky waters of the Amazon basin. It is flat-bodied and has tiny eyes, and its vision is very poor, per Phys.

Yet it hunts well. The trick is in its hands. According to the report, the toad's fingertips carry 128 sensory lobes that detect water vibrations. When a fish swims close, the toad can feel the movement in the water, even in total darkness.

Think about why this works. In silty, dark water, eyes are not much help. Vibrations, though, travel well through water. A toad that can read them with its fingers does not need to see its prey.

## Why frogs sit so still

That stillness you notice in frogs on land has a sensory explanation too. One angle in the Phys feature is cutaneous mechanoreception, which is a technical way of saying the skin can sense touch and movement. This lets a frog monitor danger without moving and attracting predators.

In other words, staying put is not passive. A motionless frog may be actively listening with its skin.

**What to notice:** Approach a frog slowly and watch how long it holds still before it jumps. Sudden movement, or even vibrations through the ground or water, may be what triggers it. Move carefully and you will get a longer look.

## Pit vipers: a heat map of prey

Some snakes sense something humans cannot see at all. Pit vipers have heat-sensing pit organs that detect infrared radiation. The effect is something like a heat map of nearby prey, one that is invisible to human eyes, as the Phys report describes it.

If you have ever seen thermal imaging footage of a warm animal glowing against a cool background, you have a decent mental picture of what this might be like. It is an analogy, not a claim that snakes see exactly that image.

The pits themselves are visible features. On pit vipers, they sit on the face between the eye and the nostril. Rattlesnakes are a well-known group of pit vipers in North America.

## Tasting the air with a forked tongue

The flicking tongue of a snake is another sensory tool. The Phys feature points to how snakes taste the air to hunt and find mates. Each flick samples the environment, and the fork lets the snake gather information from the air as it moves.

**What to notice:** If you see a snake flicking its tongue, it is gathering information, not threatening you. It is usually reading the area around it.

## Sea turtles and a built-in compass

Then there is magnetoreception, the ability to sense Earth's magnetic field. Phys highlights sea turtles, noting that young turtles navigate ocean journeys of around 8,000 miles using the magnetic field like a natural GPS.

That is a remarkable feat for an animal that hatches on a beach and heads into open water. There are no landmarks in the middle of an ocean, but there is a magnetic field to follow.

## What these senses have in common

Each of these animals has solved a problem in its own habitat. Murky water, a need to hunt warm-bodied prey, a vast featureless ocean: each calls for a different tool. The senses are not exotic extras. They fit the places where these animals live.

Phys also frames this as relevant to conservation. As biodiversity declines, understanding how animals perceive their surroundings helps us appreciate their roles in the ecosystem and what they depend on to survive.

## Your next walk

You do not need a rainforest to put this to use. Next time you pass a pond, a garden wall, or a sunny rock where a reptile might bask, slow down and watch. Notice how still the animal holds, and what finally makes it move. You are watching an animal that is likely sensing far more than you are. Give it space, and let it keep its secrets.
