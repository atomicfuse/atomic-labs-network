Picture a flock of small shorebirds working the edge of a beach at low tide, probing wet sand, then lifting off together when a wave runs higher than expected. Now picture that same beach with a seawall or a parking lot behind it and water rising in front. There is nowhere for the birds, or the beach, to go.

That is the situation described in a recent report from Phys on a review of 37 studies about sea-level rise and migratory shorebirds. The short answer: rising seas are shrinking and degrading the beaches, salt marshes, and tidal flats these birds rely on, and the review points to widespread risks to habitat, survival, and migration. By the end of this article you will understand how that squeeze works and know what to look for on your own stretch of coast.

## What "coastal squeeze" actually means

Coastal habitats are not fixed in place. As water rises, beaches, marshes, and flats can naturally shift inland, if there is room. Coastal squeeze happens when that room disappears.

Per Phys, climate-driven sea-level rise and coastal development are converging to trap ecosystems between water on one side and infrastructure on the other. Think of a sandwich: the sea presses from the front, roads, buildings, and hard shoreline structures press from behind, and the habitat in the middle gets thinner.

This is happening just as many shorebird populations are already declining, according to the report. So the habitat loss lands on birds that have little margin to spare.

## Why shorebirds are especially exposed

Shorebirds depend on three kinds of places the review highlights:

- **Beaches**, where birds feed and rest along the waterline.
- **Salt marshes**, which provide habitat at the edge of the tide.
- **Tidal flats**, the muddy or sandy stretches exposed when the tide goes out.

All three sit at the exact boundary where land meets sea, so even small changes in water level can alter how much of each is available. Tidal flats, for example, are only useful when they are exposed. If the water stays higher, there is less ground to feed on.

Because these are *migratory* birds, the problem is multiplied. A species may use breeding grounds, wintering areas, and stopover sites in different places, and trouble at any one of them can affect the whole journey.

## Bottleneck sites: where a loss hurts most

The review flags risks to migration bottleneck sites. A bottleneck is a place where large numbers of birds concentrate during migration, often to refuel before the next leg. When a lot of a population depends on a few such spots, damage to one of them can have an outsized effect.

The report details available here do not name specific sites or give counts for individual species, so it would be a guess to say which beaches are most at risk. What the review does show, per Phys, is that this kind of vulnerability turns up across the research.

## Why this matters beyond the birds

Phys notes that these habitats do more than feed shorebirds. They defend human communities, store carbon, and support broader biodiversity. Salt marshes and similar coastal ecosystems act as natural buffers, so losing them removes a layer of protection for people living nearby.

That is why the report frames protection as increasingly urgent. Saving shorebird habitat and protecting coastal communities are, in many cases, the same project.

## Seawalls versus nature-based protection

Hard barriers are the familiar response to rising water, but they are also a main ingredient in coastal squeeze, because they fix the shoreline in place and leave habitat nowhere to move. Nature-based approaches work differently: they keep beaches, marshes, and flats functioning, and so can protect both wildlife and people. The details of which approach suits which coast depend on local conditions, and the report summary here does not rank specific methods. But the logic is simple: leave room for the shoreline to shift, and the habitat can shift with it.

## What to notice on your next coastal walk

You do not need to be at a famous migration site to see the pattern. Try these checks:

1. **Look behind the beach.** Is there open dune, marsh, or vegetation, or a wall, road, or building right at the edge? That tells you whether the shore has space to move.
2. **Watch the tide line.** At high tide, how much dry sand is left for birds to rest on? A narrow strip means little room.
3. **Visit at low tide too.** Exposed flats are feeding grounds. Notice how much ground appears and how many birds use it.
4. **Note the timing.** Shorebirds pass through during migration seasons, so repeat visits can show who is using the site and when.
5. **Keep your distance.** Resting and feeding birds need to save energy, so give flocks space and keep dogs leashed.

## The takeaway

Sea-level rise is not only an ice-cap story. It plays out on the beach you can walk to. Next time you are at the shore, look at what sits behind the waterline, and if your community is making shoreline decisions, support plans that leave room for beaches, marshes, and tidal flats to survive.
