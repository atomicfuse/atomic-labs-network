Picture a strand of hair, a fingernail, a few dry leaves. Small things, easy to overlook. Yet in the high Andes, chemical traces in hair and nails have just told researchers what some of the Inca Empire's most important ritual participants consumed in their final days.

The short answer: coca, in every case studied. Beyond that, the picture varied from one mummy to the next. This is a sober subject, because the mummies are people who were sacrificed, many of them children. But the findings show how closely human ritual was tied to particular plants, and how much local variation there was.

## What the researchers did

Per a report from Phys, archaeologists ran toxicological analysis on eight mummies. These were people sacrificed at mountain shrines across the Inca Empire as part of capacocha, described as the empire's most sacred ritual and one that involved child sacrifice.

The key is the tissue. Hair and nails preserve in dry, cold conditions, and they can hold traces of substances a person took in. Advanced toxicology techniques have only recently become sensitive enough to detect the very low concentrations of ancient drugs left in them. That is what opened this window.

## Coca was the constant

Coca leaves showed up in all eight mummies. That is the most consistent result, and it fits a picture of coca as a core part of the ritual rather than an optional extra.

Coca is a plant leaf, the kind of thing you could picture sitting in a pouch or being chewed. The finding is a reminder that for many ancient societies, the botany around them was inseparable from religion. A leaf was an ingredient in ceremony.

## Alcohol and a possible ayahuasca link

Beyond coca, the results diverged:

- **Alcohol** appeared in some of the mummies, but not all.
- **Harmine**, a compound linked to ayahuasca, was found in at least one mummy.

Harmine's association with ayahuasca is the notable part. Ayahuasca is a plant-based brew, and finding a related compound in an Inca mummy suggests that substances tied to other regions or traditions may have played a role in at least some cases. The researchers' findings do not tell us everything about how or why it was used, and it is wise to treat the interpretation as open.

## The ritual was not one-size-fits-all

The larger lesson is that capacocha does not look like a single standardized procedure. If every sacrifice followed an identical script, you would expect similar chemistry in every mummy. Instead, coca was universal while other substances came and went.

That points toward a religious system adapted to local places and traditions rather than stamped out identically across an empire. The reporting also highlights the Lady of Ampato, known as Juanita, as a case where the substances differed from those of other sacrificed children. That hints at mountain-specific practices, though it is a suggestion rather than a settled conclusion.

One caution: eight mummies is a small sample. It is enough to challenge the assumption of uniformity, but not enough to map every shrine's customs.

## Why a plant story belongs here

We usually think of nature as something outside human affairs: birds at a feeder, trees changing color. But people have always been entangled with plants, and this research shows how deeply. A leaf, a fermented drink and a compound linked to a rainforest brew all appear in the chemical record of a single ritual tradition.

It also shows how science can read small remnants. Hair and nails do not look like archives, yet they can hold a record of what someone took in, long after the person is gone.

## Something to keep in mind

These findings come from human remains, and the people studied were sacrificed, many of them children. That is a reason to read the science with care and respect, treating the results as understanding rather than spectacle.

Next time you see a plant-based tradition, whether a ceremonial tea or a festival garland, ask what the plant meant to the people who used it and where they got it. The answer, as these mummies show, is rarely simple, and it often varies from place to place.
