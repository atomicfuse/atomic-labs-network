Picture a quiet stretch of the Derbyshire Peak District in central England: stone walls, green hills, sheep. Now picture the same landscape more than 1,300 years ago, with mines and smelters at work and children growing up breathing and absorbing lead. The proof isn't in a document. It's in their teeth.

The short answer: tooth enamel keeps a record of childhood lead exposure for life, and researchers used it to show that lead production in the Peak District was up and running by the mid-7th century. Here is how that works, and what the teeth say about who paid the price.

## Why teeth hold a record of pollution

The usual ways of dating old lead industries have a problem. According to the report in Popular Science, manufacturers from the 16th to 19th centuries repeatedly re-smelted lead waste from ancient Rome and medieval England. That destroyed much of the evidence of where the metal came from and how it was used.

Teeth offer a workaround. Tooth enamel, the hard outer layer, finishes forming during childhood. Any lead a child absorbed while it formed stays embedded in the enamel for the rest of that person's life, even centuries after death. Each tooth is a small sealed time capsule of the environment someone grew up in.

## What the researchers did

A team including Christopher Loveluck of the University of Nottingham analyzed enamel from people buried in three Peak District cemeteries. The burials date from between 650 and 1500 CE. The Peak District was one of Europe's largest suppliers of lead during the Middle Ages, which made it a logical place to look.

The results, published in the journal Antiquity, pushed the timeline back. By the 7th century, children in Derbyshire had lead levels the same as, or higher than, those of children in industrialized cities in the mid-18th and 19th centuries. By the mid-8th century, the lead industry's reach had spread into surrounding areas such as Castleton.

Loveluck said in the study's announcement that the team was delighted to have confirmed the date the early medieval lead industry was established in the Peak District "through evidence provided by the people themselves." Put simply, after the Roman era, mining and smelting restarted around the mid-7th century.

Exposure peaked in the late 12th and early 13th centuries, which matches earlier historical and environmental data.

## Lead wasn't shared equally

Everyone in these communities was exposed, but not to the same degree. Graves of higher-status people included weapons and other personal items, and their teeth showed lower lead concentrations than those of poorer residents. The researchers read this as a sign that wealthier people owned lead-containing possessions, but of higher quality.

Working-class families employed in the mines and in processing, meanwhile, routinely exposed their children to larger amounts of lead. In other words, the same industry that made some people money loaded its health costs onto the people doing the work, and onto their kids.

## Why young women may have been hit hardest

The study also points to women as especially vulnerable. Three women who died between ages 17 and 25 had particularly high lead levels in their enamel. Across the cemeteries, most men survived to middle or old age, while women often died in young adulthood.

The researchers are careful here. Anna Bloxam, a Castleton excavation co-director and study co-author, said the results show that in these mining communities, girls and boys alike were raised in dangerously polluted homes. She said this exposure "perhaps contributed to higher rates of premature death among women of child-bearing age." That "perhaps" matters: the teeth show exposure, and the pattern of early deaths fits, but the study doesn't prove lead was the cause in each case.

## What this means for how you see a landscape

Colin Merrony, a historian at the University of Sheffield and study co-author, said the work lets researchers look more deeply, for the first time, at the impact lead production had on individuals and communities.

Lead contamination is still a serious issue today, though conditions are far better than in medieval England. The underlying pattern, where environmental harm lands hardest on people with the fewest options, hasn't gone away.

### Something to notice next time

When you walk through an old mining region, a river valley with historic works, or even an older neighborhood, remember that the land can hold a pollution history that the scenery hides. Small signs, such as spoil heaps, old smelting sites, or place names, can point to industry that once shaped people's health in ways nobody wrote down.

The lesson from Derbyshire is that pollution leaves a record in living things. If you want to know what a place was like, look at who grew up there.
