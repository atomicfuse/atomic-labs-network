Picture a jar of yogurt left on a counter. A film forms, the smell turns tangy, and the container is never quite the same after washing. Now picture that process happening in a clay pot more than 5,000 years ago, and that pot ending up in a stone tomb.

That is the idea behind a study reported by Phys. Experimental archaeology suggests that corroded Neolithic pottery from Danish passage tombs was likely used to ferment dairy, especially fresh unpasteurized milk. The researchers argue that fermented milk products may have carried symbolic or ritual power in funerary practices.

The short answer: the pots seem to have held more than ordinary food. By the end of this article, you will know how scientists tested that idea and why a cow's milk may link to a burial ceremony.

## What experimental archaeology actually does

Most archaeology works backward. You find an object and guess how it was used. Experimental archaeology works in both directions: researchers recreate the object and the conditions, run the process, and see what happens.

In this case, the question was what substances were fermented in Neolithic Scandinavian pottery that was later deposited in tombs. Per Phys, the experiments point toward dairy, especially fresh, unpasteurized milk, as the likely candidate for the corrosion found on the Danish vessels.

That matters because pottery that sat in the ground for millennia rarely tells its story plainly. Residue breaks down, and the clay itself changes. Recreating the fermenting process lets researchers compare what happens to a modern replica with what they see on the ancient pots.

## What pottery corrosion can reveal

Fermenting liquids can be chemically active. As milk sours, it becomes acidic, and acid can leave its mark on a surface. The study treats corrosion on the pots as evidence in its own right, a kind of fingerprint of what was inside.

I would not read this as proof that every pot held milk. The reported conclusion is that the corroded vessels were likely used for fermenting dairy. That is a careful claim, and it is worth keeping it that way.

## Why fermented milk, not just milk?

Fresh milk and fermented milk are different foods. Fermentation changes how a food keeps and how it feels to eat. Phys describes early European farming communities as using fermentation for preservation and digestion. Many adults cannot digest large amounts of fresh milk, and fermented products are often easier to tolerate. That is a general point rather than a finding about these particular pots, so treat it as background.

The new angle is meaning. The study argues that fermented dairy was not only a practical food but something that mattered symbolically around death.

## Why put it in a tomb?

The pots were deposited in passage tombs, which are stone burial structures reached by a narrow entrance passage. Their placement suggests the vessels were part of funerary practice, not simply lost or discarded household items.

The researchers' argument, as reported, is that fermented milk products held symbolic or ritual power. One plausible reading is that a substance that visibly transforms, turning from liquid milk into something sour and thick, could feel meaningful in ceremonies about death and change. That interpretation is mine, not a quoted finding, and archaeologists generally treat ritual explanations cautiously because we cannot ask the people involved.

What the evidence does support is that dairy was handled in a way that connected everyday farming life with burial.

## Why this resonates now

Fermentation is having a moment, from yogurt and kefir to gut health. This study offers a longer view: people in Neolithic Denmark were already working with microbes, whether or not they understood them, and giving the results cultural weight.

## What to notice next time

You do not need a tomb to see the process at work.

- **Watch milk sour.** Leave a small amount of fresh milk in a clean glass in a cool kitchen for a day. Pasteurized milk spoils differently from raw milk, so do not drink the result. Just note the smell and the change in texture.
- **Look at old containers.** Hard water, acidic foods, and long storage can leave dull, etched marks on glass and ceramics. That is a small-scale version of the surface wear archaeologists read on ancient pots.
- **Visit a museum.** Neolithic pottery is often plain at first glance. Check the labels for where it was found. Pots from tombs, settlements, and bogs can tell different stories.

## The takeaway

When you see an old pot in a museum case, ask what it held, not just how it was made. Chemistry, experiments, and context can turn a corroded surface into evidence of what people ate, and what they thought was worth sending with the dead.
