Picture a ferry crossing a stretch of the Salish Sea on an ordinary day: gray water, a line of dark islands, passengers at the rail watching for a spout. Now picture that same crossing ending with a collision. That is what happened to Notcho, a humpback whale struck by a BC Ferries vessel, and the public response suggests many people felt his death personally.

If you have ever felt sad about a lost tree, a drained wetland, or an animal you never met, there is a name for that feeling, and it is normal. This article explains what ecological grief is, what Notcho's story shows about it, and a few practical ways to carry it.

*A note on sourcing: the details here come from a report by Phys on Notcho's death and the grief that followed. I am working only from that summary, so I haven't added specifics about the collision beyond what it states.*

## What ecological grief is

Ecological grief is the sorrow people feel over losses in the natural world. Those losses can be an individual animal, a whole ecosystem, or climate-related change. Per Phys, the public reaction to Notcho's death reflects a wider cultural shift toward treating grief for animals, ecosystems, and climate losses as valid rather than silly or exaggerated.

That matters because many people quietly assume they shouldn't feel this way. They think grief is reserved for people or pets. The framing in the Phys piece pushes back on that. Feeling a pang when a wild animal dies is not a sign that you are overreacting. It is a sign that you were paying attention.

## Why one whale became a bigger story

Phys reports that Notcho's death became a national story. It's hard to say exactly why one animal draws that attention, but the pattern is familiar from backyard life too. A named animal is easier to care about than a statistic. A single heron that returns to the same creek each spring can feel like a neighbor. Give a whale a name and a face-like tail pattern, and a faceless headline becomes a individual loss.

There is also the setting. The Salish Sea is a busy waterway shared by ferries, boats, and whales, and people can picture themselves on that water.

## Why recovering whales still face human-made risks

Here is the tension at the center of the story. As whale populations recover in busy waters like the Salish Sea, Phys notes, collisions between wildlife and people are becoming more visible and more emotionally charged.

In plain terms: good news and bad news can arrive together. More whales in shared waters means more chances to see them, and more chances for vessels and animals to meet. Recovery is a conservation success, but it does not remove the risks that come from heavy boat traffic.

You can see a land version of this near you. When deer, coyotes, or owls rebound in suburbs, road strikes tend to become more noticeable too. The animals are not the problem. The overlap is.

## Two ways to respond: act or mourn

The Phys article describes two responses to environmental loss, and argues both help communities cope.

### Taking action

Action turns grief into purpose. For a loss like Notcho's, that points toward prevention: making shared waters safer for whales. If you feel helpless, doing something concrete can ease that feeling. Support local wildlife groups, follow the work of those studying collisions, and speak up where decisions about busy waterways are made.

### Expressing emotion and mourning

Not every loss has a fix, and not every feeling needs one. The article also argues that emotional expression and meaningful mourning practices matter. Saying out loud that you are sad, writing about it, or sharing it with others who feel the same are all valid. Skipping this step and rushing straight to action can leave the grief sitting unprocessed.

Most people do better with a mix. Action without space to feel can turn into burnout, and feeling without any outlet can turn into despair.

## How to mark a loss respectfully

The Phys piece points toward personal rituals for nature loss, with one important caution: don't appropriate Indigenous ceremony. You don't need to borrow someone else's traditions to mourn well. Here are simple, doable options of your own making:

- **Write a few lines.** Note what the animal or place meant to you, even if you never met it.
- **Visit a place.** Spend ten quiet minutes at a shoreline, creek, or park that connects you to the species or habitat you lost.
- **Share it.** Tell a friend or family member. Grief shared tends to feel lighter.
- **Pair it with a small act.** Donate, volunteer, or learn how local wildlife is protected, then do one thing.
- **Return later.** Come back in a season and notice what is still there.

The point is to make it deliberate and yours. A ritual can be small and still count.

## What to notice next time

Pay attention to your own reaction the next time a wildlife story lands hard, whether it's a whale, a bird hit by a window, or a favorite tree cut down. Ask yourself two questions: what do I feel, and is there one thing I can do? Often the answer to both is available. Name the feeling, then pick one small act. That is how grief for the natural world becomes something you can live with, and sometimes something that helps.
