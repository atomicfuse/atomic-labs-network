Picture a pigeon flock wheeling over a city square, then scattering all at once. Somewhere above them, a dark, sharp-winged shape has just committed to a dive. That's a peregrine falcon (*Falco peregrinus*), the fastest animal on the planet.

Here's the short answer to a surprising new finding: in the United Kingdom, a warming climate could make more land suitable for peregrines, not less. But that is only part of the story. By the end of this article, you'll know which other raptors may gain or lose ground, and why "more suitable habitat" is not the same as "more falcons."

## What the new study found

Connor Panter, a researcher at the University of Nottingham's School of Geography, published the work in the journal *Frontiers in Ecology and Evolution*. Per Popular Science, which first reported it, Panter modeled how future climate change could make different parts of the UK more or less suitable for 16 species of breeding birds of prey.

He tested two scenarios: a "middle-of-the-road" one and a "worst-case" one. For peregrines, the numbers look like this:

- **Today:** 42,570 square miles of suitable habitat in the UK.
- **Middle-of-the-road scenario:** a possible 40.6 percent increase by 2060.
- **Worst-case scenario:** a possible 73.5 percent increase by 2080.

Those are modeled projections, not predictions that falcons will actually spread across that much ground.

## Climate winners and losers among UK raptors

The peregrine isn't alone. According to the study, several other birds of prey could also gain suitable habitat:

- Hen harrier (*Circus cyaneus*)
- Golden eagle (*Aquila chrysaetos*)
- Little owl (*Athene noctua*)
- Long-eared owl (*Asio otus*)
- Western barn owl (*Tyto alba*)
- Western marsh harrier (*Circus aeruginosus*)

Three species aren't predicted to change much in any scenario: the common buzzard (*Buteo buteo*), European sparrowhawk (*Accipiter nisus*), and tawny owl (*Strix aluco*).

Four are likely to lose significant amounts of suitable land: the short-eared owl (*Asio flammeus*), red kite (*Milvus milvus*), merlin (*Falco columbarius*), and Eurasian goshawk (*Astur gentilis*).

Notice that the merlin, a small falcon, is on the losing list while its relative the peregrine is on the winning one. Close cousins can respond very differently.

## Why more habitat doesn't mean more birds

This is the part worth remembering. "It is also important to recognise that an increase in the area suitable for a species does not necessarily mean that its population will grow or that it will become more widespread," Panter told Popular Science.

Suitable habitat is a map of where conditions could work. Whether a falcon actually moves in depends on other things. Panter listed several: the availability of prey, nesting opportunities, threats from humans, and interactions with other species.

Think of it like a new apartment building going up in your town. The building existing doesn't fill it. People need jobs nearby, groceries, and a reason to move.

For a peregrine, that means enough birds to hunt, a suitable ledge or cliff to nest on, and few people disturbing or harming it. A newly suitable region missing any of those stays empty.

## Why this makes conservation harder

It's tempting to assume climate change simply shrinks habitat for every animal. This study suggests the real picture is mixed, even among birds that share the same landscape.

Panter's work emphasizes that climate change will probably not affect all birds of prey in the same way, even in the same areas. According to Panter, that matters for protection efforts, because future conservation will have to account for those differences. A plan that helps a gaining species might do little for a losing one, and vice versa.

As Panter put it to Popular Science, "Raptors play important ecological roles as predators, and understanding how their distributions might change is important for informing future conservation efforts."

## What to notice next time you're outside

You don't need to live in the UK to apply this idea. Peregrines are found in many parts of the world, though this study covers only the UK, so its numbers shouldn't be applied elsewhere.

Wherever you are, a few things are worth watching for:

- **Look at the skyline.** Peregrines favor tall, ledge-like structures. If you see pigeons suddenly bolt from a cliff face or tall building, look up.
- **Check the prey.** Falcons follow food. A healthy bird population in a park or along a river is part of what makes a place workable for a predator.
- **Spot the shape.** Peregrines have pointed, swept-back wings and a fast, direct flight. Other local raptors will have different silhouettes, and noticing which ones show up tells you something about your area.
- **Pay attention to change.** If a raptor appears where it wasn't seen before, or disappears, that's useful information. Local bird clubs and wildlife agencies often welcome sightings.

## The takeaway

When you hear that climate change is reshaping wildlife, remember that it sorts species into gains, losses, and little change. The next time you see a raptor overhead, ask what it needs from the landscape below it: food, a place to nest, and room to be left alone. Those are things people can influence.
