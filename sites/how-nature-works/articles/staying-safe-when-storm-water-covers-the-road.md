Picture the road ahead of you shining under your headlights, and then the shine doesn't end where the pavement dips. That was the reality for many travelers this Thanksgiving. Per HuffPost, heavy rain, high winds and flooding hit much of the U.S. during one of the busiest travel periods of the year. The storms brought power outages, flight delays and infrastructure damage from the East Coast to California, with heavy snow in other parts of the country. Macy's 100th Thanksgiving Day Parade was postponed because of the weather.

Most of us will never see a parade called off, but plenty of us will meet a flooded road. Here is how that water gets there and what to do when you reach it.

## The short answer: water goes where it can, and roads are low

Rain that falls faster than the ground can soak it up runs downhill. Pavement doesn't absorb anything, so streets, underpasses and low-lying intersections collect that runoff quickly. Add wind that pushes water along and storm drains that fill with leaves and debris, and a road can go from wet to impassable in minutes.

That is why a flooded road often sits in a spot that looked perfectly ordinary yesterday. The land hasn't changed. The water has simply found the low point.

## What to do if you come to water on the road

The National Weather Service's long-running advice is "Turn Around, Don't Drown." It's short because the decision has to be quick.

### Turn around

You can't tell how deep water is by looking at it. The pavement underneath may be washed out, and the surface can hide debris. Guidance from the National Weather Service notes that just six inches of moving water can knock an adult off their feet, and about a foot can float many cars. Find another route, even if it adds an hour.

### Don't follow the car in front of you

If another driver pushes through, that tells you only that their car made it. It says nothing about the next one, or about the water level a minute later.

### If your car stalls in rising water

Leave the vehicle if you can do so safely and move to higher ground. A car can be replaced. Don't wait for the water to reach the doors.

### Plan around the dark

Flooded roads are far harder to spot at night. If storms are forecast for your route, try to drive in daylight, and keep a full tank or charged battery, since outages and closures can leave you waiting a long time.

## Why coastal flooding can surprise you

HuffPost's coverage highlights coastal flooding and king tides among the storm's effects. A king tide is an especially high tide, the kind that happens when the sun and moon line up in a way that boosts the normal pull on the oceans. On a calm, sunny day, a king tide may only creep into a low parking lot or the end of a street.

The trouble comes when it overlaps with a storm. Wind pushing water toward shore and heavy rain trying to drain toward the sea meet a tide that is already high. Water that would normally flow out through storm drains has nowhere to go, so streets near the coast can flood even when the rain isn't the heaviest in the region.

If you live or travel near the coast, check the tide schedule as well as the rain forecast. A high tide at the wrong hour can turn a manageable storm into a flooded commute.

## What to notice next time

You can learn a lot about where water will go before the next storm arrives:

- **Look for the low spots.** Notice which intersections, underpasses and driveways dip below their surroundings. Those are the first to flood.
- **Check the storm drains.** Clear leaves from a drain near your home if it's safe and permitted. Fall leaves are a common reason that water pools.
- **Watch the debris line.** After a storm, a stripe of leaves, twigs and sand along a curb or lawn shows how high the water reached. Remember it.
- **Find the tide line near the coast.** A band of seaweed or sand well above the usual waterline shows where a high tide pushed.

## When the storm is also a power problem

The same weather that floods roads also knocks out power. Wind brings down limbs and lines, and saturated ground loosens roots, which makes trees easier to topple. Treat downed lines as live and stay well away from them. Report them to the utility rather than moving anything yourself. Keep phones charged ahead of a storm and know where you'd go if a long outage left your home cold.

## The takeaway

Before your next trip in stormy weather, pick a second route and decide in advance that you'll use it the moment you see water over the road. That decision, made calmly at home, is the one that matters when you're staring at a flooded street.
