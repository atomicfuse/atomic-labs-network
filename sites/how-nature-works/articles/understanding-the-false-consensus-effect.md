Picture a feeder hanging in your yard. For a week, nearly every bird that lands is a house sparrow. By Friday, you might feel sure the whole neighborhood is sparrows. Then a friend three streets over mentions the cardinals and chickadees at her place, and your confident count falls apart.

That small mistake, treating what you see around you as what is true everywhere, is a close cousin of something researchers have just documented in people who believe conspiracy theories. Here is what the finding says, why it happens, and how you can use the feeder lesson to check your own sense of "what everyone thinks."

This is a departure from our usual birds and rivers, but it is about the same skill: counting honestly from a limited view.

## What the new research found

Per a report from PsyPost, new research finds that people who believe conspiracy theories tend to overestimate how many other people agree with them. The pattern is known as a false consensus effect: the belief that your own views are more widely shared than they really are.

The report points to a possible consequence. If believers think their views are common, those views may feel more socially accepted than they are, which may help fringe beliefs stick around. The PsyPost summary frames this as a reinforcing loop, not a proven one-way cause, and we are limited to what that report states. It does not give us study sizes or exact percentages here, so we will not guess at them.

## The feeder problem: why a small view feels like the whole picture

False consensus is not unique to conspiracy thinking. It is a general quirk of how people judge the crowd. You know your own opinion intimately, and you tend to spend time with people who share it. Those two facts make your own view feel like the default.

Back at the feeder, you are not lying to yourself about the sparrows. Every one you counted was real. The error is in the sample: one feeder, one week, one patch of habitat. The more you rely on that narrow window, the more your estimate drifts from reality.

The same logic applies to opinions. If the people you talk to and the posts you see mostly agree with you, your count is accurate for that window and wrong for the wider world.

## How online spaces can make fringe views look mainstream

The PsyPost report notes that conspiracy thinking remains a major force in politics, health, and online culture, and that social platforms can amplify perceived agreement. That fits the feeder picture well.

Online, you can often choose where to "hang the feeder." Follow certain accounts, join certain groups, and the platform tends to show you more of the same. A few things can then make a small group look large:

- **Visible agreement.** Likes, shares, and supportive replies are easy to see. The silent majority who scroll past are not.
- **Selective company.** People drift toward communities where their belief is treated as normal.
- **Repetition.** Seeing the same claim from many angles can feel like many independent voices, even when it is a smaller circle echoing itself. That last point is our analysis, not a finding from the report.

The result is a feeling of validation. If "everyone knows" something in your feed, it is easy to assume everyone knows it in the world.

## Why the feeling matters

The report's central idea is that believing others agree can strengthen a belief. Think about how you treat a bird sighting. If only you saw a rare warbler, you might double-check your field guide. If you think a dozen neighbors saw it too, you stop doubting.

Perceived agreement works like that. It lowers the bar for self-checking and makes a belief feel safe to hold and share. That may be one reason misinformation communities can grow and persist, as the report suggests: believers feel supported, so they stay and recruit.

It also suggests something about trying to change minds. Facts alone may bounce off if a person's confidence rests partly on a sense of being in the majority. That is our reading of the finding, not a tested recommendation from the study.

## How to check your own sense of the crowd

None of us is immune to false consensus, so a few feeder-style habits help.

### Move the feeder

Bird counters get better data by sampling several places. Do the same with opinions. Ask people outside your usual circle what they think, and treat their answers as real data about the wider group.

### Count the quiet birds

For every visible post agreeing with you, many people saw it and said nothing. Do not read silence as support.

### Look for the real count

When someone says "everybody knows," look for an actual survey or measurement. Careful counts exist for many questions, and they are a better guide than your feed or your friend group.

### Notice who is missing

If every voice in a conversation sounds alike, that is a clue your sample is narrow, just as a feeder visited only by sparrows says more about the feeder than about the neighborhood.

## What to do next

The next time you feel sure that "everyone" agrees with you, treat it like a feeder count: ask how many places you actually looked, then go look somewhere else.
