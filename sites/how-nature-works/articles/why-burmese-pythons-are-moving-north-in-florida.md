Picture a quiet Florida canal bank on a cool morning: sun on the grass, a few holes in the dirt where small mammals have dug in. Nothing looks out of place. But according to Fox News, that kind of ordinary habitat may now be giving shelter to one of the most destructive invasive predators in the United States.

The short answer to why Burmese pythons are spreading north: they appear to be using animal burrows to get through colder temperatures. Here is what that means, why it matters, and what you can do if you see one.

## What's happening

Fox News reports that Burmese pythons have been damaging the Florida Everglades ecosystem for more than 25 years. Experts now warn the snakes have been spotted farther north than before, which suggests the problem may no longer be confined to South Florida.

Per the report, tens of thousands of pythons are already established in the state, and they breed quickly. That combination is what makes a northward push worrying: more snakes, in more places, with more native wildlife in reach.

## Why burrows matter

Burmese pythons are not native to Florida, and cold is one of the things that can limit where a tropical snake survives. According to the Fox News report, the pythons are using animal burrows to survive colder temperatures.

That is a simple but powerful trick. A burrow is a shelter from the air above it. A snake that can slip underground when the weather turns cool has a better chance of getting through a chilly spell than one stuck out in the open.

The report doesn't spell out how far north the snakes may eventually settle, and it's too early to say where the limit is. What the burrow behavior suggests, and this is our reading rather than a finding from the report, is that cool snaps alone may not be a reliable barrier.

## What pythons do to native wildlife

Fox News describes the pythons as devastating to native mammals and reptiles in the Everglades, and it frames the stakes as growing as the snakes spread. An invasive predator that has no long history in a habitat can hit local animals hard, because those animals did not evolve alongside it.

## Meet the hunters

The Fox News piece follows professional python hunter Amy Siewe, whose work reflects the broader push to remove these snakes from the wild. The reporting highlights three things as critical:

- **Early detection:** finding snakes before they become established in a new area.
- **Public education:** helping people recognize the problem and respond sensibly.
- **Removal efforts:** taking snakes out of the landscape.

If pythons are moving into new territory, spotting them early is the best chance to keep a local problem from becoming a regional one.

## How a pet becomes an ecosystem problem

The story is a reminder of how invasive species can take hold. The Fox News report presents the python crisis as an example of an invasive animal causing ecological damage far from where it belongs. Once an animal like this is established and breeding, undoing the damage becomes a long, difficult job.

## What to do if you see one

The report stresses early detection and public education, though the summary of it doesn't lay out a step-by-step checklist. These are general, commonsense precautions, not instructions from the report:

1. **Keep your distance.** Don't try to handle a large snake yourself.
2. **Note the location.** Landmarks, the time, and what the snake was doing help officials.
3. **Take a photo if it is safe.** A clear picture helps confirm the species.
4. **Report it.** Contact Florida's wildlife authorities and ask about their current reporting channels.

## What to notice next time

If you live in or visit Florida, pay attention to the places small animals dig: canal banks, brushy edges, and burrow openings. Those are the spots the northward-moving snakes may use to ride out the cold. You don't need to poke around them. Simply knowing that burrows are part of the story makes you a better observer, and early reports from ordinary people are exactly what makes detection work.

If you spot a python, don't assume someone else has already reported it. Note where you saw it and report it.
