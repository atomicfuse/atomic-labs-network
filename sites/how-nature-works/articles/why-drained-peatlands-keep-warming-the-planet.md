Picture a bog on a quiet morning: spongy moss underfoot, dark water pooled between the plants, a faint swampy smell in the air. It looks like a place where not much is happening. Underneath, a slow chemical balancing act is under way, and a drained version of that same ground can keep warming the climate for centuries.

The short answer: when peatlands are drained, the buried plant material dries out and starts releasing carbon dioxide, and it can keep doing so for a very long time. New MIT-led research, reported by Phys, estimates that global peatland drainage has caused about 2.7% of present-day human-driven warming. Here is how that works, and what you can take from it.

## The balancing act inside a bog

Peatland ecosystems such as bogs and swamps do two things at once, according to the Phys report. Their vegetation takes up huge amounts of carbon dioxide. Meanwhile, their soil microbes produce methane.

That creates a trade-off. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas, so a wet peatland is not a simple "good" ecosystem with no downside. But the carbon dioxide the plants pull in, and the carbon that ends up locked in the wet peat, are the other side of the ledger. The study's focus is on what happens when people tip that balance by draining the land.

## Why drainage creates a long-lasting problem

Peat is made of plant material that has built up over time in waterlogged ground. Water keeps it preserved. Remove the water and the peat is exposed to air.

Per the research summary, exposed peat can emit carbon dioxide for centuries. That is the key to the study's finding. Drainage is not like a one-time puff of pollution that fades. It turns a natural carbon store into a long-term source of emissions.

This also means land-use choices made long ago still matter. Ditches dug generations back can still be sending carbon dioxide into the air today, and the warming effect the researchers estimate, about 2.7% of present-day human-driven warming, reflects that long tail.

## Small footprint, big carbon store

Peatlands cover only a small share of the planet's land. Yet according to the Phys report, they store more carbon than all forests. That is why a relatively modest area of drained ground can carry such an outsized climate effect.

It is easy to picture forests as the planet's main carbon bank, because trees are tall and obvious. Peat stores its carbon out of sight, in the soil itself.

## What rewetting means

Rewetting is the reverse of drainage: restoring water to degraded peatland so the ground is saturated again. In practice, that generally means blocking or removing drainage channels so water stays put. The idea is to bring back the waterlogged conditions that kept the peat intact in the first place.

The research strengthens the case for halting drainage and rewetting degraded peatlands, as restoration and land-use policy become central to climate strategy. One important point is what restoration can and cannot do. It cannot undo warming that has already happened. It can prevent additional future warming from peat that would otherwise keep releasing carbon dioxide.

Because rewetted peatlands will again host methane-producing microbes, the methane-versus-carbon-dioxide trade-off is part of the picture. The study's argument is that the long-term carbon dioxide burden from drained peat is the larger concern to address.

## What to notice next time

You do not need to live near a bog to spot the signs of this story. A few things to look for on walks and trips:

- **Ditches cutting through flat, marshy ground.** Straight channels in low wet areas often mean someone has tried to move water off the land.
- **Moss and sedge versus dry grass.** A healthy wet peatland feels springy and soggy. Dry, crumbly dark soil in a former wetland is a sign the water is gone.
- **Signs at nature reserves.** Many protected wetlands explain restoration work, such as blocked drains or raised water levels. That is rewetting in action.

If you buy garden products, check the label on potting mixes. Peat is harvested from peatlands, and choosing peat-free options is one small way to avoid supporting that damage. The study itself focuses on drainage, so treat this as a practical extension rather than something the researchers measured.

## The takeaway

Wet ground is doing climate work you cannot see. Next time you pass a boggy field or a ditch through a marsh, remember that keeping the water in place is what keeps the carbon in the ground, and support the protection and restoration of peatlands where you can.
