If you suspect you have ADHD as an adult, the path to an assessment is changing in parts of Australia. By the end of this article, you'll understand what GP-led ADHD care is, why researchers say it needs more support, and which questions are worth asking before you book.

## What the new research says

University of Queensland research, reported by Medicalxpress, finds that expanding ADHD care to general practitioners (GPs) should be matched with additional training and Medicare support. The researchers argue that expanding adult ADHD diagnosis and treatment to GPs needs mandatory ADHD-specific training and better Medicare funding.

The work highlights Queensland's GP-led model. Per the report, GP training in Queensland remains voluntary, which raises concerns about quality, access, and affordability. Australian states are actively reshaping who can diagnose and treat adults, and demand for ADHD care is rising.

## What GP-led ADHD care actually means

In a GP-led model, your regular doctor can take part in diagnosing and treating adult ADHD. That could mean less hassle than hunting for a specialist. For many people, a GP they already know is the easiest door to knock on.

But an easier door only helps if the doctor behind it is equipped for the job. That is the gap the Queensland researchers point to.

## Why adult ADHD is harder to assess than it sounds

The research warns that adult ADHD assessments are complex and time-intensive. That matters because a standard GP appointment is short. Doing the assessment properly takes time, and time is exactly what a typical consultation, and its funding, may not allow for.

The practical lesson for you: a good assessment is not a quick checklist. If someone offers a diagnosis after a very brief chat, it's reasonable to ask how they reached it.

## Why Medicare settings shape who gets care

The researchers say better Medicare funding is needed. The report warns that without support, adult ADHD care risks becoming inequitable. My reading of that: when a thorough assessment costs more than the rebate covers, the gap tends to be paid by patients, and people with less money may delay or skip care. That's analysis, not a finding from the study, but it explains why funding sits alongside training in the researchers' recommendations.

## Questions to ask before you book an assessment

The research doesn't hand patients a checklist, so treat these as sensible prompts drawn from its concerns:

- **Has the doctor done ADHD-specific training?** In Queensland this is currently voluntary, so asking is fair.
- **How long will the assessment take?** Given that assessments are complex and time-intensive, find out whether you'll get proper time or a single short slot.
- **What will it cost out of pocket?** Ask about fees and what Medicare covers before you commit.
- **What happens after diagnosis?** Since GPs may also treat, ask how follow-up and ongoing care will work.

## The takeaway

GP-led ADHD care could widen access, but the Queensland research suggests it works best when doctors are trained and the funding makes proper assessments realistic. Before you book, ask about training, time, and cost, and choose care that gives your assessment the time it needs.
