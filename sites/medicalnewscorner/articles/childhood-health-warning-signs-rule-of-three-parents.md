Most tiredness, bruises and lumps in children turn out to be nothing serious. That's the reassuring part, and it's also why the rare exceptions are hard to spot. By the end of this article you'll know which symptoms deserve attention, how to tell a passing bump from a pattern, and when to book that GP appointment.

The context, per the BBC: cancer is rare in children, but it is the leading cause of death from disease among under-15s in the UK. Awareness campaigns are pushing for earlier referrals, because faster diagnosis can mean less intensive treatment and better outcomes.

## Why symptoms get missed

Children get tired, bruised and poorly all the time. The BBC's reporting, which draws on the experiences of Amelia Carroll and Jake Swinscoe, highlights how the early signs of childhood cancer can look ordinary. Nothing about them necessarily announces itself as serious.

The BBC also reports that many parents lack confidence in spotting symptoms. That's understandable. You can't take a child to the doctor every time they're tired, and nobody should have to. The goal isn't constant vigilance. It's knowing what a worrying pattern looks like.

## The warning signs to know

The BBC's piece points to these signs:

- **Extreme tiredness.** Not an after-school slump, but fatigue that seems out of proportion or doesn't lift.
- **Unexplained bruising.** Bruises with no obvious bump or fall behind them.
- **Paleness.** A child who looks noticeably washed out.
- **Growing lumps or swelling.** Anything that is getting bigger rather than staying put.

On their own, each of these has plenty of harmless explanations. The article's emphasis is on signs that persist, and on signs that show up together.

## The 'rule of three'

The experts quoted by the BBC urge parents and professionals to act on persistent clusters of symptoms. This is the idea behind the "rule of three": a single symptom that comes and goes is rarely a worry, but when the same problem keeps returning or doesn't settle, or several symptoms appear together, it's time to have your child checked.

I can't offer the exact wording of the rule beyond what the BBC summary supports, so treat it as a practical prompt rather than a formal diagnostic test. The spirit is clear: look for persistence and clusters, not one-off events.

## A quick checklist you can save

Ask yourself:

1. **Has it lasted?** Is the tiredness, paleness or swelling still there after what you'd expect to be a normal recovery?
2. **Has it returned?** Do you keep seeing the same symptom again and again?
3. **Is there more than one?** For example, tiredness plus paleness plus unexplained bruises.
4. **Is it growing?** A lump or swelling that's getting bigger deserves a check.
5. **Does it feel off to you?** You know your child's normal better than anyone.

If you answer yes to one or more, especially more than one, contact your GP.

## How to make the appointment count

This section is my own practical advice, not drawn from the BBC report. Before you go, jot down what you've noticed, when it started and whether it's changing. Note any symptoms that seem unrelated, since a cluster is exactly what a doctor needs to see. If symptoms persist after a first visit, it's reasonable to go back and say so plainly.

## Keeping it in proportion

Cancer in children is rare, and most of the time these symptoms have a simple explanation. Checking doesn't mean assuming the worst; it means making sure. The BBC's message is that earlier referral can lead to less intensive treatment and better outcomes, so a short appointment that turns out to be nothing is a good trade.

## The takeaway

Don't worry about every bruise. Do act when tiredness, paleness, bruising or a growing lump lingers, returns or arrives with company. Write it down, call your GP, and trust your instincts about your own child.
