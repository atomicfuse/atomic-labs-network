Flying with a baby or toddler goes better when your carry-on is built around one idea: bring what your child already knows and likes. That's the thrust of advice from flight attendants, as reported by Travel + Leisure. They point parents toward familiar snacks, comfort items, and activities as the best way to head off in-flight meltdowns.

This guide turns that advice into a packing plan you can follow in about 20 minutes. It suits parents of babies and toddlers on any flight, and it matters most in busy holiday and vacation periods, when crowded airports and long delays make a tired kid's patience even thinner.

One note on sourcing: the details below come from the Travel + Leisure report's summary of flight attendants' advice. Where I'm adding my own suggestions on how to organize things, I'll say so.

## The core rule: pack the familiar

Per the report, flight attendants' central advice is to bring familiar items. A plane is loud, crowded, and strange, and the unfamiliar is hard on small kids. The things your child already recognizes (a favorite snack, a beloved comfort item, an activity they know) do the calming for you.

That gives you a simple test for every item you consider packing: does my child already love this? If the answer is no, think twice. A flight is a poor time to introduce a new food or a toy you hope will work.

## Three categories to fill

The advice falls into three buckets: snacks, comfort items, and activities. Here's how to use each.

### Snacks

Familiar snacks are on the flight attendants' list. My practical take: pack more than you think you need, because delays are common and a hungry toddler escalates quickly. Choose snacks your child eats happily at home, and keep them where you can reach them without digging through the overhead bin.

### Comfort items

This is the category that matters most for calm. Think of whatever your child reaches for when they're tired or upset: a stuffed animal, a blanket, or something similar. Keep it in the bag under the seat, not in checked luggage, so it's there when a meltdown starts or when it's time to sleep.

### Activities

The report also highlights activities as a way to prevent meltdowns. Pick things your child already enjoys and can do in a small seat. My suggestion, not the flight attendants': don't hand everything over at once. Bring out one activity at a time so the novelty of switching lasts longer.

## Babies vs. toddlers: how to adjust

The same three categories apply to both ages, but what fills them changes. The specifics below are my own planning suggestions rather than reported advice.

### For babies

Focus on comfort and routine. Their needs are simple, so the goal is making the flight feel as much like a normal day as possible. Keep the soothing items and feeding supplies together and within reach.

### For toddlers

Toddlers need more variety because their attention spans are short. Pack a few familiar activities and a good supply of snacks. They also hold on tightly to a comfort item, so make sure it never leaves your personal bag.

## How to organize the bag

This part is my own advice, and it's what makes the kit work in practice.

1. **Use your personal item as the kit.** Keep the snacks, comfort item, and activities in the bag that fits under the seat in front of you, not the overhead bin.
2. **Pack in the order you'll need things.** Put the comfort item and a snack on top. You'll want them first, often during boarding.
3. **Pre-sort by category.** One pouch for snacks, one for activities. You can grab what you need one-handed.
4. **Do a home test.** Check that everything in the bag is something your child actually likes. This follows directly from the flight attendants' familiarity rule.

## If you only have five minutes

Pack three things: a snack your child loves, their comfort item, and one familiar activity. That covers all three categories in the flight attendants' advice, and it's far better than a bag full of untested novelties.

## Why this approach works

The appeal of this advice is that it doesn't ask you to buy anything or learn a trick. Meltdowns on planes often come from a child being overwhelmed, and familiar things lower that stress. It also helps the people around you, since a calmer child makes for a quieter cabin.

Before your next flight, set out your child's favorite snack, comfort item, and activity on the kitchen counter the night before. If it's already theirs, it belongs in the bag.
