Holiday airfare has reached a 10-year high, per Yahoo's reporting: round-trip tickets for Thanksgiving average $402, and Christmas averages $452. Before you assume the car is the cheaper answer, run your own numbers. This guide gives you a way to compare the two honestly, including the costs people forget, and tells you which kinds of trips tend to favor each option.

The short version: driving usually wins as the number of travelers grows, and flying usually wins as the distance grows. Where your trip falls between those two points is what you're about to figure out.

## Start With the Benchmark Prices

Per Yahoo, the averages to beat are:

- **Thanksgiving:** $402 round trip, per ticket
- **Christmas:** $452 round trip, per ticket

These are averages, so your route may come in higher or lower. Treat them as a reference point, then pull up real fares for your dates and airports. Multiply by the number of people traveling. A family of four at those averages is looking at roughly $1,608 for Thanksgiving and $1,808 for Christmas in tickets alone, before bags or getting to the airport. That's simple arithmetic on the reported averages, not a quote for any specific route.

## Build Your Flying Total

Ticket price is only the first line. Add:

1. **Fares for everyone** in your group.
2. **Checked bags**, if your airline charges for them and you're bringing gifts.
3. **Getting to and from the airport**: rideshare, parking, or a ride from a relative.
4. **Ground transportation at your destination**, such as a rental car if no one can pick you up.
5. **Airport food**, which is rarely cheap.

Write down the total. That's your number to beat.

## Build Your Driving Total

This is where people undercount. Gas is the obvious cost, and Yahoo notes that holiday travel costs are rising across flights, gas, and road expenses. Include:

1. **Fuel**: round-trip miles divided by your car's miles per gallon, times the current price per gallon.
2. **Tolls** along your route.
3. **Food on the road**: meals and snacks add up over a long drive, especially with kids.
4. **Lodging**, if the drive is too long for one day.
5. **Wear and tear**: a long round trip adds miles to your car. Even a rough allowance keeps you honest.
6. **Parking or other costs** at your destination.

If you'd need an overnight stop each way, add two nights of lodging. That one line can erase the savings of driving.

## When Driving Usually Wins

Driving tends to come out ahead when:

- **You're traveling as a family or group.** One tank of gas covers everyone, while airfare multiplies per person.
- **The trip is a day's drive or less.** No hotel, fewer meals, less hassle.
- **You're bringing a lot.** Gifts, a pie, a cooler, and a pack-and-play fit in a trunk without bag fees.
- **You need a car at your destination anyway.** Driving removes the rental cost.

## When Flying Still Makes Sense

Flying can be the smarter choice when:

- **You're traveling solo.** A single ticket may cost less than a long drive's fuel, lodging, and food combined.
- **The drive would require overnight stays.** Hotels can push the road total past the airfare.
- **Your time off is short.** If a drive eats a day on each end, the extra vacation days have value too.
- **You can travel light and get a ride at the other end.** That keeps the flying total close to the ticket price.

## Solo Travelers vs. Families

The same fare changes meaning depending on who's in the car. A solo traveler pays $402 or $452 once at the reported averages. A family of four pays four times that. Cars don't scale that way: adding a passenger costs almost nothing in fuel. For couples, the answer often sits in the middle, so do the math instead of guessing.

## Count the Time and Weather Tradeoffs

Yahoo's coverage frames this as a financial and practical decision, and the practical side matters. Driving means hours behind the wheel on busy holiday roads, and winter weather can slow a route or force a change of plans. Flying means airport time and the risk of delays. Give each option a realistic travel-day estimate, and build in a buffer either way.

A useful test: if driving saves you less than the value of the extra time and stress, fly. If flying costs you hundreds more for a trip you could finish in a few hours behind the wheel, drive.

## Ways to Trim the Bill Either Way

- **Compare your own dates.** If you can shift a departure or return day, check the fares around it.
- **Pack smart.** Carry-on only avoids bag fees; a full trunk avoids them too.
- **Bring food.** Snacks and water for the car, or an empty bottle and snacks for the airport, cut impulse spending.
- **Split the driving costs.** If you're heading to the same gathering as a relative, carpooling cuts fuel and tolls per household.
- **Plan stops ahead.** If you need a hotel, decide where before prices and availability tighten.

## A Quick Worksheet

On a piece of paper, make two columns, Fly and Drive. Fill in every line above, total each column, then add a note about time and weather risk. If the totals are within about $100 of each other, choose the option that makes the trip easier. If one is far cheaper, you have your answer.

## Your Next Step

Before you book anything, spend ten minutes filling out both columns with real numbers for your route. The reported averages show that flying has gotten expensive, but only your own total tells you whether the car is actually the bargain.
