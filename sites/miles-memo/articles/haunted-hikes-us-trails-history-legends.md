Afar recently rounded up spooky U.S. hikes where ghost stories, abandoned settlements, wartime ruins, and local legends run alongside scenic trails. The set covers eight trails in the original; the details below cover five of them. Pick the one that fits your region, and you have the start of a trip built on history as much as chills.

The common thread is worth taking seriously: these hikes work whether or not you believe in ghosts. You get woods, quiet, and a story to chew on. Afar's pitch is no civilization and no phone signal, just you and those strange sounds in the woods.

## Before You Pick a Trail

A few planning notes apply to all five:

- **A car helps.** These are trailheads in state forests, parks, and preserves, not places you reach easily by transit. Plan on driving to each.
- **Download maps first.** If you lose signal, you still need to find your way back.
- **Check conditions before you go.** Hours, seasonal closures, parking, and any fees or reservation rules vary by park. Confirm with the managing agency (state park, national forest, or regional preserve) before you leave, since I can't vouch for current details here.
- **Go in daylight.** Atmosphere is better when you can see your footing, and you'll want time to read any signs along the way.

These suit curious hikers, history fans, and anyone who wants a fall outing that isn't a theme-park haunted house. Pairing one trail with a nearby town for lunch makes a solid day trip.

## Moonville Tunnel Rail Trail, Zaleski State Forest, Ohio

This one is the classic: a rail trail that leads to a haunted tunnel. A rail trail generally means a gentler, flatter route, so it's a reasonable pick if you want atmosphere without a serious climb. Zaleski State Forest gives you the woodland setting.

**Best for:** casual walkers and first-time haunted hikers who want the tunnel as the payoff.

## Fort Stevens State Park, Oregon

Fort Stevens offers three named routes: Fort Stevens, Jetty Loop, and Fort Stevens Ridge Trail. The draw here is wartime ruins rather than a single ghost story, so the spooky feeling comes from walking through military history on the Oregon coast.

**How to use it:** with several trails in one park, you can scale the day. Take one loop if you're short on time, or link more than one if you want a longer outing.

**Best for:** history-minded hikers who like ruins and a coastal setting.

## Babson Reservoir Dogtown Loop Trail, Dogtown Common, Massachusetts

Dogtown Common is the abandoned-settlement entry on the list. The loop starts at Babson Reservoir and moves through land with a strange past, which is exactly the kind of place where local legends take hold. Read up on the area's history before you go; the story makes the walk.

**Best for:** hikers who like a quiet, story-driven walk in New England woods.

## Massacre Falls Trail, Tonto National Forest, Arizona

The name alone sets the mood, and Tonto National Forest provides the desert-country backdrop. This is the entry to approach with some care and respect: the name points to dark history, so treat the legends as local lore and the history as something to read about from reliable sources.

**Best for:** travelers who want a Southwest hike with a heavier backstory. Bring plenty of water, as you would for any Arizona trail.

## Nortonville and Black Diamond Loop, Black Diamond Mines Regional Preserve, California

This loop passes through the remains of a former settlement inside a regional preserve. It's a good example of Afar's larger point: haunted hikes are often really about the people who lived and worked somewhere and then left.

**Best for:** Californians and Bay Area visitors who want an easy-to-frame day trip with history built in.

## How to Choose Between Them

- **Want the most traditional ghost story?** Moonville Tunnel in Ohio.
- **Want ruins and ocean air?** Fort Stevens in Oregon.
- **Want an eerie, abandoned-place mood?** Dogtown Common in Massachusetts or Nortonville in California.
- **Want a Southwest trail with a dark name?** Massacre Falls in Arizona.

If you only have a half day, choose a single trail and give yourself time to read about its history beforehand. If you have a weekend, the Oregon park's multiple routes make it the easiest to stretch.

## Make It Better

Tell someone your route and return time. Carry a headlamp even on a daytime hike, in case you linger. And before you set out, read the real history of the place you're visiting. The legend is more fun when you know what's behind it.

Pick one trail, check the park's current conditions, and put a date on the calendar before the leaves are gone.
