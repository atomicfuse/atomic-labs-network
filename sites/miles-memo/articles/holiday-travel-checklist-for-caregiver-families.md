Holiday travel is stressful for most people. For a person living with dementia, crowds, schedule changes and unfamiliar places can be overwhelming, and the caregiver carries much of that load. This checklist helps you plan the trip in four stages: before you go, what to pack, how to work with airlines, trains and other transportation providers, and how to keep days feeling familiar once you arrive.

The framework comes from tips the Alzheimer's Foundation of America shared, as reported by WNCT. Per that report, the guidance centers on planning ahead, packing thoughtfully, coordinating with transportation providers, and keeping routines as familiar as possible. The specific suggestions below are my own practical ideas built on those four themes, and they are not a substitute for advice from your loved one's doctor.

## Who This Suits and How Much Lead Time You Need

This approach fits any family or caregiver bringing a person with dementia along for a Thanksgiving, December holiday or New Year's trip, whether by plane, train or car. Start planning as early as you can. Several steps, such as calling a transportation provider or talking with a doctor, take a few days to resolve, and you want time to adjust the plan if something doesn't work.

A car gives you the most control over timing, stops and surroundings. Flying or taking a train can still work, but it requires more coordination, which is where the planning steps below earn their keep.

## Step 1: Plan Ahead Before Anything Is Booked

Planning is the first theme in the AFA guidance, and it starts with an honest look at the trip itself. Ask yourself a few questions:

- **Is the destination the right one?** A quiet stay with a few familiar relatives may go better than a packed house of guests.
- **How long is the travel day?** Shorter and simpler is usually easier. If you have the option, choose itineraries with fewer connections.
- **Who is the second adult?** Where you can, bring another person so the caregiver isn't managing luggage, tickets and a loved one alone.
- **Where will they sleep?** Think about whether the bedroom, bathroom and stairs at your destination are safe and easy to navigate.

It also helps to talk with your loved one's doctor before you go, especially about medications and how to handle any changes in routine.

## Step 2: Pack a Caregiver Bag, Not Just a Suitcase

The AFA tips emphasize packing thoughtfully. Keep a small carry-on or tote that stays with you the entire trip, separate from checked luggage. Consider including:

- Medications in their original containers, plus a current medication list
- Contact information for doctors and family members
- Comfort items your loved one recognizes, such as a favorite sweater or blanket
- Familiar snacks and a water bottle
- A change of clothes, in case of spills or accidents
- Simple activities they enjoy, like photos, music or a puzzle

The logic is straightforward. If a bag gets delayed or a plan falls apart, the essentials and the comforting items are still within reach.

## Step 3: Coordinate With Airlines, Trains and Other Providers

Another AFA theme is coordinating with transportation providers. Call ahead rather than explaining your situation at a crowded gate. Ask what assistance is available, how boarding works, and whether there are quieter options for waiting. Airports and train stations are loud, busy and full of announcements, so knowing in advance where you'll wait and how long can make a real difference.

A few habits tend to help in transit:

- Arrive with plenty of buffer time so no one is rushed.
- Keep tickets, IDs and documents in one easy-to-reach place.
- Tell your loved one what is happening next in short, calm sentences.
- Plan restroom and snack stops before they're urgent.

I'd suggest confirming specific services directly with each provider, since offerings vary and I can't verify what any particular airline or rail line offers.

## Step 4: Driving? Build Stops Into the Plan

If you're going by car, the same logic applies: reduce stress and fatigue by giving the day room. Break the drive into segments, schedule breaks for stretching and meals, and avoid pushing through when your loved one is tired. A familiar playlist or a few favorite snacks in reach can keep the mood steady. Sit them where they're comfortable and where you can easily check in.

If the drive is long, consider whether it makes more sense to split it over two days instead of one exhausting stretch.

## Step 5: Keep the Routine Familiar When You Arrive

Keeping routines as familiar as possible is the AFA's fourth theme, and it matters most once you reach your destination. The holidays tempt everyone toward late dinners, packed schedules and a stream of visitors. Try holding on to the anchors of a normal day:

- Meals, medications and bedtime at their usual times
- A morning and evening ritual your loved one knows
- A quiet space where they can retreat when things get loud
- A short list of what's planned each day, with plenty of open time

You can also give relatives a heads-up before the visit. A quick explanation of what helps, such as introducing themselves, speaking one at a time and keeping gatherings short, makes it easier for everyone to be supportive.

## Step 6: Plan the Return, Too

The trip isn't over when you arrive home. Build in a day to rest and settle back into regular routines before anything else is scheduled, and check in with your loved one's doctor if you notice changes you're concerned about.

## The One Thing to Do Today

Before you book anything, write down your loved one's normal daily routine, then circle the parts you most want to protect on the trip. Plan the travel days and the destination schedule around those anchors, and the rest of the checklist gets much easier to follow.
