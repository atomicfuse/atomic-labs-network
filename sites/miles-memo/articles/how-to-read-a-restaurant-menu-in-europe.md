You can usually tell whether a restaurant is worth your money before you sit down. The menu is posted outside, and it says a lot. This guide shows you how to read it in about 30 seconds, so you spend less time at overpriced tourist tables and more time eating food locals would order.

The idea comes from Rick Steves, whose approach to eating in Europe was recently highlighted by Food & Wine. The core of it is simple: skip tourist-trap menus and look for restaurants that cater to locals. Per the coverage, the menu's design, language, and size can reveal whether a place offers authentic, worthwhile food.

## Who this helps, and when

This is for anyone eating their way through a European trip, whether it's a three-day city break or a longer route. It costs you no extra time and needs no car, no reservation system, and no research beyond what you can see from the sidewalk. It matters most in crowded destinations, where restaurants aimed squarely at visitors tend to dominate the busiest streets.

## The rule in one sentence

Eat where the restaurant is clearly trying to please local customers, not passing tourists. Everything else here is a way to spot the difference.

Food & Wine points to three signals: how the menu is designed, what language it's in, and how big it is. The article, as summarized, doesn't hand over a rigid scorecard. What follows is my own reading of those three signals, so treat it as a practical starting point rather than Steves' exact words.

## Check the menu before you check the seat

### Language

A menu written mainly for visitors tells you who the restaurant is courting. A menu in the local language, even with translations alongside, suggests a kitchen that also serves people who live nearby. Don't be scared off by not understanding it. A translation app on your phone can handle a dish list in seconds, and a menu you have to work a little to read is often a good sign.

### Size

Menu length is a clue because it hints at how the kitchen works. My take: a huge menu that covers every cuisine and every craving is hard for any single kitchen to do well. A tighter menu tends to suggest a place focused on a few things it can do properly. If a menu runs for pages, ask yourself how much of it could possibly be made fresh.

### Design

The way a menu looks is part of the pitch. Menus built to catch the eye of passersby, rather than to simply list what's cooking, are aimed at people who don't know the place. A plainer menu that reads like a list of what the kitchen is making today is more likely aimed at regulars. Design alone isn't proof, but combined with language and size it adds up.

## A 30-second sidewalk check

Use this before you commit to a table:

1. **Read the language.** Is the menu primarily in the local language, or is it built for visitors?
2. **Scan the length.** Does it look focused or endless?
3. **Judge the design.** Does it read like a working menu or a sales pitch?
4. **Look at who's eating.** My own addition: if the tables are full of people who seem to know the staff and the routine, that's worth weighing alongside the menu.

If a place fails two or three of these, keep walking. You rarely have to go far.

## Build it into your itinerary

Good food shouldn't cost you a half day of searching, so plan around it:

- **Walk a few blocks off the main sights.** Restaurants right beside major landmarks are the ones most likely to be built around visitors. Moving a street or two away gives you more local-facing options. This is general planning advice, not something drawn from the Steves coverage.
- **Pick your meal spot while you're already out.** Instead of deciding when you're hungry and tired, make a note of one or two promising menus as you sightsee, then circle back.
- **Have a fallback.** If you're short on time, apply the three-signal check to the first two or three places you pass and take the best of them.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Choosing the restaurant with the best view first and the menu second.** The setting can be lovely, but check the menu before you sit.
- **Treating a translated menu as a deal-breaker.** Having English alongside local-language text isn't automatically bad. What matters is whether the place seems to serve locals too.
- **Ignoring the size of the menu because the dishes sound familiar.** A long list of crowd-pleasers can be a warning sign rather than a comfort.

## What this rule can't do

No menu check guarantees a great meal. A modest-looking place can be mediocre, and a polished one can surprise you. The point of the rule is to improve your odds, not remove all risk. If you want certainty, ask your hotel or a local shopkeeper where they eat. That's my suggestion, not Steves', but it pairs well with the same principle.

## The takeaway

Before your next meal in Europe, stop at the door and read the menu for three things: its language, its size, and its design. If it looks built for locals, go in. If it looks built for you, keep walking.
