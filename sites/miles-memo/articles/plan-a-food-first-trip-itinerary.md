Most trip planning starts with sights and fits meals in around them. A food-first trip flips that: you pick the meals, markets, and cooks you want to experience, then build the days around them. Here is a simple way to do it, whether you have a weekend or a week.

The idea comes from a recent Travel Weekly feature on culinary travel, which frames dining as a way to turn a trip into lasting memories and a way for travelers to connect with a destination. The publication points to a broader shift toward immersive, experience-led trips over passive sightseeing, with food as an accessible entry point into local culture, identity, and storytelling. What follows is my own planning approach built on that premise, not a set of findings from the article.

## Start With the Planning Basics

Before you pick a single restaurant, settle three things:

- **Time:** A food-first trip works well over two or three days. That is enough for a market, a standout meal, and a hands-on experience without rushing between reservations.
- **Getting around:** Choose a base where you can walk to a market and a few places to eat. If your best meals are spread across a region, a car helps. If they cluster in one city, skip it and save the parking stress.
- **Who it suits:** Anyone who likes eating, including first-time food tourists. You do not need a refined palate, just curiosity and a little appetite.

## Pick Your Anchors First

The core of a food-first itinerary is a short list of anchors. Travel Weekly's coverage points to the building blocks: meals, markets, chefs, cooking classes, and regional specialties. Choose one or two per day, not five.

- **A market visit** gives you a look at what locals buy and eat. Go in the morning when it is busiest, and graze instead of committing to a sit-down meal.
- **A cooking class** turns a dish into a skill you take home. It also puts you in a room with someone who can explain why the dish is made the way it is.
- **A chef-led or signature meal** is your splurge. Book it first and plan the rest of the day around it.
- **A regional specialty** is the thing the place is known for. Make a point of eating it at least once, ideally somewhere a local recommended.

Verify hours, seasonal closures, and reservation requirements directly with each market, school, or restaurant before you lock plans in. Small food businesses change schedules often, and classes and popular tables can fill well ahead.

## A Sample Three-Day Rhythm

This template is a framework you can adapt to any city or region.

### Day one: Orient yourself through food

Arrive, drop your bags, and head to a market. Eat a light lunch there, then walk the neighborhood. Keep dinner casual and close to your lodging, ideally somewhere serving the regional specialty so you start with the essentials.

### Day two: Go deep

This is your big day. Take a morning cooking class or market-and-cook experience, then leave the afternoon open for a rest or a wander. Book your signature meal for the evening. Having a quiet afternoon between two big food moments keeps you enjoying dinner instead of enduring it.

### Day three: Revisit and bring something home

Return to the place you liked best, whether a stall, a bakery, or a café, and order what you skipped the first time. If you have a few hours left, shop for a local ingredient or two to carry home.

### If you only have one day

Pick one market in the morning and one memorable meal at night. Everything else is optional.

## Find Authentic Places Without Overthinking It

Authenticity is hard to define, but a few habits help:

- Ask the people you meet, such as your host, a market vendor, or a class instructor, where they eat on an ordinary day.
- Eat where the menu is focused. A short menu centered on a regional dish usually beats a long one trying to please everyone.
- Start at the market. Vendors are often happy to point you toward a favorite counter or a place that cooks what they sell.
- Take a class. Instructors tend to have strong opinions about where to eat, and that insight is hard to get from a list online.

## Why the Meals Stick

Food engages every sense, and it is tied to people: the cook who explains a recipe, the vendor who hands you a sample, the table you share with strangers. That personal thread is what Travel Weekly's framing gets at when it talks about identity and storytelling. A cathedral is something you look at. A meal is something you take part in, and that tends to be what you retell later.

## Tips for First-Time Food Tourists

- **Do not overschedule.** Two anchors a day is plenty. Leave room to follow a smell or a recommendation.
- **Eat smaller portions more often.** Sharing plates and sampling at markets lets you taste more without hitting a wall by mid-afternoon.
- **Write it down.** Jot the dish, the place, and one detail about who made it. Photos fade; a name and a note help you recreate it at home.
- **Be flexible with your list.** If a local points you somewhere better than your plan, go.

## Your Next Step

Pick one destination, then choose a single anchor: a market, a class, or a meal you would be sorry to miss. Book that first, and let the rest of the trip fill in around it.
