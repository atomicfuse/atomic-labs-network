Euronews recently ran a roundup of food and drink tours it considers worth travelling for. The full list isn't something we can reproduce here, so we won't pretend to hand you its picks. What we can do is more useful: give you a way to decide whether any food tour, in any destination, deserves to be the reason you book a trip.

By the end, you'll know how to match a food-led trip to your tastes and budget, how much time to give it, and what to check before you pay.

## Why food-led trips are worth planning properly

Per the Euronews piece, culinary tourism has become a major driver of travel decisions, as people look for immersive, experience-led holidays beyond sightseeing. Food and drink tours also appeal to travellers who want local culture, sustainability and authentic regional storytelling.

That's a good reason to plan with care. A tour built around a region's food can tell you more about a place in three hours than a day of landmarks. But a poorly chosen one is an expensive, crowded snack break.

## Step 1: Pick the type of trip that matches how you like to eat

Start with what you actually enjoy, not what's famous. Three broad categories cover most food-led travel:

### Wine regions

Best for travellers who like a slower pace, scenery and a long lunch. Plan for fewer stops per day, not more. A car helps in most rural wine areas, but then someone has to stay sober. A guided tour with transport solves that.

### Beer and spirits trips

These often work well in cities, where breweries or distilleries cluster close together and you can walk or use transit. They suit shorter weekends and groups with different budgets.

### Street food and market cities

Best for curious, flexible eaters on a modest budget. You'll be on foot, so comfortable shoes matter more than any booking.

## Step 2: Decide how much time you need

A practical rule of thumb from our side, not from Euronews: give a food tour its own half-day, and don't stack a second big activity behind it.

- **If you have one day:** Book one tour, ideally late morning or midday, and leave the evening open for a dinner you choose yourself.
- **If you have a weekend:** Do one guided tour on day one to get your bearings, then spend day two eating independently using what the guide told you.
- **If you have three days or more:** Add a second type of experience, such as a market visit or a cooking class, so the trip doesn't become one long tasting.

Travel time counts. If the tour is a drive from your base, factor that in so you aren't rushing a tasting to catch a train.

## Step 3: Test whether a tour is worth the journey

Not every tour earns a trip. Before you book, check:

1. **Is it rooted in place?** A good tour explains why the food belongs to the region: the ingredients, the producers, the history. If the description could apply to any city, keep looking.
2. **Is the group size stated?** Small groups mean you can ask questions and actually taste things. Large groups often mean queueing.
3. **What's included?** Confirm how many tastings or stops you get, and whether drinks, transport and a meal are in the price.
4. **Does it meet dietary needs?** Ask in advance about allergies, vegetarian options and alcohol-free alternatives.
5. **Can you talk to the producers?** Meeting the people who make the food is usually what separates a memorable tour from a forgettable one.
6. **Are there recent reviews?** Look for specifics, such as the guide's name or what people ate, rather than general praise.

## Step 4: Build the trip around the tour

Once you've chosen the experience, the rest of the trip should support it.

### Choose a base close to the action

Stay somewhere you can reach the tour start point easily, ideally on foot or a short ride. In wine country, a base within a short transfer saves you from driving after tastings.

### Keep the schedule loose

Tours run long. Leave a gap afterward instead of booking a museum slot an hour later. Eating and drinking well takes the energy out of a packed itinerary.

### Book the essentials early

Reserve the tour itself, and check opening days, seasonal closures and reservation requirements directly with the operator. Producers and small venues often shorten hours or close outside peak season, so confirm before you commit to dates.

## Step 5: Set a realistic budget

Prices vary widely by destination and tour type, so rather than guess a figure, work out the full cost:

- Tour price (and whether tastings or meals are included)
- Transport to and from the start point
- A tip, if customary where you're going
- Extra bottles, cheese or ingredients you'll be tempted to buy
- One unplanned meal, because you'll probably want to repeat your favourite dish

A lower-cost option is to pair a single paid tour with self-guided eating at markets and local counters afterward.

## Who this kind of trip suits

Food-led travel works well for first-timers who want a structured introduction to a place, couples who like shared experiences, and solo travellers who want company at a table. It's less ideal if you dislike group activities, have strict dietary limits that the operator can't accommodate, or prefer to spend the day outdoors and active.

## Book the tour first, then everything else

If a food or drink experience is the reason you're going, treat it as the anchor. Choose the tour that fits your taste and your budget, confirm the details with the operator, and only then book flights and a place to stay around it.
