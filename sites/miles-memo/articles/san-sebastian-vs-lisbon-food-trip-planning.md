Ask six chefs and travel experts where they most love to eat, and you might expect six different answers. Travel + Leisure did exactly that, and per the outlet, they all landed on the same place: the Iberian Peninsula. San Sebastián and Lisbon were the two cities singled out.

That's useful if you're planning a food trip, but it leaves a real question. You probably have time for one city, maybe two. This guide helps you decide which one fits how you like to travel, and how to plan around that choice.

One caveat up front: Travel + Leisure's report points to these two cities as standout food destinations, but I'm working from that high-level pick rather than a full list of restaurant names or dishes. So treat what follows as a planning framework, and verify hours, seasonal closures and reservation requirements for any specific restaurant before you book.

## Why the Iberian Peninsula keeps winning over food people

The appeal, as Travel + Leisure frames it, is a combination of acclaimed restaurants, markets, coastal flavors and food discovery that's accessible to visitors. That mix matters. Some food destinations are great if you can score a hard-to-get table, and thin if you can't. Others are casual and delicious but offer little to build a trip around. The experts' pick suggests Iberia delivers at both ends.

San Sebastián and Lisbon sit in different countries (Spain and Portugal), so you're choosing between two distinct food cultures, not two versions of the same thing.

## How to choose: a quick decision guide

These are my own suggestions for matching a city to a traveler, built on the general strengths Travel + Leisure highlights: restaurants, markets and coastal food.

### Pick San Sebastián if...

- Food is the main reason you're going and you want the trip organized around meals.
- You like a compact destination where you can settle in rather than sightsee constantly.
- You're happy to plan ahead, since popular restaurants anywhere tend to need advance booking.

### Pick Lisbon if...

- You want food to be one strong thread in a broader city trip.
- You like exploring markets and neighborhoods on foot and eating as you go.
- You prefer a trip with room to improvise.

If you're still torn, a coastal setting is part of the draw in both cases, so seafood-minded travelers have reason to be happy either way.

## How much time you need

For a single city, three full days is a realistic minimum for a food-first trip. That gives you roughly nine meals and snacks to work with, plus a buffer for a long lunch that runs late or a reservation that falls through.

If you only have two days, go with one anchor meal per day (a sit-down lunch or dinner you've booked) and fill the rest with markets and casual stops. Don't try to book two big meals in a day. You'll rush the first and skip the second.

If you want both cities, give yourself at least a week. Flying or traveling between them eats a day, and rushing is the fastest way to turn a food trip into a logistics headache.

## A sample three-day food weekend

This structure works for either city. Swap in specific places once you've confirmed what's open.

### Day 1: Start at the market

Arrive and head to a local market for a first meal. It's the quickest way to see what's in season and what locals actually eat. Keep the afternoon open for walking, and book a modest dinner rather than your showpiece reservation. Jet lag and big tasting menus don't mix.

### Day 2: Your anchor meal

This is the day for the restaurant you've been researching. Plan light around it: a coffee and pastry in the morning, a long walk in the afternoon, then the big meal. If your anchor is lunch, spend the evening on a casual wander and a small bite.

### Day 3: Eat like a local, then leave room

Spend the morning in a neighborhood you haven't visited and eat where the crowd looks local rather than where the menu is translated into six languages. Save your last meal for whatever you've loved most so far. Repeating a favorite beats chasing something new.

## Planning details that save you hassle

- **Book early for the meals that matter.** Choose one or two must-do restaurants and reserve as soon as you know your dates. Leave the rest flexible.
- **Stay central.** In a food city, a base within walking distance of markets and restaurants means you can eat well without relying on transit or taxis.
- **You likely don't need a car.** For a city-focused food trip, walking and local transit usually cover it. A car makes sense only if you add day trips.
- **Check closing days.** Restaurants and markets often close on certain days or take seasonal breaks. Confirm before you build your schedule around them.
- **Pace your appetite.** Eat a big meal, then a small one. Travelers who try to do everything usually flag by day two.

## Look past the obvious spots

The most famous restaurants are only part of why chefs love this region. A good rule: for every headline meal, schedule two unglamorous ones, such as a market snack, a coffee-and-pastry stop or a casual counter lunch. That's where you learn what the city actually tastes like, and it keeps your budget sane.

## The bottom line

Pick your city by travel style, not by hype: San Sebastián if the meals are the trip, Lisbon if they're part of a bigger city getaway. Then book your one anchor meal first and build the rest of the itinerary around it.
