Thanksgiving is one of the busiest travel periods in the U.S., and recent nationwide and airline-specific ground stops have travelers worried about delays and cancellations. You can't control the weather or the FAA, but you can control what you have in hand when a flight goes sideways. This guide covers what to do before you leave, during a disruption, and afterward, so you know who owes you what and what to save.

The core advice comes from a local travel agent interviewed by KOAT, and it centers on one idea: the cause of a delay determines who pays.

## Know Who Pays: Airline Delay vs. Outside Factors

This is the single most useful distinction to understand before you get to the airport. Per KOAT's reporting, coverage and airline responsibility depend on whether the delay is caused by the airline itself or by outside factors, such as FAA ground stops.

In practical terms:

- **Airline-caused problems** are the ones where you have the strongest claim to refunds or help from the carrier.
- **Outside-factor problems**, like a ground stop, are where you may be left covering costs yourself unless you have a policy that applies.

That gap is exactly what travel insurance is meant to fill. If you're unsure which category your delay falls into, ask the gate agent and write down what they tell you, including the time and the stated reason.

## Consider Travel Insurance, and Buy It Early

The travel agent KOAT spoke with explained that a policy can cover unexpected hotel, meal, and prepaid expenses when a trip is disrupted. The timing detail matters: according to the report, you can purchase a policy up to 24 hours before your flight.

That makes it a late-stage decision you can make once you've checked the forecast and the news. If your Thanksgiving plans involve a lot of money already spent, it's worth weighing:

- **Prepaid, nonrefundable costs:** hotel bookings, rental cars, tours, or event tickets at your destination.
- **A tight schedule:** a missed connection could mean a night in an airport city you hadn't planned on.
- **Travelers who'd struggle to absorb a surprise bill:** an unplanned hotel night and meals add up quickly.

Before buying, read what the policy actually covers and what causes it recognizes. Coverage terms vary, and I can't tell you what any specific policy includes, so check the details for the plan you're considering.

## Before You Leave: A Short Checklist

Do these the day before you fly:

1. **Check your flight status** and look for any airline or FAA alerts affecting your route.
2. **Decide on insurance** while the 24-hour purchase window is still open.
3. **Save your booking confirmations** in one folder on your phone, including hotel and prepaid reservations.
4. **Download the airline's app** so you can rebook or check status without waiting in a line.
5. **Screenshot your itinerary and receipts** in case you lose signal or battery.

If you're booking a Thanksgiving trip from scratch, building in slack helps. An earlier flight gives you more room to recover if something goes wrong, though that's general planning sense rather than a point from the KOAT report.

## During a Disruption: Document Everything

The reporting highlights documenting expenses, and it's the habit that makes reimbursement possible. When your flight is delayed or canceled:

- **Keep every receipt** for meals, hotels, and transportation you pay for because of the delay.
- **Note the reason given** for the disruption and who told you, with the time.
- **Photograph departure boards** showing delays or cancellations.
- **Save all messages** from the airline, including texts, emails, and app notifications.

This matters whether you're filing an insurance claim or asking the airline for a refund. A claim with a clear paper trail is far easier to support than one built from memory.

## Ask About Refunds Before You Accept a Rebooking

The KOAT piece points travelers toward understanding their refund rights. Before agreeing to a new flight you don't want, ask the airline what your options are. If the airline caused the disruption, your position is generally stronger. If a ground stop caused it, expect the conversation to be different, and lean on your documentation and any insurance policy.

A simple script works fine at the counter or on the phone:

- "What is the reason for the delay or cancellation?"
- "What are my options for rebooking or a refund?"
- "Is there any assistance with meals or lodging for this situation?"

Write down the answers. Polite, specific questions tend to get clearer replies than general frustration.

## After the Trip: File Promptly

Once you're home, gather your receipts, screenshots, and notes into one place and submit your claims. If you bought a policy, follow its claim process. If you're requesting a refund from the airline, include the dates, flight numbers, and the reason you were given for the disruption. Doing it while the details are fresh saves you from reconstructing a messy timeline weeks later.

## The Takeaway

Before you head to the airport, decide on travel insurance while the 24-hour window is open, then put every confirmation in one folder. If something goes wrong, keep every receipt and write down what the airline tells you about the cause, because that cause decides who pays.
