Italy rewards the traveller who does less. That's the core idea behind a list of 15 tips from Maria Pasquale, author of *How to be Italian*, published by Qantas. Her advice is about slowing down, stepping off the tourist trail and picking up the rhythms of daily life. This article takes her ideas and turns them into planning decisions you can make before you book: how long to stay, how to get around, and what to build into each day.

The source doesn't give specific towns, prices or opening hours, so treat what follows as a framework. Check hours, seasonal closures and reservation rules for any place you plan to visit.

## Plan the Trip Around Fewer Places

Pasquale's first tip is to slow down your itinerary, and her twelfth is to avoid over-planning every hour. Together they point to one planning rule: pick fewer bases and stay longer in each.

A practical way to apply it:

- **Ten days or more:** Choose two or three bases rather than five or six.
- **A week:** Choose two bases, or one base with a couple of day trips.
- **A long weekend:** Choose one city and resist the urge to add another.

Leave at least one part of each day unscheduled. Anything you want to see goes in the morning or afternoon; the rest is for wandering, sitting down, and changing your mind.

## Swap One Hotspot Detour per Stop

Pasquale recommends taking detours from tourist hotspots and exploring beyond the big cities. Her brief context is overtourism, which is reshaping popular destinations like Rome, Venice and Florence. You don't need to skip the icons. You just need to balance them.

Try a simple swap for each major city on your route:

1. See the headline sight you came for, ideally at the start of the day.
2. Spend the rest of the day in a residential neighbourhood, not another landmark.
3. Add one smaller town or less-visited area to your trip instead of a fourth big city.

This keeps the highlights while spreading your time and money beyond the busiest routes.

## Use Trains Where You Can

Pasquale lists travelling by train where possible. For most itineraries linking larger towns, it fits her slow-travel approach: you watch the landscape instead of managing traffic and parking. A car may still help for rural areas, but let the train be your default between cities. Check timetables and any booking requirements before you travel.

## Choose a Base You Can Walk Around

Her third tip is to spend time in local neighbourhoods. That's easier when your accommodation sits in a lived-in area rather than beside the main attractions. When comparing places to stay, look for:

- a morning café and a few small food shops within walking distance
- a market nearby (Pasquale recommends visiting markets and small food shops)
- a residential feel, so evening life revolves around locals, not tour groups

Spend your first morning walking the immediate area with no agenda. It pays off for the rest of the stay.

## Eat on Italian Time

Two of Pasquale's tips, following Italian dining rhythms and choosing regional dishes, shape how you plan meals.

- **Match local timing.** Don't fight the local schedule. Plan around when Italians eat rather than when you'd eat at home.
- **Order what's local.** Pick regional dishes over generic favourites, and ask what the area is known for.
- **Shop small.** Markets and small food shops are good for picnic lunches and for seeing what's in season.

Build meals into the itinerary as real events, not gaps between sights.

## Make Time for Coffee and the Passeggiata

Coffee culture and the passeggiata both made her list, and neither costs much or needs a ticket. The passeggiata is the habit of an unhurried evening stroll. Block out the early evening for it. Walk, people-watch, then decide where to eat.

For coffee, treat it as a pause worth taking, not something to rush through on the way to the next stop. Both habits are also the simplest way to follow her tip about connecting with locals through daily rituals: you're sharing their routines rather than watching from outside.

## Learn a Few Phrases and Respect Local Customs

Pasquale suggests learning a few Italian phrases and respecting local customs. She doesn't specify which ones in the material available here, so start with the basics: greetings, thank you, and how to order. Before you go, read up on the etiquette of the region you're visiting, especially around restaurants, churches and small shops. A little effort tends to change how people respond to you.

## A Sample Slow Day

Here's one way to combine the tips into a single day in a city:

- **Morning:** Visit your one main sight, then stop for coffee nearby.
- **Midday:** Browse a market or small food shop and eat a regional dish.
- **Afternoon:** Leave open. Walk a neighbourhood you haven't seen, or rest.
- **Early evening:** Take a passeggiata.
- **Evening:** Eat dinner at local timing, with no second activity afterwards.

**Short on time?** Keep the morning sight, the regional meal and the evening walk. Drop everything else.

## Pasquale's Final Tip

Her fifteenth tip is simply to do as the Romans do. It works as a filter for every decision: would a local rush this, queue for it, or skip it entirely?

Before you book anything, cut your draft itinerary by a third. Then spend the freed-up hours on coffee, a market and an evening walk.
