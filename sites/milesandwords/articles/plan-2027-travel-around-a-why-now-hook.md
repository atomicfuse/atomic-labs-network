The full list sits behind the original article, and the details available to us cover only three of the picks. But those three show a useful pattern: the best trips come with a reason to go *now*. This piece shows you how to borrow that logic and plan your own 2027 trip, starting today.

## What the advisors are pointing to

Per Forbes, the highlights include:

- **Destinations tied to two major sporting events.** Forbes doesn't name them in the material we have, so check the original piece for specifics.
- **A wetland most Americans can't find on a map.** An overlooked nature destination.
- **A bicultural Alpine region.** A place where two cultures share one landscape.

The other four picks aren't covered here, so we won't guess at them. What matters is the shape of the list: an event, a hidden natural place, and a region with cultural depth. Each has a clear reason to go in 2027 rather than some other year.

## Why a 'why now' hook beats a bucket list

Bucket-list destinations will still be there in 2030. A trip with a hook has a deadline, and that changes how you plan. A hook can be:

- A one-time event that reshapes a city for weeks
- A place that's still under the radar and uncrowded
- A cultural region you'd appreciate more with time to explore it slowly

If you can't say in one sentence why you're going in 2027 specifically, keep brainstorming.

## How to plan around a sporting event

Event-driven travel rewards early decisions. Here's a doable sequence:

1. **Pick the event first, then the trip.** Decide whether you're going to watch, or just visiting the host region while it's buzzing.
2. **Check the schedule as soon as it's published.** Dates drive everything else: flights, hotels, and what else you can fit around the event.
3. **Book flexible lodging early.** Look for rooms with free cancellation so you can lock in a spot without committing fully.
4. **Build in days on either side.** Spend time in the region before or after the event, when it's easier to enjoy the destination itself.
5. **Have a plan B for tickets.** Decide in advance what you'd do if you can't get into the event itself.

A trip that depends on one ticket is fragile. A trip that uses the event as an anchor and the region as the main course holds up better.

## How to approach an underrated nature destination

A wetland most Americans can't place on a map is, by definition, not set up like a headline attraction. Expect to do more homework:

- **Work out access first.** Lesser-known places often have fewer flights and limited transfers, so how you get there may shape the whole itinerary.
- **Ask about the best season.** Wetlands and wildlife areas can change dramatically through the year. A local guide or an advisor can tell you when to go.
- **Hire local expertise.** In places without big signage and crowds, a guide is often the difference between seeing something and walking past it.
- **Pair it with a bigger hub.** Add a few days in a well-connected city for easier logistics.

This is where a travel advisor earns their fee: hard-to-reach places are hard to book alone.

## How to do a bicultural Alpine region well

Forbes highlights a bicultural Alpine region, which suggests a place where two languages and traditions overlap. You can get more out of a spot like that by treating the culture as part of the itinerary, not background scenery.

- **Eat locally on purpose.** Menus in a two-culture region often show both influences, so order what you can't get at home.
- **Learn a few phrases in both languages.** Even a greeting goes a long way.
- **Visit outside the busiest weekends.** You'll see more daily life and fewer tour groups.
- **Mix outdoor days with town days.** Hike or ski, then spend time in villages where the cultural blend is most visible.

## Should you use a travel advisor?

These picks come from advisors, so it's fair to ask whether you need one. A few cases where it makes sense:

- You're planning a multi-stop trip around a major event
- The destination has limited flights or lodging
- You'd rather spend time enjoying the trip than researching it

If you go it alone, you can still copy their method: pick a hook, book the hard parts first, and leave room for the unplanned.
