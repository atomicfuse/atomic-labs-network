Political noise can crowd out everything else: your mood, your relationships, your sense of who you are. A recent opinion piece in Deseret by pastor and theologian Derwin L. Gray offers a useful lens, even if you read it purely as a question of personal identity. You can leave this article with three concrete habits for holding political convictions without letting a party define you.

Gray's argument is aimed at Christians. Per the Deseret piece, he says churches should put allegiance to Jesus Christ ahead of political parties and candidates. He points to early Christians, who proclaimed "Jesus is Lord" inside the Roman Empire, as a model of loyalty that no political movement could claim. Whatever your beliefs, the underlying question travels well: what sits at the center of your identity, and who or what is allowed to take its place?

## Notice when a party has become your identity

Gray's concern, as summarized by Deseret, is that many people conflate their faith identity with party affiliation. You can see the secular version of this in your own life. If a headline about "the other side" changes your whole afternoon, or if you'd struggle to describe yourself without a political label, identity and politics may have fused.

Try this quick check:

- Write down five words that describe you without mentioning politics.
- Notice how long that takes, and whether it feels uncomfortable.
- Pick one of those words and spend ten minutes this week acting on it: calling a friend, cooking, walking, volunteering.

This isn't about disengaging. It's about making sure politics is something you do, not the whole of who you are.

## Tell the truth, even to your own side

Gray calls for truth-telling rather than partisan loyalty. For personal growth, that translates into a plain habit: be willing to say "my side got that wrong."

A few ways to practice:

- Before sharing a post, ask whether you'd share it if it came from the opposing camp.
- When you hear a claim that flatters your views, check it with the same scrutiny you'd give one that annoys you.
- Name one thing your preferred candidate or party does poorly. Saying it aloud loosens the grip of loyalty-as-identity.

Honesty with yourself is a mindfulness skill. It lowers the mental tax of defending positions you haven't actually examined.

## Practice love toward people you disagree with

Per Deseret, Gray also urges love for political opponents. You don't have to agree with someone to refuse to demonize them. This is the hardest of the three habits, so make it small and specific.

### Try a one-question rule

In your next political disagreement, ask one genuine question before offering your view: "What experience led you to that?" Then listen without preparing a rebuttal. You aren't conceding anything. You're treating the other person as a whole human being, which is good for your relationships and for your own nervous system.

### Watch your language

Notice when you reach for labels like "those people." Swap in a description of what a specific person actually said or did. Precision cools the temperature.

## Hold convictions without being held by them

Gray's piece pairs love of opponents with a justice-centered approach to discipleship. In other words, he isn't asking readers to be neutral or passive. Convictions matter; the point is that they should come from deeper values rather than team spirit.

You can apply the same distinction. Ask yourself:

1. Which value is driving this position: fairness, safety, freedom, compassion?
2. Would I still hold it if my party reversed course?
3. What action, beyond posting or arguing, expresses it? Voting, volunteering, donating, or showing up locally all count.

If your answer to the second question is "no," that's useful information, not a failure.

## Don't outsource your hope

The headline of Gray's op-ed is a good summary of the emotional lesson: don't wait for political leaders to save us. Waiting for a candidate or party to fix things sets your wellbeing on a calendar you don't control. Election results will come and go; your daily habits remain yours.

Build stability that doesn't depend on outcomes:

- Keep a regular practice, whether prayer, meditation, journaling, or a walk, that you do regardless of the news.
- Set a news boundary, such as checking headlines at two set times a day.
- Invest in one local, tangible good: a neighbor, a school, a food drive.

## A note on where this comes from

Gray writes from a Christian perspective, and his central claim is theological. Deseret's summary also notes that the piece draws on a 1984 Assemblies of God voter guide, but the details of its principles aren't covered here, so it's worth reading the original op-ed if you want them. You don't need to share his faith to borrow the structure: find what you hold most deeply, keep it above any party, and let it shape how you treat people who vote differently.

This week, pick one of the three habits (the five-word identity check, the one-question rule, or the news boundary) and try it before the next heated conversation finds you.
