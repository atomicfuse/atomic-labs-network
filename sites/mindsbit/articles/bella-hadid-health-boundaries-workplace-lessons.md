Bella Hadid says she is becoming more selective about work and setting firmer boundaries to protect her health, according to The Cut. You probably don't model for a living, but the logic behind her shift is one you can borrow: when you can't keep saying yes to everything, you need a filter for what earns your energy.

## What Hadid Said

Per The Cut, the supermodel opened up about managing several chronic conditions at once: Lyme disease, POTS, PMDD, and endometriosis. She described stepping back from the nonstop pace of years of modeling and choosing work more carefully. She also pushed back on public scrutiny of her appearance.

That's the full extent of what we can responsibly say about her specifics. What's useful is the pattern, because it's familiar well beyond fashion.

## The Cost of Saying Yes to Everything

In image-driven industries, availability is currency. Turning down a job can feel like handing it to someone else. The same logic runs through startups, freelancing, and on-call tech roles, where being reachable gets mistaken for being committed.

Hadid's comments land as a counterexample: ambition doesn't vanish when you get selective. It changes shape. Instead of measuring a career by volume, you measure it by what's sustainable.

## Build Your Own Boundary Filter

You don't need a celebrity schedule to do this. A simple filter works:

1. **Name your non-negotiables.** Sleep, medical appointments, recovery days. Put them on the calendar first, before work claims the gaps.
2. **Price the yes.** Before accepting, ask what it costs in energy, not just time. A two-hour meeting can cost a day if you're managing a condition that flares.
3. **Set a default response.** "Let me check my capacity and get back to you" buys you a pause and removes the pressure of answering on the spot.
4. **Review quarterly.** Boundaries aren't permanent. Look at what you agreed to, what drained you, and adjust.

## Why Invisible Illness Makes This Harder

Conditions like POTS, Lyme disease, PMDD, and endometriosis aren't obvious from the outside. People who live with that kind of illness often get judged on how they look rather than how they feel. Hadid, per The Cut, pushed back on exactly that kind of speculation about her appearance.

The practical takeaway for the rest of us: don't treat how someone looks as evidence of how they're doing. A colleague who seems fine may be spending significant effort to appear that way. Asking "what would make this easier?" does more than guessing.

## From Career Goals to Life Goals

It's our read, not Hadid's claim, that her story reflects a wider shift. Burnout, chronic illness, and boundaries are increasingly part of the conversation in celebrity culture and workplaces alike, as more public figures challenge the expectation to perform through pain.

For tech workers used to hustle culture, the reframe is worth trying. Ask not only "what's the next role?" but "what kind of week do I want to be able to live?" Goals built around the second question tend to survive a bad health year.

## Boundaries Are a Skill, Not a Luxury

It's easy to see selectivity as a privilege of the famous. Some of it is. But the underlying habit, deciding in advance what you'll protect, costs nothing to start.

Pick one commitment this week that drains you more than it returns, and decline it or reshape it. Then notice what you do with the energy you got back.
