A 1970 Chevy C10 nicknamed "Material Girl" looks like a farm truck that has seen some things. Underneath, per Autoevolution's report, it's a turbocharged 5.3-liter V8 sending around 700 horsepower to the wheels. If you've ever wondered what separates a restomod that's fun to drive from one that's merely fun to photograph, this build is a useful case study.

To be clear, this is a researched look based on Autoevolution's coverage, not a test drive. The details below are what that report supports.

## The Big Idea: Keep the Rust, Upgrade Everything Else

The C10 keeps its original patina, the weathered paint and surface wear that come with decades of use. Many builders would strip that away for a fresh coat. Here, the age is part of the look, and it sets up a sharp contrast with what the truck can do.

That contrast is the appeal. You see an old work truck, then you hear and feel something closer to a modern performance car.

**Takeaway for your own project:** decide early whether you're restoring or preserving. Patina can save paint costs, but it means the mechanical and structural work has to carry the build.

## The Powertrain: Turbo 5.3 and a Manual Gearbox

The heart of the truck is a 5.3-liter V8, a size familiar to anyone who has looked at Chevy truck engines, with a turbocharger added. Autoevolution reports roughly 700 horsepower at the wheels. Wheel horsepower is measured after drivetrain losses, so it's a more demanding figure than a crank rating.

Power goes through a Tremec six-speed manual. That choice matters for a few reasons:

- **Engagement:** a manual keeps the driver involved in a truck with this much power.
- **Character:** it suits the old-school look of the body.
- **Practicality:** a six-speed gives you a wide spread of gears, which helps with both cruising and acceleration.

A manual in a high-power build also asks more of the driver, especially in traffic. That's a fair trade for some owners and a dealbreaker for others.

## Hood-Exit Exhaust: Drama With a Purpose

The exhaust exits through the hood rather than under the truck, and the build is known for spitting flames out the back as well. It's pure show-truck theater. It also tells onlookers exactly what's going on under the sheet metal, which a patina body would otherwise hide.

If you're planning something similar, remember that wild exhaust routing is a fabrication challenge, not a bolt-on. It takes planning for heat, sound, and legality where you live. Check local rules on modified exhaust and any visible modifications before you commit.

## Stance and Tires: The Look That Defines Modern C10s

The truck is slammed, sitting low, with massive rear tires. Lowered C10s are a signature of the current custom-truck scene, and big rubber at the back signals that the power is real and meant to be used.

There's a practical side to that too. A 700-hp truck needs grip, and wider rear tires help put power down. The trade-off with any very low stance is ride quality and clearance, so think about the roads you actually drive.

## Why "Daily-Drivable" Is the Real Flex

The most useful part of this build, for everyday drivers, is the emphasis on manners. Autoevolution describes it as a truck built to be driven, not just parked at shows. It has a custom interior and the performance hardware to back up the looks.

That reflects a wider trend in restomod pickups: builders chasing high horsepower while keeping a truck comfortable enough to cruise. Reliability and comfort are what turn a build from a garage piece into something you use.

If you're considering a classic truck project, here's how to apply that thinking:

1. **Set a purpose first.** Weekend cruiser, show truck, or regular driver? Each leads to different choices on suspension, tires, and cabin.
2. **Match power to the rest of the truck.** Brakes, tires, and drivetrain should be sized for what the engine can deliver.
3. **Budget for the unglamorous parts.** Cooling, wiring, and interior comfort decide how often you'll want to drive it.
4. **Choose your transmission honestly.** A manual is rewarding, but think about your commute.

## What We Don't Know

## The Bottom Line

"Material Girl" shows that a classic truck doesn't have to choose between character and capability. Before you start your own C10 project, write down how you'll actually use it, then build the power, stance, and comfort to match that list instead of the photo you hope to take.
