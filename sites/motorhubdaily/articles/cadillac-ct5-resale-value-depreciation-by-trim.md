If you're weighing a used Cadillac CT5, or wondering what yours will fetch later, the headline number is simple: expect to lose about 40% of its value over five years. The more useful number is the gap between trims, and that gap decides whether a CT5 is a smart buy or an expensive lesson.

## The Headline Numbers

Per Slashgear, estimates put the CT5's five-year depreciation at roughly 40 to 42%. That sits in line with the broader car market, and Slashgear reports it beats the typical mid-size luxury vehicle, which tends to shed value faster.

So on average, the CT5 is neither a bargain nor a disaster. Here's a rough way to picture it: a CT5 that cost $50,000 new would be worth around $29,000 to $30,000 after five years if it follows that average. That's our arithmetic, not a market quote, and real prices depend on mileage, condition, and location.

The key phrase in Slashgear's reporting is that the average may be propped up by one model.

## Why the Average Is Misleading

Slashgear points out that the figures may be flattered by the CT5-V Blackwing, the performance version of the sedan. Performance variants tend to hold value far better than base models, and the Blackwing sits at the far end of that spectrum, with depreciation described as near zero or even positive in some cases.

That means a blended 40% figure hides two different ownership experiences:

- **Base and lower trims:** likely to lose more than the average suggests, since the Blackwing is pulling the number up.
- **CT5-V Blackwing:** holding value far better than the rest of the lineup.

If you're shopping a standard CT5, don't assume the headline figure protects you. Treat 40% as a best case for those trims, not a typical one. Slashgear doesn't give trim-by-trim percentages, so check recent sold listings for the exact trim, year, and mileage you're considering.

## Why Sedans Face a Tougher Resale Market

There's a bigger force at work. Cadillac is discontinuing both the CT4 and CT5 sedans after 2026, per the Slashgear report, as the luxury market moves toward SUVs.

Here's our read on what that could mean, and it's analysis, not a reported fact. When a model is discontinued, used buyers sometimes worry about long-term parts and service support, and fewer shoppers are looking for sedans to begin with. Those factors could put pressure on mainstream trims. Meanwhile, a final-run performance sedan can attract enthusiasts who know it won't be built again. That would help explain why the Blackwing stands apart.

None of this is guaranteed. It's a reason to check the market before you commit, not a forecast.

## How to Shop a Used CT5 Smartly

### Decide whether you're buying a car or a collectible

The Blackwing is the one trim where resale looks strong, but it also carries a higher price of entry. If you want a daily luxury sedan, a lower trim may cost far less up front, and you can accept that it will depreciate. If you're buying with resale in mind, the Blackwing is the only one the reporting supports.

### Let someone else take the biggest hit

The steepest depreciation in a car's life usually happens early. Buying a CT5 that's a few years old lets the first owner absorb much of that loss. Given the 40% five-year estimate, a gently used base-trim car can be a reasonable value play, as long as you buy at a price that reflects the trim's softer resale.

### Compare actual sold prices

Asking prices lag reality. Look at what similar CT5s sold for in your area, matched by model year, trim, and mileage. Confirm specifications for the specific model year and your market, since equipment and powertrains vary.

### Run the inspection basics

Beyond price, the usual used-car checks apply: a full service history, a clean title report, a cold-start test drive, and a pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic. A discontinued model makes it even more sensible to confirm that your local shop can service it.

## If You Already Own a CT5

Owners of base trims shouldn't panic, but they should be realistic. If you plan to sell in the next few years, keep service records, keep mileage reasonable, and keep the car clean. Those basics matter more when buyers have fewer reasons to choose a sedan. Blackwing owners are in the enviable spot, with the market apparently rewarding rarity and performance.

## What This Tells You

The CT5 is a case study in why averages can mislead. A single 40% figure covers a car that may barely lose value and others that lose more than the average. This article is a research-based look at reported estimates, not a firsthand test drive, so treat the numbers as a starting point.

Before you buy or sell, price the exact trim you care about against recent sold listings. That one step tells you more than any five-year average.
