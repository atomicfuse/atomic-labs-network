Economic coverage tends to split into two camps, and gas prices sit in the middle of the argument. This week, Fox Business host Larry Kudlow argued that the media is overlooking positive signs in the economy, including consumer spending, productivity and stock market gains, even as gas prices remain high, per Fox Business. Whether you find that persuasive or not, the national debate won't tell you what your own commute costs.

Your own numbers will. By the end of this article you'll be able to work out your fuel cost per mile, compare it against a hybrid or electric alternative for your routine, and spot the cheapest changes to make first.

## Step 1: Find your actual miles per gallon

The window sticker is a starting point, not your reality. To get your real figure:

1. Fill the tank and reset a trip meter (or note the odometer).
2. Drive normally until the tank is roughly half empty or lower.
3. Fill up again and note the gallons added.
4. Divide miles driven by gallons added.

Do this across two or three fill-ups and average the results. One tank can be skewed by a highway road trip or a week of short errands.

## Step 2: Turn it into cost per mile

Divide the price you paid per gallon by your real MPG. That's your fuel cost per mile.

Here is a hypothetical to show the method. These are example inputs, not current market prices, so swap in your own:

- Gas at $3.50 per gallon and 25 MPG works out to 14 cents per mile.
- The same gas price at 40 MPG works out to about 8.75 cents per mile.

Over 12,000 miles a year, that's roughly $1,680 versus $1,050 in fuel. The gap is about $630 a year, and it comes from the car, not the economy.

## Step 3: Price out an EV the same way

Electric cars use a different unit, but the logic is identical. Find your car's efficiency in kWh per mile (or kWh per 100 miles) and your electricity rate on your utility bill.

An example, again with illustrative numbers: a car using 0.30 kWh per mile at 16 cents per kWh costs about 4.8 cents per mile. Over 12,000 miles, that's around $576 a year.

Two cautions apply:

- **Where you charge matters.** Home charging overnight and public fast charging can price very differently. Check your own rates rather than assuming.
- **Efficiency varies by model year and market.** Look up the specific trim and year you're considering instead of relying on a general figure for "EVs."

## Step 4: Match the vehicle to your routine

Cost per mile only matters in the context of how you drive. A few patterns:

### Short, stop-and-go commutes

Hybrids and EVs tend to benefit most in this kind of driving, because they can recover energy when braking. If most of your miles are local, this is where the math often favors electrification.

### Long highway stretches

The advantage of a hybrid narrows at steady highway speeds, and an EV's range and public charging access become the deciding factors. Check how your regular routes line up with chargers before you commit.

### Low annual mileage

If you drive only a few thousand miles a year, fuel savings take much longer to pay back a higher purchase price. At low mileage, the cheapest car to own may simply be the one you already have.

## Step 5: Cut your fuel bill without changing cars

You don't need a new vehicle to move your number. These are the cheapest fixes, roughly in order of effort:

- **Check tire pressure monthly.** Use the figure on the driver's door jamb placard, not the number printed on the tire sidewall. Underinflated tires make the engine work harder.
- **Remove dead weight.** Roof racks, cargo boxes and a trunk full of stuff you don't need all add drag or load.
- **Smooth out your driving.** Gentle acceleration and early, gradual braking use less fuel than aggressive driving.
- **Combine errands.** Several short trips from a cold engine cost more than one planned loop.
- **Keep up with basic maintenance.** A clogged air filter or overdue service won't always hurt economy dramatically, but a car in good shape is the baseline for any savings.

After making changes, repeat Step 1 and see what happened to your MPG. Measuring beats guessing.

## Step 6: Count more than fuel before you switch

Fuel is only one line in ownership cost. Before swapping vehicles to save at the pump, add up:

- Purchase price and financing costs
- Insurance quotes for the specific model
- Maintenance and tire costs
- Depreciation
- Any home charging installation, if you're considering an EV

A car that saves $600 a year on fuel but costs more in insurance and payments may not come out ahead. Get real quotes, not estimates.

## Separating the headlines from your budget

Kudlow's argument, as reported by Fox Business, is one view in a long-running debate about how to read the economy. Analysts, voters and media outlets weigh the same data differently, and that debate won't be settled in a fuel-economy article. What you can settle is your own number.

So grab your last three fuel receipts tonight, calculate your cost per mile, and write it down. That figure will tell you more about your next car decision than any headline will.
