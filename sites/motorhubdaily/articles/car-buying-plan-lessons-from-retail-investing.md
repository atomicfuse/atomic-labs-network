Fox Business host Charles Payne recently used his "Today's Take" on "Making Money" to talk about retail investor sentiment, framing what he called a missing "magical ingredient" in how everyday investors approach the market, per Fox Business. The summary of the segment we have doesn't spell out what that ingredient is, so we won't guess at it or put words in his mouth.

But the premise is worth borrowing, because it maps neatly onto a place where everyday people also spend big money: the driveway. Millions of people new to investing struggle with consistent results, according to the framing around the segment. Plenty of car buyers struggle the same way, and for a similar reason. They react to the moment instead of working from a plan.

This is our take, not Payne's. Here is how to build a simple plan for your next car purchase so the decision isn't driven by a showroom mood.

## Start With Total Cost, Not Monthly Payment

The monthly payment is the number dealers love to discuss, and it hides the real price. A longer loan can make an expensive car look affordable while you pay more in interest overall.

Build a one-page budget that covers the full cost of ownership:

- **Purchase price and financing:** the out-the-door price, down payment, loan term, and interest rate.
- **Insurance:** get quotes on the specific models you're considering before you commit. Rates can differ a lot between trims and models.
- **Fuel or charging:** estimate your weekly mileage, then price it out for gas, hybrid, or electric.
- **Maintenance and repairs:** tires, brakes, fluids, and a cushion for the unexpected.
- **Depreciation:** what the car may be worth when you want to sell it.

You don't need exact figures to start. Rough ranges for each line will already show you which options deserve a closer look.

## Match the Powertrain to Your Routine

The best car on paper isn't always the best car for your week. Think about how you actually drive.

### If most trips are short and predictable

An electric vehicle can fit well if you can charge at home or work. Check whether you have a dedicated parking spot with access to an outlet or charger before you fall for a model.

### If you mix commuting with long road trips

A hybrid may suit you better, since it doesn't depend on charging stops. Verify the specs for the exact model year and market you're shopping in, because efficiency and range figures vary between versions and regions.

### If you drive very little

A reliable used car with low running costs can beat a new one. Less depreciation and cheaper insurance often matter more than the latest features.

## Check Before You Buy, Especially Used

A plan includes a checklist. For a used car, build one and stick to it:

1. Request the vehicle history report and compare it with the seller's story.
2. Look for mismatched paint, uneven panel gaps, and worn tires that suggest alignment or suspension problems.
3. Ask for service records. Regular oil changes and documented repairs are a good sign.
4. Test drive on roads you know: city streets, a highway stretch, and a rough patch. Listen for unusual noises and note how the brakes feel.
5. Pay for an independent pre-purchase inspection. It costs far less than a surprise repair.

For used EVs and hybrids, also ask about battery health and warranty coverage, and confirm what transfers to a second owner.

## Separate Research From Experience

Spec sheets, owner forums, and comparison articles are research. A test drive is experience. They answer different questions, and you need both.

Research tells you the claimed range, cargo space, and reported reliability. A test drive tells you whether the seats fit your back, whether the visibility bothers you, and whether the infotainment system makes you want to scream. When you read a review, check whether the writer actually drove the car or compiled data. Treat the two differently.

## Set Rules Before You Walk In

The pressure of a dealership is where plans fall apart. Decide ahead of time:

- Your maximum out-the-door price.
- The longest loan term you'll accept.
- The features you need versus the ones you merely like.
- A rule that you won't sign the same day you first see the car.

Sleeping on it costs nothing. If the deal is still good tomorrow, you've lost nothing, and if it vanishes, it probably wasn't as good as it sounded.

## Revisit the Plan After You Buy

Ownership is not a one-time decision. Track your real fuel or charging costs, maintenance, and insurance for the first year, then compare them with your original estimates. If you were off, adjust your next purchase. Over time that habit does more for your finances than any single deal.

## The Takeaway

Whatever Payne's "magical ingredient" turns out to be, the lesson for car buyers is plain: don't let the moment make the decision. Before your next visit to a dealership, write down your total budget, your driving routine, and your walk-away price, and bring that page with you.
