A 1967 Chevrolet Nova called "Casa Nova" is making the rounds, and the headline number is easy to remember: 1,013 horsepower. But the more useful lesson for anyone who owns, or dreams of owning, a modified car is how this build was shaped around the person who drives it. If you've ever wondered what a custom car can do beyond going fast, this one is a good case study.

The details here come from Autoevolution's profile of the car, which is owned by Dennis Terning. We haven't driven it or inspected it, so what follows is a summary of that reporting plus some practical takeaways of our own.

## What Casa Nova is

Per Autoevolution, Casa Nova is a 1967 Chevrolet Nova restomod, meaning a classic car rebuilt with modern hardware. It spent decades in storage before being brought back as a Pro Touring machine, a style that aims for modern handling, braking, and drivability rather than straight-line drag strip numbers alone.

The finish is a burgundy shade with gold accents. Autoevolution frames the car as a story of rebirth and resilience in the face of adversity, and the build's focus on accessibility gives that framing real substance.

## The feature that matters most: a swivel front seat

The standout piece of engineering, according to Autoevolution, is a custom swivel front seat designed for wheelchair access. A classic two-door like the Nova is not an easy car to get in and out of for many people, with a long door, a low roofline, and a low seat. Rotating the seat changes that equation.

We don't have the mechanical details of how the seat is built, so we won't guess at them. The takeaway is the idea itself: a car can be reengineered around how its owner actually enters, sits in, and drives it.

### Why this matters for ordinary owners

You don't need a four-figure-horsepower build to apply the thinking. Many people modify cars for comfort, mobility, or ease of use, and the Casa Nova approach suggests a useful order of priorities:

- Start with how you get in and out of the car, since that affects every drive.
- Decide what you need the car to do daily before you chase performance numbers.
- Work with a builder or shop that will custom-fabricate rather than force off-the-shelf parts to fit.

That's our analysis, not a claim from the original report, but the build illustrates it well.

## The performance side

The 1,013-hp figure is the attention-grabber. Autoevolution's profile describes the car as a Pro Touring build, and its coverage points to the chassis, LSX-based power, and brakes as the core of the hardware story. We can't responsibly list part numbers, displacement, or suspension brands because those specifics aren't in what we have, so if you're considering a similar build, check the original Autoevolution article and the builder's own documentation for the full spec sheet.

What we can say is what Pro Touring generally implies: a build like this is meant to be driven on real roads, not trailered to shows. That ties directly to the accessibility work. A car with more than 1,000 hp that its owner can't comfortably get into would defeat the purpose.

### A caution on big-power builds

If you're inspired to chase a number like this, keep in mind that power is the cheapest part of a build to talk about and one of the costlier parts to make usable. Brakes, chassis, and cooling have to keep pace. That's a general rule of thumb rather than something reported about this car, but the fact that Casa Nova is described as a Pro Touring build suggests its builders approached it as a whole package.

## From storage to street

Autoevolution notes the Nova sat in storage for decades before its rebuild. That's a familiar story for classic cars, and it comes with a practical lesson if you're shopping for a long-dormant project:

- Expect to inspect or replace anything rubber, including hoses, belts, and seals.
- Budget for fuel system work, since old fuel and tanks cause problems.
- Check the body and frame for hidden rust before you plan any modifications.

These are general checks for any stored classic, not details from Casa Nova's rebuild. In a full restomod, much of the original drivetrain may be replaced anyway, but the body and structure are what you're really buying.

## What this build says about custom cars

Restomods are often judged by dyno sheets and auction results. Casa Nova points to a different measure: does the car fit the person? A 1,013-hp Nova is impressive, but the swivel seat is what makes it distinctive, and it's what turns a fast classic into a car its owner can use.

If you're planning your own modification, whether it's a seat, a control, or a full restomod, write down what you need the car to do for you before you talk to a builder. Then judge every part on that list, and treat horsepower as the last line item rather than the first.
