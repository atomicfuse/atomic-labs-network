AWS CEO Matt Garman recently described the potential for Amazon Web Services as "massive," according to Fox Business. He was speaking on *The Claman Countdown* about AWS's third-quarter earnings, sales of cloud capacity, and demand for artificial intelligence chips.

Nothing in that coverage was about cars. But it's a useful prompt for anyone shopping for one. Cloud computing and AI are getting more investment, and modern vehicles lean on remote servers more every year. This guide gives you a checklist for judging the connected side of a car before you sign, so a flashy tech menu doesn't turn into a surprise bill.

A note on scope: the interview as reported covered AWS's business, not the auto industry. What follows is my own practical analysis, not a claim about what Garman said or about any automaker's cloud provider.

## Why a cloud story belongs in a car-buying conversation

Many current vehicles use an internet connection for things beyond the radio. Common examples include navigation with live traffic, remote start from a phone app, over-the-air software updates, and voice assistants. Those features generally depend on servers somewhere, and servers cost money to run. That's the link between a headline about cloud demand and your ownership costs.

When you buy a car, you're also buying into a software service. The question is how long it will work and what it will cost.

## Check what's free, and for how long

This is the most useful step, and it takes five minutes at the dealership or on the manufacturer's website.

- **Ask for the trial lengths in writing.** Connected services often come with a free period. Find out when it ends.
- **Get the post-trial price.** Ask for the monthly or yearly cost of each feature, not a bundle estimate.
- **Separate essentials from extras.** Remote lock and unlock matters to some drivers. Streaming music matters to others. Pay only for what you'd use.
- **Confirm what still works if you stop paying.** Some features may go dark, while basics like the phone-mirroring screen may keep going. Ask the seller to show you.

Because these terms vary by manufacturer, model year, and market, verify them for the exact trim you're considering.

## Ask how the car gets software updates

Over-the-air updates can fix bugs and add features without a service visit. They can also vary a lot between brands. Before you buy, ask:

1. Does the car update over the air, or only at a dealer?
2. Do updates cover just the infotainment screen, or also driving and safety systems?
3. Is there a stated support window for updates?

If the salesperson can't answer, check the owner's manual or the manufacturer's support pages. A vague answer is information too.

## Look at the app before you look at the car

For many connected features, the phone app is the front door. Spend ten minutes on it:

- Check the app store rating and read recent reviews, not just the average.
- Look for repeated complaints about login problems or features that stop working.
- See whether the app is made for your phone's operating system and version.

A great car with a flaky app can be frustrating if you rely on remote climate control or charging status, which matters especially for EV owners.

## Understand what data the car collects

Connected cars can generate data about location, driving behavior, and system health. You don't need to be an expert, but do three things:

- Read the privacy settings menu during your test drive.
- Find out how to disable data sharing or location tracking, and what you lose when you do.
- Ask whether you can delete your data and unlink a phone when you sell the car.

That last point matters on the used market. When you buy used, make sure the previous owner's account has been removed from the vehicle so you control the features.

## Weigh the long game

Here is my take, not a reported fact: the more a car depends on remote services, the more its long-term value depends on the manufacturer continuing to support them. Garman's "massive" comment, as reported, is about demand for cloud capacity and AI chips. It's easy to imagine more features being built on that kind of infrastructure. But a feature that's exciting at launch is only valuable to you if it still works, at a price you accept, in year seven.

A simple way to apply that when comparing two similar cars:

### Compare the five-year software cost

Add up the subscription costs you'd realistically pay over five years for features you'd use. Put that number next to the purchase price, insurance, and maintenance estimates. A car that's a few hundred dollars cheaper upfront can cost more over time if key features sit behind a recurring fee.

### Prefer features that work without a subscription

Phone mirroring, a physical set of climate controls, and a key that works without an app are all fallback options. They don't depend on a server or a payment. If a car has them alongside the cloud features, you're protected either way.

## The one thing to do before you buy

Write down every connected feature you'd actually use, then ask the dealer or manufacturer what each costs after the trial, how long it's supported, and what happens if you cancel. If you can get those answers in writing, you're buying the whole car, not just the part you can see on the lot.
