## Why your electric rate matters more than the car

An EV's running cost depends heavily on one number: what you pay per kilowatt-hour (kWh). Two identical cars can have very different fueling costs depending on where their owners live and when they charge. That's why electricity policy, which is what Fazio's campaign emphasizes, ends up mattering to drivers as well as households.

Your rate is printed on your utility bill. Look for the total cost per kWh, including delivery and supply charges, not just the headline supply rate. Divide your total bill by the kWh used to get an all-in figure.

## A four-step way to estimate your charging cost

You need three numbers: your all-in rate, the car's efficiency, and how far you drive.

### Step 1: Find your all-in rate

Take last month's bill, divide the total dollars by total kWh. That's your real price per kWh.

### Step 2: Find the car's efficiency

Efficiency is listed on the EPA's fueleconomy.gov for each model year and trim. It's shown as kWh per 100 miles. Specs vary by model year and market, so check the exact version you're considering rather than relying on a general figure.

### Step 3: Estimate your annual miles

Check your last two service records or inspection reports for odometer readings, and subtract to get a yearly figure.

### Step 4: Multiply

Annual miles divided by 100, times kWh per 100 miles, gives yearly kWh. Multiply by your rate. Add roughly 10 to 15 percent for charging losses, a common rule of thumb, since not every kWh from the wall ends up in the battery.

Compare the result with your current yearly gasoline spend: annual miles divided by your car's mpg, times your local price per gallon.

## Where the savings (or surprises) come from

### Time-of-use rates

Many utilities offer plans with cheaper overnight power. If yours does, scheduling your car to charge after bedtime can cut the cost noticeably. Ask your utility whether an EV-specific rate exists and what the peak hours are.

### Home versus public charging

Public DC fast charging usually costs more per kWh than home charging. If you can't charge at home, run the same math with the public network's price. The gap with gasoline can narrow considerably.

### Rebates and incentives

Programs for chargers, installation or EV purchases change often and differ by state and utility. Check your state energy office and your utility's website before you buy, and confirm eligibility rules for your model year.

## What the data center debate means for you

Fazio's push to repeal the state's data center tax incentive reflects a broader argument about whether large new power users push up costs for everyone else. Whether that holds in Connecticut is a policy question I can't settle from the available reporting. What you can do is watch your own rate. If it rises, rerun the four-step math, because a higher rate shifts the break-even point between an EV, a hybrid and a gas car.

## Questions to ask before you commit

- What is my all-in rate per kWh, and does my utility offer a time-of-use plan?
- Can I charge at home, and what would a charger installation cost?
- What does the EPA say about this exact model year's efficiency?
- Would a hybrid fit my routine better if my rate is high or I can't charge at home?

## The takeaway

Before you shop, pull out your latest electric bill, calculate your all-in rate per kWh, and run the four-step estimate on two or three cars. Ten minutes of math will tell you more about your real ownership cost than any campaign sound bite.
