A new iSeeCars analysis, reported by Slashgear, puts a number on something EV shoppers usually learn the hard way: where you live shapes how easy an electric car is to own. Louisville, Kentucky, ranks as the least EV-friendly major U.S. city, with 2,904 residents per public charger. San Francisco leads with just 334 residents per charger.

That gap is the useful part. You can use the same measure, residents per charger, to pressure-test your own EV plans before you sign anything. Here's how.

## What the iSeeCars ranking found

Per Slashgear's report on the iSeeCars data:

- **Best major metro:** San Francisco, at 334 residents per charger.
- **Worst major metro:** Louisville, at 2,904 residents per charger.
- **Best state:** Vermont leads the states.
- **Weakest states:** Louisiana trails, and rural states including Alaska, Wyoming and Montana are described as severely underserved.
- **Regional pattern:** California dominates nationally.

The ratio between the top and bottom metros is roughly 8.7 to 1, by our arithmetic from those two figures. In plain terms, a San Francisco driver shares each public charger with far fewer neighbors than a Louisville driver does.

The reported projection is more than 40,000 new chargers by the end of 2026, though Slashgear notes that charging network growth has slowed. Treat new-charger promises as a bonus, not a plan.

## Why residents per charger matters (and where it falls short)

Fewer residents per charger generally means less competition for a plug and more options when you need one. It's a quick way to compare places that would otherwise be hard to compare.

It's also a blunt tool. This is our analysis, not part of the iSeeCars findings: a city-wide ratio doesn't tell you whether chargers are near your home, your job, or your usual road trip routes. It also doesn't show charger speed or reliability. Use it as a first filter, then check the details locally.

## Step 1: Decide whether you can charge at home

This matters more than any city ranking. If you own a house with a garage or driveway, public charger density becomes a backup. If you rent or live in an apartment, it becomes your main fuel supply.

Slashgear's coverage cites home charging installation costs of $800 to $2,500. That range is worth getting quotes against, since your electrical panel and the distance from it to your parking spot will drive the final number. Renters usually can't make that investment at all, which is why public infrastructure matters most for them.

## Step 2: Look up your own area, not just your city

If you live in a low-ranked metro like Louisville, the ranking isn't a verdict. It's a prompt to check the specifics:

- Open a charging map app and look at your neighborhood, workplace and the places you visit weekly.
- Count how many fast chargers sit within a short drive of home.
- Check recent user comments on those stations for outages.

If your daily routine is covered by home or workplace charging, a thin citywide network may barely affect you.

## Step 3: Match the car to the network

Driving habits decide how much public charging you need. Short commutes with home charging can work almost anywhere. Frequent long trips through thinly served areas are harder. The iSeeCars data flags Alaska, Wyoming and Montana as particularly underserved, so road trips through those states deserve extra route planning.

If you're unsure, a plug-in hybrid or conventional hybrid can bridge the gap while charging catches up. Specifications and charging speeds vary by model year and market, so confirm the details for the exact car you're considering. We haven't test-driven any of this; it's a research-based framework.

## Step 4: Run a two-week charging rehearsal

Before buying, plan your real weeks as if you already own an EV. Write down where you'd plug in each day, how long you'd wait, and what you'd do if your usual spot were occupied or broken. If the plan keeps falling apart, that's information worth having before you buy.

## A quick pre-purchase checklist

- Can you install home charging, and what does a quote say against the $800–$2,500 range?
- If not, is there reliable charging at work or near home?
- How many fast chargers are within a short drive of your routine?
- Do your regular longer trips cross sparse regions?
- Do you have a fallback plan when a station is down?

## The takeaway

Don't let a ranking make the decision for you, in either direction. Look up the charger situation within a few miles of your home and workplace this week, get a home-install quote if you can, and buy the EV only when your own routine, not the national average, checks out.
