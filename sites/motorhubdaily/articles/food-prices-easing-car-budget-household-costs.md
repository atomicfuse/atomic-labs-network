Grocery prices are one of the loudest budget worries in American households, and this week brought a sign of relief. U.S. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins praised the Trump administration's affordability policies on Fox Business's "Kudlow," pointing to easing food inflation after a period of elevated prices, as reported by Fox Business. The comments come ahead of the midterm elections.

This is an auto site, so here's the practical angle: whatever you make of the political claims, easing food costs are a good moment to audit the rest of your monthly spending. Your car is likely the second-biggest line after housing. Here is how to check it and where the savings usually hide.

## What we know, and what we don't

The reported facts are limited. Rollins said food inflation has begun to ease, and she framed that as progress tied to the administration's policies. That is her characterization, and credit for inflation trends is often contested. The report as summarized doesn't include specific percentages, time periods, or regional breakdowns, so we can't tell you how big the relief is or whether it is reaching your local store.

That gap is the reason to check your own numbers rather than rely on national talking points. Your experience, and your car costs, are what you can control.

## Step 1: Compare your own grocery bill

Pull your last three months of grocery spending from your bank or card statements. Compare it with the same months a year ago if you can. If your total has dropped, you have a little room to redirect. If it hasn't, that tells you national headlines and your cart may not match yet.

## Step 2: Add up what your car really costs

Most drivers know their payment and little else. Total these for the past 12 months:

- Loan or lease payments
- Insurance premiums
- Fuel or charging
- Maintenance and repairs
- Registration, taxes, and parking or tolls

Divide by 12 for a monthly figure. Then divide the annual total by the miles you drove for a cost per mile. That one number makes comparisons easy, whether you're weighing a hybrid, an EV, or keeping what you have.

## Step 3: Hunt for the easy wins

These are usually faster than any big vehicle decision:

### Insurance
Get at least three quotes at renewal. Premiums vary widely between insurers for the same driver and car, and a 20-minute comparison can pay off all year.

### Maintenance you can stay ahead of
Check tire pressure monthly, since underinflated tires waste fuel and wear faster. Replace the engine air filter and cabin filter on the schedule in your owner's manual. Skipping oil changes or fluid service is how small bills become large ones.

### Fuel or charging habits
Smooth acceleration and sensible highway speeds improve efficiency in any gas car. If you drive an EV, charging at home overnight is typically cheaper than public fast charging, though rates vary by utility and market, so check your own tariff.

## Step 4: Decide whether a bigger change makes sense

If your cost-per-mile number is high, ask what is driving it. A long commute with a thirsty vehicle may favor a hybrid or EV. Lots of short trips may favor an EV with home charging. A low-mileage driver with an old, paid-off car may save the most by simply keeping it maintained. Specifications and incentives differ by model year and market, so verify them for the exact vehicle you're considering before you commit.

## Treat relief as a cushion, not a plan

If food costs are truly easing for your household, don't let the savings vanish unnoticed. Move the difference into a car repair fund, even $25 to $50 a month, so the next brake job or tire set doesn't land on a credit card. Then redo your numbers in six months, because both grocery prices and car costs can move.

The action for today: pull your statements, calculate your monthly car cost, and get one insurance quote. Those three steps will tell you more about your budget than any headline.
