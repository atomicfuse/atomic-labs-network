Zero-turn mowers have a reputation as pro-grade splurges. But Slashgear recently rounded up five of Home Depot's most affordable options, and every one of them shares a 42-inch cutting deck. That makes the comparison simpler than you might expect: the real choices are engine power, gas versus battery, brand, comfort and warranty.

Below is what the roundup covers, plus a practical way to decide which type fits your yard. A note on sourcing: this is a researched comparison based on Slashgear's reporting, not a firsthand test. Prices, specs and warranty terms change by model year and retailer, so confirm them on the Home Depot listing before you buy.

## The five mowers at a glance

Per Slashgear, the lineup is:

- **Murray Cruz**, 19 horsepower, 42-inch deck
- **Troy-Bilt Mustang**, 22 horsepower, 42-inch deck
- **John Deere**, 20 horsepower, 42-inch deck
- **Cub Cadet Ultima Z2**, 21.5 horsepower, 42-inch deck
- **Ryobi 80V HP**, electric, 42-inch deck

Slashgear compares them on price, engine power, cutting width, yard capacity, comfort features and warranties. I'm not repeating specific prices or warranty lengths here because the summary I worked from doesn't include them. Pull those from the product pages.

## Start with the gas-versus-electric decision

Four of the five are gas-powered, and their horsepower figures range from 19 to 22. The Ryobi is the outlier: an 80V electric model. That split is the biggest fork in the road, so settle it first.

### Why you might choose gas

Gas models give you the most options in this group. If you want to compare brands head to head, the Murray, Troy-Bilt, John Deere and Cub Cadet all compete on similar terms. Horsepower is the easiest number to compare, with the Troy-Bilt Mustang at the top (22 hp) and the Murray Cruz at the bottom (19 hp). Keep in mind that gas means routine engine upkeep, such as oil, filters and fuel. That's the ownership trade-off.

### Why you might choose electric

The Ryobi is the pick if you'd rather skip engine maintenance. Electric riding mowers generally trade oil changes and fuel for charging and battery care. Check the listing for runtime and charge time, since the summary doesn't include those, and runtime should be measured against your yard's size.

## Match the mower to your yard

Zero-turn mowers earn their keep on large or tricky lawns: wide open stretches, plus obstacles like trees, flower beds and garden edges that you have to circle. The tight turning is what saves time around them.

Slashgear frames these mowers as a practical upgrade for homeowners with big or awkward yards who don't want to go fully premium. Here's a simple way to apply that:

1. **Estimate your mowing area.** Slashgear lists yard capacity for each model. Use the stated figure, and leave yourself some margin.
2. **Count the obstacles.** More trees and beds means more value from zero-turn steering.
3. **Check your terrain.** Zero-turn mowers can be tricky on steep slopes, so read the manufacturer's slope guidance before buying.

If your yard is a flat quarter acre with few obstacles, a zero-turn may be more machine than you need. If it's a few acres of curves and trees, the category starts to make sense.

## What the 42-inch deck means for you

All five mowers use the same deck width, so cutting width won't separate them. A 42-inch deck is a middle-of-the-road size. It cuts a decent swath per pass while still fitting through many standard gates, though you should measure your own gate before ordering.

Since the decks match, your decision comes down to the factors that actually differ: power source, horsepower, comfort features, warranty and brand.

## Don't skip comfort and warranty

Slashgear specifically weighs comfort features and warranties, and you should too. A mower you dread using sits in the shed, and a cheaper machine with thin coverage can cost more over time.

### Comfort checks

- Sit on one in the store if you can. Seat padding and control reach are personal.
- Look at the reported comfort features on each listing and compare them like for like.

### Warranty checks

- Compare the length of coverage on the engine, frame and deck separately, if the listing breaks them out.
- Note whether the warranty differs for residential use, which is how most homeowners use these.
- For the Ryobi, look for separate battery coverage.

## Think about brand and service

The list mixes familiar names: Murray, Troy-Bilt, John Deere, Cub Cadet and Ryobi. Brand matters mostly for two things: how easy parts are to find and where you can get service. Before buying, check whether a Home Depot or a local dealer near you can handle repairs. A mower that can't be serviced nearby is a bigger problem than a slightly lower horsepower figure.

## A quick way to pick

- **Want the most horsepower in this group?** The Troy-Bilt Mustang at 22 hp leads on paper, with the Cub Cadet Ultima Z2 at 21.5 hp close behind.
- **Prefer a well-known name in mowers?** The John Deere, at 20 hp, is the one to look at.
- **Want the least engine maintenance?** The electric Ryobi 80V is the only one here without a gas engine.
- **Shopping lowest on the power scale?** The Murray Cruz is 19 hp.

Horsepower is a rough guide, not a verdict. A higher number doesn't guarantee a better cut or a longer-lived machine.

## The takeaway

Before you compare a single spec sheet, decide whether you want gas or battery, then measure your yard and read the warranty on your top two choices. Those three steps will narrow five similar-looking mowers down faster than any horsepower number.
