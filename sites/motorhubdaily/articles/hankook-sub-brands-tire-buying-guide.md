The name on the sidewall isn't always the name of the company behind the tire. Hankook is a fixture at tire shops worldwide, and per Slashgear, the same manufacturer also sells tires under other names in certain regions and for specific market segments. Knowing that can help you shop smarter when replacement costs sting.

This guide covers the four Hankook-owned labels Slashgear names beyond the flagship brand, and how to think about a tire when you spot a name you don't recognize.

## The four sub-brands Slashgear lists

Slashgear's rundown names these brands as owned by Hankook, alongside the main Hankook label:

- Laufenn
- Optimo
- Kingstar
- Aurora

According to the report, each one targets different markets, price points, and vehicle needs. I don't have detailed specs, model lineups, or regional availability for each, so check the manufacturer's current catalog for your market and model year before you commit to any specific tire.

### Laufenn

Laufenn is the name most likely to show up in a side-by-side comparison with Hankook itself. The general idea behind a sub-brand like this is to give a major manufacturer a second shelf in the store: a different name, often a different price tier, backed by the same parent company.

### Optimo, Kingstar and Aurora

These three are less familiar to many drivers, and that's the point of this exercise. Slashgear describes them as part of Hankook's broader portfolio aimed at different segments. If a shop quotes you one of these, you now know to ask which segment it's built for and what the tread-life warranty covers.

## Why manufacturers split into sub-labels

Slashgear's framing is that major manufacturers compete across premium and affordable segments without always using the parent brand name. My read: it lets a company chase budget buyers without cheapening the reputation of its flagship line. It also means two tires from the same parent may be engineered to very different priorities.

That last part matters. Shared ownership doesn't guarantee shared performance. A tire from a parent company's value label is not the flagship tire with a different sticker, and you shouldn't assume it is.

## Are budget sub-brand tires worth it?

It depends on what you need, and this is my analysis rather than a finding from the report. A few practical checks:

1. **Match the tire to the job.** Replacement shoppers are increasingly weighing value, EV, SUV, all-weather, and budget options, per Slashgear. Decide which category you're actually in before comparing prices.
2. **Compare the warranty.** Treadwear warranties in miles or years are printed by the manufacturer. A shorter warranty on a cheaper tire may erase the savings.
3. **Read the size and load ratings.** Make sure the tire matches your owner's manual or door-jamb placard, including load index and speed rating.
4. **Check availability.** A tire sold in one region may be hard to find in another, so confirm you can get a matching set, or a replacement later, locally.
5. **Look at independent test results** for the exact model, not just the brand family.

The cost angle is simple: the cheapest set today isn't the cheapest if you replace it sooner. Divide the price of the set by the warrantied mileage for a rough cost-per-mile comparison.

## How to spot parent-company branding

If you want to know who stands behind a tire, a few habits help:

- **Ask the shop directly.** Tire sellers can usually tell you which manufacturer makes a brand.
- **Check the manufacturer's website.** Parent companies typically list their brands and where each is sold.
- **Read the sidewall.** It carries the brand, model, size, and load and speed ratings. The DOT code lets you confirm manufacture date, and tires that have sat for years deserve a second look.

None of this requires a test drive. It's research, and it's worth doing before you pay for four tires.

## A quick shopping framework

Before you walk into a shop, write down four things: your tire size, your top priority (price, wet grip, winter capability, EV efficiency, or SUV durability), your budget for the full installed set, and how long you plan to keep the car. Then ask for two quotes: one from a flagship line and one from a value-oriented sub-brand. Compare warranties and independent reviews side by side.

If both come from the same parent company, that's useful context, not a verdict. Make the decision on the specific tire.

## The takeaway

Before you buy, flip the question around: don't ask "Is this brand good?" Ask "Who makes it, what is it built for, and does it fit how I drive?" Spend ten minutes on that and you'll make a better choice than any logo can make for you.
