If you have a little garage space and want to stop paying for every tire swap, a small set of tools covers most of the job. Slashgear recently rounded up five highly rated options for inflating, removing, lifting, breaking beads, and reseating tires. This guide sorts them by what each one does and when you would actually need it, so you can build a kit in the right order instead of buying everything at once.

A note on sourcing: this is a researched rundown based on Slashgear's picks, not a firsthand test of the products. Prices, availability, and exact specs vary by retailer and market, so check the details for your own vehicle before you buy.

## Start with the basic swap: three tools

If your goal is swapping a wheel (say, seasonal tires already mounted on rims, or a spare in place of a flat), you need to lift the car, loosen and tighten lug nuts properly, and set the pressure. Three of Slashgear's picks map to exactly that.

### Pittsburgh 2 Ton Compact Trolley Jack

This is the lifting tool. A trolley jack rolls under the car and raises it more easily than the scissor jack that typically ships with a vehicle. The "compact" label suggests it suits tight garage spaces, which is the kind of setup this guide is about.

Before you buy any jack, check your owner's manual for your car's lift points and weight. A jack lifts the car; it should never be the only thing holding it up while you work. Pair it with jack stands, which are a separate purchase beyond the five tools in this list.

### Husky 1/2 in. Drive Torque Wrench

This is the tool that most separates a careful home job from a risky one. A torque wrench lets you tighten lug nuts to the specification for your vehicle rather than guessing. Over-tightening can stress studs and wheels, and under-tightening can leave a wheel loose. Your owner's manual lists the correct figure for your model and year.

A practical habit: snug the lug nuts with the wheel off the ground, lower the car, then do the final torque in a star pattern. Many drivers also recheck torque after the first short drive.

### AstroAI AIRUN H Portable Inflator and Pressure Gauge

Correct pressure matters after any tire work, and a portable inflator with a built-in gauge means you can set it in your garage instead of hunting for a gas station air pump. The right pressure for your car is usually on a sticker in the driver's door jamb, not on the tire sidewall. The sidewall number is the tire's maximum, not your target.

This is also the cheapest item on the list to justify, since you will use it year-round for routine top-offs, not just during swaps.

## Go deeper: two tools for tire servicing

The remaining two picks are for a different job: separating a tire from its wheel and putting it back. That is tire servicing, not just wheel swapping, and it is a step up in effort.

### BeadBuster XB-450i Bead Breaker

The bead is the edge of the tire that seals against the rim. After a tire has sat for a while, it can be stubbornly stuck. A bead breaker is the tool that pushes it loose so you can remove the tire from the wheel. If you only ever swap complete wheel-and-tire assemblies, you will not need one.

### XtremepowerUS Heavy Duty Tire Bead Seater

Going the other way, a bead seater helps push the tire's edges back against the rim so the tire seals and can hold air. Slashgear lists it for reseating tires. It is the tool for people mounting tires themselves.

Both of these tools involve working with pressurized air and stiff rubber. If you have never mounted a tire, watching the process done properly first, or having a shop handle that step, is a sensible way to start.

## Must-have versus nice-to-have

This ranking is my own read of the list, not Slashgear's.

- **Must-have for any home swap:** the torque wrench and the jack. Lifting and fastening are the two steps you cannot skip or fudge.
- **Strongly recommended:** the inflator and gauge. It is the easiest tool to use often.
- **Only if you service tires yourself:** the bead breaker and bead seater. These make sense if you plan to dismount and remount tires, and they are overkill for wheel swaps.

That order also works as a budget path. Buy the swap tools first, run a season, and add the servicing tools only if you find yourself wanting them.

## Safety mistakes to avoid

None of the following comes from the Slashgear piece; it is general good practice, so confirm the specifics in your owner's manual.

- **Working on soft or sloped ground.** Use a flat, solid surface like a level garage floor.
- **Skipping jack stands.** Never rely on a jack alone to support the car while you are under or near it.
- **Guessing at lug nut tightness.** Use the torque wrench and the figure for your vehicle.
- **Forgetting the pressure check.** Set tires to the door-jamb number once the job is done.
- **Ignoring EV and hybrid differences.** Heavier vehicles and specific lift points may apply, so read your manual before lifting.

## Why the home setup appeals

Slashgear's framing is that tire care at home can save money and spare you from waiting on a shop or roadside help. That holds up as a practical idea: a flat on a weekend feels very different when the tools are already on your garage shelf. The savings depend on how often you swap and what your shop charges, so run your own numbers before committing.

## The takeaway

Buy in the order you will use things: torque wrench and jack first, inflator next, and the bead tools only when you are ready to mount tires yourself. Then read your owner's manual for lift points and torque specs before you ever raise the car.
