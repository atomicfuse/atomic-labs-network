Voters regularly name the economy as a top concern, so expect the volume to stay high until the votes are counted.

What we can do is help you with the part of the economy you control: what your car actually costs you. By the end of this piece, you'll have a simple way to measure that, and a clearer sense of when a purchase makes sense regardless of the headlines.

## Why Your Own Numbers Beat National Ones

National economic talk is an average. Your garage is not. A commuter who drives 40 miles a day and a retiree who drives 4,000 miles a year face very different costs, even in the same town.

So start with your own data. You don't need a spreadsheet wizard's skills, just a few months of receipts and a calculator.

## Step 1: Total Your Real Monthly Cost

Add up these categories for the last three to six months, then divide by the number of months:

- **Payment or depreciation:** your loan or lease payment, or if you own outright, a rough estimate of how much value the car loses per year.
- **Fuel or charging:** pull from card statements or your charging app.
- **Insurance:** your premium, converted to a monthly figure.
- **Maintenance and repairs:** oil changes, tires, brakes, and anything unexpected.
- **Registration, taxes, and fees:** these vary by state and market, so check your local rules.

The total often surprises people. Many drivers remember the payment and forget the rest.

## Step 2: Find Your Cost Per Mile

Divide your monthly total by the miles you drive in a month. Your odometer and your service records make this easy.

Cost per mile is the number that makes comparisons fair. It lets you put your current car next to a hybrid, an EV, or a newer used model on equal terms.

## Step 3: Compare Fuel Costs for Your Routine

Fuel is the category most tied to price swings, which is why it dominates economic conversation. To compare options, use this approach:

1. Find the EPA fuel economy rating for the exact model year you're considering. Ratings change between years and trims, so verify the specific one.
2. Divide your monthly miles by that mpg (or by miles per kWh for an EV).
3. Multiply by your local price per gallon or per kilowatt-hour.

For EVs, use your actual electric rate, and if you charge at home, check whether your utility offers a cheaper overnight rate. Public fast charging usually costs more than home charging, so be honest about how often you'd rely on it.

## Step 4: Price the Hidden Costs

The sticker price is only the opening bid. Before you commit to any vehicle, get real quotes for:

- **Insurance:** call your insurer with the exact vehicle. Premiums can differ widely between models that look similar.
- **Tires:** replacement costs vary a lot, and heavier vehicles, including many EVs, can wear them faster.
- **Known repair patterns:** for used cars, look up the model year's recall history and common complaints.
- **Financing:** compare your bank, a credit union, and the dealer's offer. A small rate difference compounds over a multi-year loan.

## Step 5: Decide Whether to Buy, Wait, or Keep

Headlines can make a purchase feel urgent or risky. Your numbers should settle it instead.

**Keep your car if** it's paid off, reliable, and its cost per mile is lower than any replacement you've priced. A repair bill that looks painful is often smaller than a year of new-car payments.

**Consider buying if** your current car's repairs are climbing, or a more efficient model would clearly cut your cost per mile for the way you drive.

**Wait if** you can't yet cover the full monthly cost comfortably. Building a repair fund or a larger down payment usually helps more than chasing a deal.

## A Quick Note on Researched vs. Tested

Everything above is a research method, not a firsthand test drive. Specs, fuel ratings, and incentives change by model year and market, so confirm the details for the exact car and location you're considering before you sign anything. Tax credits and incentives for EVs and hybrids in particular can change, so check current official sources rather than relying on older articles, including this one.

## The Takeaway

Whatever the campaign trail says about the economy, spend one evening this week totaling your car's real monthly cost and dividing it by your miles. That single cost-per-mile figure will tell you more about your next car decision than any speech will.
