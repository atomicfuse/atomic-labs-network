A van built for a hobby has to answer one question before anything else: what does the biggest, dirtiest, most awkward piece of gear need? The Moto Roamer, a custom 2025 Mercedes-Benz Sprinter conversion by ADF Sprinters, is a clean case study. Per Autoevolution, it was built to carry two motorbikes while keeping a race team comfortable on long-distance trips. Whether or not you ever race, the way it's organized offers a useful template for planning any gear-first van.

This is a research-based look at the build as reported by Autoevolution. We haven't driven or inspected the van, and the details below are limited to what that report covers.

## Start With the Biggest Item, Then Build Around It

The Moto Roamer's interior is split into two key parts, according to Autoevolution: a massive garage in the back for two motocross bikes, and a living space with basic comforts up front.

That split is the lesson. The garage isn't leftover space after the bed and kitchen were placed. It's the anchor, and the living area was designed to fit around it. If you haul bikes, kayaks, climbing gear, or camera equipment, try this order:

1. Measure your largest item, with the space you need to load and tie it down.
2. Lock in that zone first.
3. Fit sleeping, cooking, and storage into what remains.

Most people do the reverse, then find the gear lives in the passenger seat.

## A Garage Needs to Be Durable, Not Pretty

Autoevolution highlights the van's durable garage as a core feature. Motocross bikes bring mud, fuel, chain oil, and sharp edges. A cargo area for that job should tolerate abuse and be easy to hose or wipe down.

When you plan your own, ask:

- Can the floor take dirt, fluids, and the weight of loaded bikes?
- Can you clean it quickly without damaging anything?
- Is the living space separated well enough that the garage's mess stays in the garage?

## Off-Road Upgrades: Match Them to Where You Actually Go

The report notes the van has off-road upgrades. Race events often mean unpaved parking fields and rough access roads, so a standard cargo van can be a poor fit. The takeaway for your own build isn't to copy a parts list. It's to ask what surfaces your trips really involve. Many travelers pay for capability they never use, while others underbuild and get stuck at the trailhead.

If you're shopping for a conversion, ask the builder for specifics on what was changed and why, and check that the details apply to your model year and market. Sprinter configurations vary by year and region.

## Compact Living: Basics Done Well

The Moto Roamer's living space is described as well-designed, with basic comforts. Per Autoevolution, that includes insulation, a kitchen, and a sleeping setup. None of that is flashy, and that's the point. After a long race day, a team needs three things:

- **A comfortable temperature.** Insulation matters for sleep, whether you're parked somewhere cold or hot.
- **A way to cook and clean up.** A kitchen means you aren't depending on fast food at a remote venue.
- **A real place to sleep.** Recovery is part of performance.

The design principle is to spend your limited square footage on comforts you'll use every trip, and skip the ones that only look good in photos.

## Power Is the Hidden Backbone

The report also points to a high-capacity power system. Autoevolution's summary doesn't give figures in the material we have, so we won't quote any. But the reason it matters is easy to explain. Lights, refrigeration, device charging, and cooking appliances all draw from the same battery bank. Before you commit to a layout, list every device you'd run in a weekend and estimate how long each runs. That tells you whether your power system is adequate or merely hopeful.

## Why Purpose-Built Vans Are Gaining Ground

The trend here is specialization. Instead of generic vanlife styling, buyers increasingly want rigs built around one hobby, and the Moto Roamer sits where motorsports, overlanding, and mobile comfort overlap. That's our read of the trend, not a figure from the report, but it makes sense: a van that does one job well often beats one that does everything adequately.

## What to Borrow for Your Own Build

You don't need a Sprinter or a race team to use this thinking. Here's a quick checklist:

- Define the one use case that matters most.
- Size the cargo zone to your largest item before anything else.
- Choose surfaces that survive your messiest gear.
- Add only the off-road capability your routes require.
- Prioritize insulation, sleep, and a workable kitchen.
- Size your power system to a written list of devices.

Before you sketch a single cabinet, write down the one piece of gear your van must carry without compromise, and design everything else around it.
