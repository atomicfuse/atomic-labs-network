A Utah driver recently bought a 2016 Mercedes-AMG C63 S with very low mileage, and then checked the tires. According to Motor1, the rubber was stamped from early 2017. That detail did two things at once: it backed up the car's low odometer reading, and it raised a safety question. You can run the same check in about 30 seconds, and this guide shows you how.

## Why the tire date matters in this story

The tires on a low-mileage car tell you something the odometer can't. As reported by Motor1, tires dated early 2017 fit a 2016 model that has barely been driven. They suggest the car didn't burn through several sets of rubber, which supports the claim of low miles.

The same fact is also a warning. Tires that are close to a decade old are a concern no matter how much tread is left. A car can look like a bargain on the odometer and still need a full set of tires right away.

## How to read the four-digit date code

Every tire sold in the US carries a DOT code on the sidewall. The part you want is the last four digits of that code, usually inside a small oval or rectangle.

1. Find the DOT stamp on the sidewall.
2. Look at the final four digits.
3. Read the first two as the week of the year and the last two as the year.

So a code ending in 0517 means the tire was made in the fifth week of 2017, roughly early February. That matches the "early 2017" timing in the Utah story.

A few practical tips:

- The full DOT code is sometimes printed only on one side of the tire. If you don't see it, check the inner sidewall or look at the other side of the car.
- Check all four tires, plus the spare if there is one. Mismatched dates can mean a tire was replaced after damage.
- Take a photo of each code so you can compare it with the listing and the service records later.

## Why old tires can be unsafe even with good tread

Tread depth is only part of tire health. Rubber ages whether the car moves or sits. A low-mileage car that spent years parked can have tires that look fine and are still past their safe life.

That's the trap with low-mileage buys, and it's why Motor1's piece frames tire age as a safety issue and not just a cosmetic one. A powerful rear-drive performance car like a C63 S puts a lot of demand on its tires, so aged rubber is a poor match.

When you inspect a used car, look for these signs in addition to the date code:

- Cracks in the sidewall or between the tread blocks
- Dry, hardened-looking rubber
- Bulges or uneven wear

If the tires are many years old, treat replacement as a cost you'll pay immediately and negotiate accordingly. Tire manufacturers and carmakers publish their own guidance on maximum tire age, so check your owner's manual for your specific model and market.

## Tire age as a clue to odometer honesty

This is the second use of the date code. Tires are one of the cheapest ways to sanity-check a story.

- **Dates match the car's age and mileage:** Tires from the car's first year or two on a low-mile example are consistent with a car that hasn't been driven much. That's what the Utah buyer found.
- **Tires are new and the car claims high mileage:** That's normal, because owners replace tires.
- **Tires are much newer than you'd expect on a car that supposedly has very few miles:** It's worth asking why. Maybe the owner replaced them, maybe not, and the answer should show up in the paperwork.

The date code is a clue, not proof. It's one data point that should agree with the others: the service history, the wear on the pedals and seat, and a vehicle history report. If the stories don't line up, ask questions before you pay.

## Low miles don't mean problem-free

Buyers hunting for low-mileage used performance cars are often willing to pay a premium, and that makes it easy to skip age-related checks. Time wears out parts as surely as miles do. Tires are the easiest example to spot, but they're not the only one.

Before you sign, build a short checklist:

1. **Check the tire date codes** on all four tires and the spare.
2. **Inspect the rubber** for cracking, hardening, or bulges.
3. **Compare tire ages** with the odometer and the service records.
4. **Ask for maintenance history,** especially anything that happened because of age and not mileage.
5. **Get a pre-purchase inspection** from an independent mechanic, ideally one familiar with the brand.
6. **Budget for a fresh set of tires** if the current ones are old, and use it as a bargaining chip.

On a performance car, a new set of quality tires can be a significant expense, so it's worth knowing the number before you negotiate and not after.

## What to do on your next car visit

Crouch down at each wheel and read the last four digits of the DOT code before you talk price. If the tires are close to ten years old, count them as a purchase you haven't made yet, and make the seller's price reflect that.
