A Trump administration aide says rolling back Biden-era regulations will save consumers billions of dollars. Jarrod Agen, an assistant to President Donald Trump, made the case on Fox Business's "The Evening Edit," where he discussed the administration's energy policies and the president's efforts to rally voters ahead of Election Day, per Fox Business.

That is a political claim, and the coverage available to us doesn't say which rules are targeted, how the savings were calculated, or how much a household would keep. So this piece won't tell you what your next car will cost. It will show you how to test a claim like this against your own budget, so you can tell a real saving from a headline number.

## What we know, and what we don't

Here is the reported part: an administration aide argues that deregulation will save consumers billions. Agen's remarks came in the context of energy policy.

Here is what the report, as we have it, does not give us:

- A list of the specific regulations being rolled back
- The methodology behind the "billions" figure
- A per-household or per-driver estimate
- Any independent cost-benefit analysis

That gap matters. "Billions" sounds large, but spread across the whole country it can shrink to a modest amount per person. Without the other numbers, treat it as a claim to verify, not a number to budget around.

## Why drivers should care about energy policy

Energy policy touches car ownership in a few direct ways. Fuel is the obvious one. Electricity prices matter if you drive or are considering an EV. Vehicle prices matter when you shop. Any of these could move with policy, but the direction and size depend on details we haven't seen reported here.

The practical move is to stop thinking about "the economy" and look at the line items you actually pay.

## Build a baseline before anything changes

You can't tell whether a policy saved you money unless you know what you were spending. Take 20 minutes and write down your real numbers.

### Fuel or charging

Pull the last three months of fuel receipts or your card statements. Divide total spend by miles driven to get a cost per mile. If you drive an EV, do the same with your home electric bill: note your rate per kilowatt-hour from the bill, plus any public charging charges.

### Insurance, registration and maintenance

Record your annual premium, registration fees and what you spent on service last year. These are the costs that policy debates about energy rarely touch, which is a useful reminder of how much of ownership sits outside the headlines.

### Payment and depreciation

If you're financing, note your rate and monthly payment. If you're shopping, note the price you were quoted and the date. Prices move for many reasons, so a dated record keeps you honest.

## Check the claim like a skeptic

When any official says a policy will save consumers billions, ask four questions.

1. **Which rules, exactly?** A savings claim is only testable once you know what is being changed.
2. **Savings for whom?** Businesses, consumers and taxpayers can see very different effects from the same change.
3. **Over what period?** A figure spread over a decade reads differently from an annual one.
4. **What is the trade-off?** Lighter rules can lower some costs and shift others. Look for independent analysis, not just the side making the argument.

That is analysis, not a finding about this specific claim. We haven't seen the numbers behind it, and it would be wrong to say it is true or false.

## Verify by model year and market

Policy changes can affect different vehicles differently, and details vary by model year and by country or state. If you hear that a change will make cars cheaper, cleaner, or costlier to run, check:

- Whether it applies to new vehicles only, or to ones already on the road
- Whether your state has its own rules that stay in place
- Whether the manufacturer has actually changed pricing, specs, or incentives for the model year you want

Until a dealer quote or a manufacturer's published spec sheet changes, nothing has changed for your purchase.

## A realistic way to use this

If you're buying in the next few months, don't delay or rush on the strength of a headline. Get written quotes from two or three dealers, compare total cost of ownership over five years (price, fuel or charging, insurance, maintenance), and keep the baseline you built. If prices or energy costs do shift, you will see it in your own numbers faster than in any press statement.

If you're not buying, the same logic applies to your current car. Track cost per mile each quarter. A real saving shows up as a lower number.

## The takeaway

Write down what you pay per mile today, then check it again in three months. That one figure will tell you more about whether any policy helped your wallet than a claim about billions ever will.
