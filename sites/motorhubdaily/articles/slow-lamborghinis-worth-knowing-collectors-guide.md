Lamborghini's reputation rests on loud, wedge-shaped poster cars. But some of the most interesting cars in its history were the ones that weren't chasing top speed. Autoevolution recently ran down six Lamborghinis that were slow by the brand's own standards and are hard to find today. This guide uses that list to show what each car is, why it matters, and what to keep in mind if you ever go looking for one.

A caution first: the list we're working from doesn't come with performance figures, production numbers, or prices, so this piece doesn't offer any. If you're seriously considering one of these cars, verify specifications by model year and market before you commit, since details varied across years and regions. Everything below is a researched overview, not a test drive.

## The 400 GT 2+2: Where the grand touring idea starts

The 400 GT 2+2 is the first car on the list, and its name explains the pitch: a grand tourer with room for more than two. Per Autoevolution's framing, this is the side of Lamborghini that appealed to founder Ferruccio Lamborghini himself, who reportedly favored GT comfort over supercar drama.

**What to take from it:** If you think of Lamborghini only as a maker of two-seat statement cars, this is the model that complicates that story. It's a good starting point for understanding the brand's early identity.

## The Islero: A quieter kind of Lamborghini

The Islero is another of the grand tourers on the list. It belongs to the same family of comfortable, long-distance cars rather than the drama-first models that came to define the badge.

**What to take from it:** Rarity is part of the appeal. The source describes these six cars as hard to find today, so expect a long search rather than a casual browse of online listings.

## The Espada: The practical one

The Espada rounds out the early grand tourers in the lineup. It's the car to look at if your idea of an exotic includes the possibility of actually carrying people somewhere.

**What to take from it:** The Espada shows that Lamborghini's history includes cars built around usability. That makes it a useful reference point when you compare these models with the brand's better-known supercars.

## The Jarama 400 GT: Another GT, another overlooked name

The Jarama 400 GT continues the grand touring thread. Like the others here, it's a car most casual fans can't picture, which is exactly why it earns a spot on a list like this.

**What to take from it:** When a model is this far outside the popular image of the brand, buyers and admirers tend to value it for its story rather than its numbers. That's a different way to shop, and it rewards research.

## The Urraco: The "baby" Lambo

The Urraco is one of the so-called baby Lamborghinis the article highlights. It represents the company's effort to build something smaller than its flagship cars, and it sits in the lineage of forgotten V8 Lambos that came well before the modern entry-level era.

**What to take from it:** Smaller and more modest doesn't mean unimportant. According to the article's argument, models like this helped the brand survive beyond its V12 poster cars.

## The LM002: The oddball utility vehicle

The LM002 is the wildcard. It's the utility vehicle on the list, and the strangest entry in a lineup that already strays from what most people expect from the badge.

**What to take from it:** If you want proof that Lamborghini has always experimented beyond raw speed, this is it. A utility vehicle with that badge is a reminder that the company's history is stranger and more varied than its posters suggest.

## Why this matters as Lamborghini changes

Autoevolution's broader point is that Lamborghini is moving deeper into hybridization and wider luxury positioning, and these older cars show the brand has long tried things beyond outright speed. That's the article's take, and it's a reasonable one. Our own read: it suggests that "cool" in car culture increasingly comes from story, rarity, and character, not 0-60 times.

## How to use this list if you're curious about buying

You don't need a collector's budget to benefit from this approach. A few habits carry over to any rare used car:

- **Confirm the exact model year and market.** Specifications for older, low-volume cars can differ, and a listing that lumps variants together isn't proof of anything.
- **Expect scarcity.** Plan on patience, and don't let urgency push you into a rushed decision.
- **Get a specialist inspection.** Rare cars need people who know that specific model, not just a general shop.
- **Buy the story you actually like.** If the appeal is a comfortable GT or a strange utility vehicle, make sure that's what you're getting.

The next time someone says speed is what makes a Lamborghini interesting, point them to the LM002. Then start your research with the model that matches the way you'd actually want to drive.
