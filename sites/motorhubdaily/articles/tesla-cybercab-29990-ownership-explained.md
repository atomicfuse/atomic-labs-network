Tesla has opened order books for the Cybercab at $29,990. Before you picture a driverless car parked in your garage, read the terms. Per Autoevolution, which first reported on the offer, buyers are not the real owners in the traditional sense, because Tesla retains the rights that matter. This guide explains what the deal appears to involve, so you can judge whether it's a car purchase or something closer to a subscription.

## The short version: a sticker price is not ownership

Autoevolution's reading is that the Cybercab "sale" works more like a tightly controlled subscription product than a private purchase. Here is what the outlet reports about the conditions:

- You need an invite to buy.
- You must live inside Tesla Robotaxi geofences.
- You pay $299 per month to operate the car.
- You accept limits on use, modifications and access, plus the possibility of suspension.

That is a very different bundle from the one you get when you buy a typical new car, where the title is yours and the manufacturer cannot decide where you drive it.

## What the $299 a month does to the math

The $29,990 headline is only the entry fee. The monthly operating charge is where the real cost sits, and it's easy to calculate.

- $299 a month is $3,588 a year.
- Over five years, that is $17,940.
- Add the $29,990 purchase price and the five-year total reaches $47,930, before anything else you might pay for.

That figure is my arithmetic from the reported prices, not a number Tesla has published. It also leaves out insurance, charging and any other costs, since we don't have details on those. But it shows why "under $30,000" undersells the commitment.

## Where you can use it

The geofence requirement is the most practical limit. If you live outside a Tesla Robotaxi service area, you reportedly can't take part. Even inside one, the car is tied to that footprint. A conventional car goes wherever roads do, whether that's a road trip or a move to another state. A geofenced vehicle makes your location part of the product.

This is also an area where details matter. We don't have confirmation of how big the zones are, how they may change, or what happens if you relocate. Ask those questions before putting money down.

## What Tesla keeps

Autoevolution says Tesla retains all the rights. In practice, the reported restrictions on modifications, access and suspension mean the company can set the rules for how the car is used and can pause your use of it. That puts the Cybercab in the same conversation as other software-locked products, where a purchase gives you the hardware but not full control.

Liability is the other open question. Autoevolution frames the launch as part of a wider debate about autonomous liability. When a car drives itself, who is responsible if something goes wrong: the owner, the operator or the manufacturer? Reading the fine print on this point should come before everything else. We don't have the specific terms here, so we can't say how Tesla answers it.

## Own one or just hail one?

The most useful comparison is between buying a Cybercab and simply riding in a Tesla Robotaxi when you need it. We don't have Robotaxi fare data, so this is a framework rather than a verdict.

### Buying may make sense if

- You live inside a Robotaxi geofence and expect to stay there.
- You're comfortable with Tesla's limits on modification and access.
- Your usage would be high enough to justify $299 a month plus the purchase price.

### Hailing probably makes more sense if

- You'd use the car only occasionally.
- You don't want a fixed monthly bill.
- You'd rather not accept the risk of suspension or the unresolved liability questions.

If you're counting every dollar, run your own numbers: estimate your weekly rides, multiply by the local fare once you know it, and compare that to roughly $3,588 a year in fees alone.

## Why this is also a credibility test for Tesla

Per the same coverage, Tesla treats the Cybercab as central to its future, and Elon Musk promised to sell one to regular customers before the end of 2026. Opening orders by invitation, with these conditions, lets Tesla claim progress on that promise. Whether an invite-only, geofenced, subscription-tied sale satisfies what most people mean by selling a car to regular customers is a fair thing to question. That's my take, not a reported fact, and buyers can decide for themselves.

## Questions to ask before you order

1. Who is liable if the car is involved in a crash?
2. What exactly can Tesla suspend, and under what conditions?
3. What happens to the car if you move outside a geofence?
4. Can you resell it, and does the buyer need an invite too?
5. What does the $299 monthly fee cover, and can it change?

We can't answer these from the information published so far, and that's the point. A car you can't fully control, can't freely move and can't be sure you can resell isn't priced by its sticker alone.

**Before you get excited about $29,990, get the full terms in writing and price the whole arrangement, not just the car.**
