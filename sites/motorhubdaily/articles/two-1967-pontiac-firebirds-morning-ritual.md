If you're shopping for a classic and the Camaro and Mustang prices make you wince, a story from Tennessee offers a useful nudge: look at the car that shared the showroom with them. Per Autoevolution, Grammy-winning musician and sound engineer Julian King owns two 1967 Pontiac Firebirds, and the way he lives with them says more about classic ownership than any auction result.

## The Cars: One Model Year, Two Personalities

According to Autoevolution's profile, King's pair includes a red convertible and a Plum Mist Ram Air 400 coupe. Same year, same nameplate, different characters.

That split is a handy way to think about your own purchase:

- **Convertible:** The open-air choice. It suits a car you plan to drive for the experience of driving.
- **Coupe:** The more traditional shape, and in King's case it's the one wearing the Ram Air 400 badge and a distinctive Plum Mist color.

I can't tell you from the profile how the two compare in value, condition, or driving feel, so treat that as your own homework. Specifications and prices vary by model year, trim, and market, and a Ram Air car in particular needs documentation to confirm it is what it claims to be.

## Why a Firebird Is Worth a Look

The Firebird argument is simple: everyone knows the Camaro and Mustang, which means everyone is also bidding on them. Autoevolution's piece points to enthusiasts looking past those obvious collector picks toward more distinctive classics. That's a trend, not a guarantee of anything about future values, so don't buy one as an investment on that basis alone.

What you can say is that a less common pony car gives you a conversation starter. Park a '67 Firebird next to a row of Mustangs and you'll be the one answering questions.

### A quick checklist before you buy any 1960s pony car

This is general used-classic advice, not something from King's story:

1. **Inspect for rust first.** Check floors, trunk pan, and the lower body panels. Mechanical work is usually cheaper than metal repair.
2. **Verify the details.** If a car is sold as a special variant, ask for paperwork and have a marque specialist check numbers.
3. **Look at the convertible top and frame.** Tops, seals, and the structure around them can be costly to fix.
4. **Budget for the unglamorous items.** Tires, brakes, fuel system, and wiring are where old cars often need attention.
5. **Drive it before you decide.** Photos hide a lot.

## The Morning Ritual Is the Real Takeaway

The detail that makes King's story stick isn't the horsepower. Autoevolution's description is of a man who wakes up early, watches the sun rise from his driveway, and keeps the garage doors open to his cars. He quietly wrenches on them before the day gets going.

There's a practical lesson in that. Classic cars stay healthy when you spend small, regular time with them. A few minutes of looking and listening tends to catch problems early:

- Look under the car for fresh drips.
- Check fluid levels and tire pressures.
- Glance at belts, hoses, and battery terminals.
- Start it, let it settle, and listen.

That routine costs nothing and turns maintenance from a dreaded project into a habit. Whether King does exactly this I can't say; the profile describes the early-morning tinkering, not his checklist. But the habit is what keeps an old car usable instead of a garage ornament.

## Cars as a Family and Creative Thread

Autoevolution frames King's Firebirds as intersecting with family, music, and his work as a sound engineer. That's a reminder that people tend to keep classics for reasons beyond the spec sheet. If you're weighing a purchase, ask what role the car will play: a weekend drive with your kids, a shared project, or a place to unwind before work.

The answer helps you pick between body styles, set a realistic budget, and decide how much work you actually want to do yourself.

## Owning "the Same" Car Twice

Two 1967 Firebirds might sound redundant, but the pairing makes sense when the cars differ in body style and mission. If you're tempted by a second one, be honest about the practical side: space, insurance, storage, and time. A second project doubles the maintenance, not just the fun. Many owners find that one car gets driven and the other gets worked on, which is a perfectly good arrangement if you plan for it.

Before you chase the famous name, spend an afternoon looking at the cars parked beside it.
