The Vespa badge belongs to one of the most recognizable scooters ever made. But Piaggio also put that nameplate on a tiny car, the 1959 Vespa 400, and a restored example is now listed for sale in Florida with a $37,500 asking price, per Autoevolution's report.

This piece uses that listing to answer a practical question: how do you decide whether a rare microcar is a smart collectible or an expensive curiosity? You will leave with a clear picture of what this car is and a short checklist for judging any quirky classic.

## What the Vespa 400 actually is

The Vespa 400 is a microcar, not a scooter with extra wheels. Autoevolution describes it as a product of postwar microcar thinking: small, economical transportation built for a Europe that needed affordable mobility. Piaggio built it under the Vespa name, which is why it surprises people who only know the two-wheelers.

Its production run was short, which is the main reason it counts as rare today. Autoevolution also cites a modest 14 horsepower, a figure that tells you what this car was built for: simple, low-cost, close-quarters driving rather than speed.

## The car for sale

The example in the listing is a 1959 model, restored and finished in Narvik Blue, located in Florida. The asking price is $37,500.

I have not seen or driven this car, and the details here come from Autoevolution's coverage of the listing. Treat an asking price as a seller's opinion, not a market verdict. Only a completed sale tells you what a buyer actually paid.

## Why tiny cars like this matter

Microcars came from a specific need: people wanted a roof and four wheels without the cost of a full-size car. That context is half the appeal now. A vintage microcar is a small, space-efficient piece of history with plenty of nostalgia.

Autoevolution's framing suggests collector attention is moving toward cars like this, once overlooked and now treated as serious enthusiast-market items. That is the publication's read of the market, and one listing does not prove a trend. It does show that a seller believes there is a buyer at that level.

## Is $37,500 too much for a 14-hp car?

Horsepower is the wrong yardstick. Collectors of rare microcars pay for scarcity, story, and condition. A few questions give you a better read:

### How rare is it, really?

A short production run helps, but rarity only matters if people want the car. The Vespa name adds a hook that most obscure microcars lack.

### What does "restored" mean here?

Ask for documentation: photos of the work, receipts, and who did it. A cosmetic refresh and a mechanical rebuild are very different things, and the listing summary does not say which this is.

### Can you get parts and service?

Obscure cars can be costly to keep running. Before buying any rare microcar, find out who can service it and where parts come from. This belongs in your ownership cost estimate alongside the purchase price.

### How will you use it?

A 14-hp car suits short, gentle drives and car shows. If you want something for regular highway commuting, a car like this is the wrong tool no matter the price.

### What are comparable sales?

Look for recent sold prices of similar European microcars, not just asking prices. That gives you a ceiling and a floor.

## A quick buyer's checklist for any rare classic

1. Get the paperwork: title, history, restoration records.
2. Inspect in person or hire a specialist who knows the model.
3. Price the upkeep, including parts sourcing and storage.
4. Compare sold prices, not listings.
5. Decide whether you are buying it to enjoy or to hold as an investment. Enjoyment is the safer reason.

## The takeaway

The Vespa 400 is charming, and the surprise factor is real. Whether $37,500 is fair depends on documentation, condition, and your own use. If you are tempted by a rare microcar, do the homework first: verify the restoration, check the sold comps, and buy it because you want to drive and show it.
