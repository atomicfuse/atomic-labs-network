You've probably seen it on the back of a semi or a rental moving truck: a plate with the word "Apportioned" where you'd expect a state slogan. By the end of this article, you'll know what that word means, why a truck from one state can run legally across dozens of others, and why a fleet's plates often come from a state you wouldn't expect.

The short version, per Slashgear's explainer: an apportioned plate marks a commercial vehicle registered under the International Registration Plan, or IRP.

## What an Apportioned Plate Means

The IRP is a system that lets commercial fleets operate across multiple states and provinces without registering separately in each one. Instead of a stack of registrations, the carrier registers through one base jurisdiction and gets one plate. The "Apportioned" label tells you the vehicle is covered under that agreement.

The key word is the one on the plate. Registration fees are apportioned, meaning divided, among the jurisdictions the vehicle travels through.

## How the Fee Splitting Works

Under the IRP, registration fees are divided based on how far a vehicle travels in each jurisdiction. A truck that logs most of its miles in two states and a few in a third pays a share of its fees to each, weighted by distance, rather than paying full registration to every state it touches.

That has two practical effects for the carrier:

- **Less paperwork.** One registration process replaces many.
- **Fairer costs.** Fees track actual road use instead of a flat charge everywhere.

Per Slashgear, this simplifies fleet operations and reduces compliance complexity for carriers working across state lines.

## Why You Rarely See Trucks With Multiple Plates

Before a system like this, a truck crossing several states would have needed separate registration for each. Today, one apportioned plate does the job. If you notice that long-haul trucks seem to carry just a single plate, that's the IRP at work. It's a big part of why interstate trucking looks so uncluttered on the registration side.

## Apportioned vs. Regular Commercial Plates

The difference comes down to scope. A standard commercial plate generally ties a vehicle to registration in a single state. An apportioned plate signals registration through the IRP, which is built for vehicles that operate in multiple jurisdictions. Same idea, a business vehicle with a business registration, but the apportioned version is designed for crossing borders as part of the job.

For a local plumber's van that never leaves one state, the IRP isn't the right tool. For a carrier running routes through many states and provinces, it is.

## Why U-Haul Trucks Share a Home State

Here's the detail that explains something many drivers have noticed. Large fleets like U-Haul register their operations through one base state, and per the Slashgear piece that state is Arizona. That's why a rental truck you see in Ohio or Georgia may carry Arizona-linked registration even though you rented it nearby.

It's not a sign that the truck is far from home or improperly registered. It's the IRP letting a national fleet manage registration from one place, with fees still divided among the jurisdictions where the trucks actually drive.

## Who Qualifies

Not every vehicle can use the IRP. It's aimed at commercial carriers operating across state or provincial lines, and carriers need to meet eligibility requirements to register. The specific thresholds vary, and the source summary doesn't spell them out, so if you run a business vehicle and wonder whether you qualify, check with your base state's motor vehicle agency before assuming either way. Rules and fee structures can differ by jurisdiction and change over time.

## What This Means If You're Not a Trucker

Most drivers will never register an apportioned vehicle. But the knowledge pays off in small ways:

- **Renting a moving truck?** An out-of-state plate on the vehicle is normal for national fleets.
- **Shopping for work vehicles for a small business?** If your routes stay in one state, standard commercial registration is likely what applies. If they cross lines regularly, ask about the IRP.
- **Working near logistics?** Knowing the plate tells you how a carrier handles registration across its whole operation.

## The Takeaway

Next time you spot "Apportioned" on a plate, read it as shorthand: this vehicle is built to cross borders, and its registration costs are shared by the places it drives. If you run a business vehicle that regularly leaves your state, call your state's motor vehicle agency and ask whether IRP registration fits your routes.
