Pull up to a green curb and you have a short window: typically 15 to 20 minutes. That's long enough to grab a takeout order or drop off a package, and not long enough to park while you run errands or sit through a meeting. Here's how to read a green curb correctly so a quick stop doesn't turn into a fine.

The basics come from SlashGear's explainer on curb colors, and we'll stick to what it reports. The most important point is that rules vary by jurisdiction, so the paint tells you only part of the story.

## What a green curb means

Per SlashGear, green curbs mark short-term parking spaces. The limit is typically 15 to 20 minutes. These spaces are designed for quick stops, not extended or overnight parking.

Think of them as a turnover zone. A business wants customers coming and going, so the space is meant to be used briefly and then freed up for the next person.

## Why the paint isn't the whole answer

The time limit is a typical figure, not a universal one. Local rules can differ, and they may include:

- Different time restrictions
- Specific hours when the limit applies
- Special conditions posted on nearby signage

So treat the green paint as a signal to look around, not as permission to relax. If a sign says 10 minutes, or says the limit applies only during certain hours, the sign is what you follow. SlashGear notes that this extra information is usually found on signage close to the space.

## A quick routine before you leave the car

This takes about ten seconds and is the best ticket prevention there is.

1. **Spot the color.** Green means short-term. Note it as you pull in.
2. **Find the sign.** Look at the nearest pole or wall for time limits, hours, or conditions.
3. **Note your arrival time.** Put a reminder on your phone for a few minutes before the limit ends. If the limit is 15 minutes, set it for 10.
4. **Plan your exit.** If your errand might run long, park somewhere else. A longer spot costs less than a ticket.

## Why it's worth the effort

Urban parking keeps getting more regulated and more crowded, per SlashGear, which makes understanding curb colors more useful than it used to be. Violations can be costly, and they're also avoidable.

Our take: the most common mistake is probably treating a short-term space as a normal one because the stop "will only take a minute." Minutes pass quickly when you're in a line or waiting on an order. Setting a timer removes the guesswork.

## Don't assume other colors work the same way

Green is only one part of the curb color system. Other colors carry their own meanings, and the rules behind them can also differ by place. This article covers green only, because that's what we can support. If you're unsure about a yellow, red, blue, or unpainted curb, check the posted signs or your local parking authority's website rather than guessing.

## Driving somewhere new

If you're in a city you don't know, assume nothing. A green curb at home may follow different hours or limits than one across town or in another state. Reading the sign every time is a small habit that protects you wherever you drive.

## The takeaway

When you see green paint, treat it as a short stop and a prompt to read the nearest sign. Set a timer, and if your errand might run past 15 minutes, move your car to a spot that suits the time you actually need.
