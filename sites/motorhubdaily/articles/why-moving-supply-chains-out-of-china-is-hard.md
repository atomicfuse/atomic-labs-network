Western companies keep saying they want to rely less on China. Actually doing it is another matter, and the gap between the talk and the reality helps explain why some car parts, batteries and electronics don't get cheaper or simpler to source on the timeline headlines suggest.

Forbes recently examined why moving supply chains out of China is easier said than done. Francis Bassolino, Managing Partner at Alaris, argues that Western companies "need to break their dependence on China so that they have more control over their business." The harder question is how long that takes and what it costs.

This article is a general look at that business problem, based on Forbes' reporting. It does not cover specific automakers, models or parts, and nothing here is a claim about any particular vehicle's supply chain.

## The pressure to leave is real

Per the Forbes report, geopolitical tensions, trade restrictions and recent supply chain disruptions have made reducing reliance on China a corporate priority. When one country sits at the center of so much production, a single disruption can ripple through everything downstream.

For drivers, that is the logic behind "de-risking." It is less about leaving a market than about not having all your eggs in one basket.

## Why the exit is slow

The reported challenge is that the practical and financial hurdles of moving operations remain largely underestimated by both companies and policymakers. Several factors explain why.

### China's infrastructure and cost advantages are hard to copy

One of the key angles in the coverage is that China maintains infrastructure and labor cost advantages that competitors cannot easily replicate. A new location may match one strength, but matching the whole package is harder.

### "Nearshoring" needs big upfront money

Moving production to India, Vietnam or Mexico sounds straightforward. In practice, it requires massive upfront investment, per the angles outlined in the reporting. A company pays to build or qualify new capacity before it sees any savings, and that can make the first years look expensive on paper.

### Existing relationships and scale keep suppliers in place

Established vendor relationships and scale economies also keep production anchored in China. A supplier network built over years does not transfer overnight, and a smaller new operation rarely matches the unit costs of a large existing one.

### Geopolitical risk doesn't automatically become a plan

Worry about tensions is easy to express. Turning it into a concrete supply chain strategy is harder, and the reporting suggests that's where the approach can fall short: risk is identified, but execution lags.

## The timeline: years, not quarters

The framing in the source material is that reshoring is a 5 to 10 year project, not a quick pivot. That is the most useful number for anyone trying to read corporate announcements. A promise to "diversify" this year is a long-range commitment, not a near-term change.

## What this means for you as a car owner

This is my own analysis, not something Forbes reported about the auto industry: if supply chain shifts take this long and cost this much upfront, don't expect fast price drops or sudden changes in where parts come from. Some practical habits follow.

- **Treat sourcing claims as long-term.** When a brand says it is moving production, it is describing a multi-year effort.
- **Ask about parts availability before you buy.** For a new or used car, check how easily replacement parts and service items can be found. A dealer or independent shop can tell you what is routinely in stock for a given model year and market.
- **Factor in repair timing.** Parts that depend on long, concentrated supply lines are the ones most exposed to delays, so build some patience into your ownership plans.
- **Keep up with routine maintenance.** Staying ahead of wear items reduces the chance you need a hard-to-get part at a bad moment.

## The takeaway

When you hear that a company is cutting its dependence on China, read it as a decade-long project rather than a switch it can flip. Plan your own car purchases and repairs around the world as it is now, and check parts availability before you commit.
