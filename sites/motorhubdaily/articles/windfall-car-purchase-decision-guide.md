Fox Business host Charles Payne recently shared the story of his first windfall, which came when he was a teenager in Harlem. He told it at the network's "Undeniable Windfalls Town Hall," per Fox Business. The coverage we have doesn't spell out the details, so we won't guess at them. But the theme is useful for anyone who has ever come into a lump sum: what you do with the first real money tends to shape the habits that follow.

For many drivers, that lump sum, whether a bonus, a tax refund, a settlement, or a gift, gets pointed straight at a car. This guide shows how to decide if that's the right move, and how to count the full cost before you sign anything.

## Pause before the showroom

The most common windfall mistake is speed. Money feels urgent when it arrives, and a dealership is very good at turning that feeling into a purchase.

Try a simple rule: park the money for a set period, such as 30 days, in a savings account. If the car still makes sense after that, you've lost nothing. If it doesn't, you've saved yourself a bad decision. This is our suggestion, not something from Payne's story, but it works with any lump sum.

## Fix what you already own first

Before buying anything new, check whether your current car needs money to stay dependable. A windfall can go further here than you'd expect.

- **Overdue maintenance:** Fluids, brakes, and tires are the usual suspects. Your owner's manual lists the service schedule for your specific model year.
- **Tires:** Worn tires hurt braking and wet-weather grip. A new set is a safety purchase, not an upgrade.
- **Get a written estimate:** Ask a trusted shop what the car needs over the next year. Compare that total to the cost of replacing the car.

If the repair bill is small next to a replacement, keeping the car is often the smarter use of cash.

## Price the whole car, not the sticker

If you do decide to buy, a lump sum can lower or erase your financing, but the purchase price is only part of ownership. Add up each of these before comparing vehicles:

1. **Insurance:** Get quotes for the exact model and trim. Rates can differ widely between similar cars.
2. **Fuel or charging:** Estimate your yearly mileage, then compare fuel costs with electricity costs at your local rates.
3. **Maintenance:** Look up the recommended service schedule for the model year you're considering.
4. **Registration and taxes:** These vary by state and market.
5. **Depreciation:** New cars generally lose value fastest early on, so think about what you could sell it for later.

This kind of side-by-side math is how you compare a gas car, a hybrid, and an EV fairly. Check specifications and incentives for your model year and market, since both change.

## Don't put the whole amount in one place

A windfall rarely needs to be spent in a single move. A split can give you a car upgrade and a cushion at the same time. For example, you might:

- Keep an emergency fund topped up, since car repairs tend to arrive unannounced.
- Put a larger down payment on a car to reduce the loan balance and interest paid.
- Set aside a portion for the first year of insurance and maintenance.

The exact percentages depend on your situation. The point is that the car shouldn't swallow everything.

## Buy used with a plan

If stretching the money matters, a used vehicle can lower both the price and the depreciation hit. Before you buy:

- Request the vehicle history report.
- Have an independent mechanic inspect it.
- Test drive it on roads you actually use, including the highway.
- Check for open recalls using the VIN.

On a used EV or hybrid, ask about the condition of the battery and any remaining warranty coverage, which can vary by model year and market.

## Why early money moments matter

Payne's story landed because first windfalls are memorable. They set expectations about what money is for. A first windfall that goes toward something durable, whether that's a reliable car, a paid-off loan, or a safety net, tends to build a habit of thinking before spending.

That's our read, not a claim from Payne. But it's a good lens for any big purchase.

## The takeaway

When a lump sum lands, wait a few weeks, price the full cost of ownership, and only then decide whether a car belongs on the list. The best deal you can make is the one you didn't rush.
