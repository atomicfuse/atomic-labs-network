A jury awarded scientist David Sabatini $6 million after rejecting sexual-misconduct claims that, per Fox News, ended his MIT career and shuttered his Whitehead Institute lab. The piece, an opinion column by Mike Davis, argues the case shows MIT and Whitehead mishandled the allegations and calls for a DOJ investigation. That is the author's argument, not a settled finding, and this article takes no side on it.

There is a practical lesson here for any reader, whatever you think of the case. When your job, reputation, or livelihood is questioned, the person with clear, dated, backed-up records is in a far stronger position. By the end of this guide, you will have a simple setup for keeping your own work records safe, organized, and retrievable.

## Why records matter when a dispute lands

The column frames the Sabatini case as a due-process story: what an institution owes someone before taking career-ending action. Whatever the merits, the general point holds in any workplace or organization. Disputes often turn on what can be shown, not what people remember. Emails, calendar entries, and saved files are the kind of material that can help you explain what happened and when.

Two cautions up front. First, keep records of your own work and communications that you are legitimately entitled to keep. Do not copy confidential or proprietary files to personal accounts if your employer's policy forbids it. Second, if you are in a real dispute, a lawyer, not a blog, should guide what you collect and how.

## Step 1: Check what your employer allows

Before you save anything, read your employer's acceptable-use or data policy. Look for rules on:

- Forwarding work email to personal accounts
- Storing work files on personal devices or cloud services
- Taking screenshots of internal systems

If the policy is unclear, ask HR or IT in writing. Their reply is itself a useful record.

## Step 2: Keep a simple running work log

This is the highest-value habit and costs almost nothing.

1. Create a document or note called "Work Log" in your notes app of choice (Apple Notes, Google Keep, or any plain text editor works).
2. At the end of each day, add one dated line: what you worked on, who you spoke with, and any decisions made.
3. Keep entries factual. Skip opinions and speculation.
4. Do it consistently. A log that is updated daily is more believable than one written in a rush later.

## Step 3: Back up your own files using the 3-2-1 rule

The 3-2-1 rule is a standard, easy-to-follow backup approach: keep three copies of important files, on two different types of storage, with one copy offsite.

A practical version for most people:

1. **Copy one:** the working file on your computer.
2. **Copy two:** an external drive. Plug it in weekly and copy your documents folder over. On Windows, File History does this automatically; on a Mac, Time Machine does.
3. **Copy three:** a cloud service you personally control, such as iCloud, Google Drive, or Dropbox, for files you are permitted to store there.

Check your settings on whatever version of your operating system you run, since menu names shift between updates. Search for "backup" in your system settings to find the right screen.

## Step 4: Name and organize files so you can find them

A backup you cannot search is nearly useless. Use a consistent pattern:

- Start file names with the date in year-month-day format, such as 2026-10-25_project-summary.docx
- Use one folder per project or topic
- Avoid names like "final_v2_REAL"

This ordering sorts files chronologically on any device, which makes it easy to reconstruct a timeline.

## Step 5: Lock down your accounts

Records only help if they stay yours and stay intact. Take ten minutes to:

1. Turn on two-factor authentication for your email and cloud accounts, using an authenticator app rather than text messages where possible.
2. Use a password manager to create unique passwords for each account.
3. Review which devices are signed in, and remove any you do not recognize.
4. Set a recovery email or phone number, and make sure it is current.

## Step 6: Preserve important messages properly

If a message matters, do not rely on memory or a cropped screenshot alone. Keep the original email in your mailbox, export important threads to PDF where your policy allows, and note the date you saved them. For text conversations, both iPhone and Android let you take screenshots, but keep the original thread on the phone too, since the full context is what counts.

## Step 7: Know when to stop DIY and call a professional

If you are facing an investigation, a formal complaint, or a possible termination, stop improvising. Avoid deleting anything, since removing records during a dispute can create its own problems. Contact an employment attorney or your union representative, and show them what you have kept.

## The takeaway

Whatever your view of the Sabatini case and the column's call for a DOJ probe, the everyday lesson is plain: set up a daily work log and a 3-2-1 backup this week, within your employer's rules, so your records are ready long before anyone asks for them.
