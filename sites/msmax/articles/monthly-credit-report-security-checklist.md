A clean credit report feels like proof that your identity is safe. It isn't. Per Fox News, criminals have at least six ways to steal from you without ever triggering a credit monitoring alert. This guide shows you what credit monitoring tends to miss and gives you a monthly routine to catch the rest.

Credit monitoring watches for new accounts and loans opened in your name. Identity theft has spread well beyond that into phones, benefits, healthcare, and data broker exposure. If you only watch your score, you may miss faster-moving scams that drain accounts or take over your access.

## What Credit Monitoring Can Miss

Here are the six categories Fox News flagged, with a plain-English note on each.

### 1. Existing bank account takeover

If someone gets into an account you already have, no new credit line is opened, so nothing shows up on your credit report. The first sign is money moving that you didn't move.

### 2. Government benefits and tax fraud

Someone can use your identity to claim benefits or file taxes. Neither necessarily touches your credit file.

### 3. Medical identity theft

Your information can be used to get healthcare services. Again, your credit score may look perfectly normal.

### 4. SIM swap and phone account fraud

A SIM swap is when a criminal gets your phone number moved to a SIM they control. That can compromise access to anything that texts you a verification code, such as banking logins.

### 5. Scams involving direct payments

If you are tricked into sending money yourself, no credit account is involved. Your report stays clean while your balance shrinks.

### 6. Personal information exposed through data brokers

Data brokers collect and sell personal details. That exposure can feed other fraud without ever appearing as a credit event.

## Your Monthly Identity Check, Step by Step

Set a recurring calendar reminder for the same day each month, say the first Saturday. Each step below matches one of the risks above. The whole routine should be quick once you've done it a couple of times.

### Step 1: Review your financial accounts regularly

Open each bank and card account in its app or website and scan recent transactions. Look for charges you don't recognize, even small ones. Also confirm that your contact details, such as email and phone number, are still yours.

### Step 2: Watch for suspicious tax activity

Stay alert for anything odd related to your taxes, such as notices about returns or income you didn't file or earn. An unexpected letter is a reason to investigate, not to ignore.

### Step 3: Check your Social Security earnings record

Review your Social Security earnings record for work or income that isn't yours. Wages you never earned are a sign someone may be using your identity.

### Step 4: Review medical bills and insurance statements

Read the explanation-of-benefits statements your insurer sends, and compare them with care you actually received. Services you don't recall are a red flag for medical identity theft.

### Step 5: Protect your mobile phone account

Your phone number is a key to many other accounts, so treat the carrier account like a bank account. Practical moves:

- Log in to your carrier account (on your phone's browser or the carrier's app) and look for a security or account protection section.
- Add a PIN or passcode to the account if one is offered.
- Watch for sudden loss of service, such as your phone showing no signal for no reason. Per general SIM swap guidance, that can be a warning sign, so contact your carrier right away from another device.

Menu names vary by carrier and app version, so check your carrier's own help pages for exact steps.

### Step 6: Check where your personal information appears online

Search your own name and see what shows up, including on data broker sites. Knowing what's public tells you what a scammer could use against you. Many brokers offer ways to request removal, though that process differs by site.

## Know the Limits of This Routine

This checklist helps you spot problems early. It can't stop a direct-payment scam, because in that case you are the one authorizing the transfer. The habit that helps there is simple: pause before sending money to anyone who contacted you first, and verify the request through a channel you trust.

## The Takeaway

Pick a day this week, put a monthly reminder on your calendar, and walk through all six steps once. A clean credit score is one good sign. It is not the whole picture.
