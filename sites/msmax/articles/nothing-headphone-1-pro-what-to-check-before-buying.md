The $400 Nothing Headphone 1 Pro looks great, feels great to use, and has strong noise cancelling. Per Marques Brownlee's review, the one place it stumbles is the thing you're paying headphones to do: sound the best. Here is how to decide whether that trade-off works for you, and how to test it yourself before you commit.

## What Brownlee found

Brownlee's review frames the headphones as Nothing's move into a crowded premium noise-cancelling market, where Sony, Bose, Beats and Apple already set high expectations. At $400, buyers reasonably expect near-flagship performance with very few compromises.

His verdict, in short:

- **Strengths:** design, materials, physical controls, battery life, and an improved ANC and transparency mode that he found better than expected.
- **Weakness:** sound quality. It's fun, but he says it lacks bass punch and instrument separation compared with top rivals.
- **His main criticism:** Nothing copied the wrong competitive trend, taking on a pro-tier price without matching top-tier audio.

That's one reviewer's take, and sound is personal. But it gives you a useful checklist.

## Decide what you actually value

Premium headphones compete on five things. Rank them before you shop, because no pair wins all five.

1. **Sound quality** (bass punch, separation between instruments)
2. **Noise cancelling and transparency mode**
3. **Controls** (physical buttons versus touch)
4. **Design and materials**
5. **Battery life**

If you rank sound first, Brownlee's review suggests the Headphone 1 Pro may not be your pick at this price. If you rank design, controls and ANC higher, it looks much more appealing.

## Why physical controls matter more than they sound

Brownlee lists the physical controls as a major strength. In daily use, buttons and dials you can find by feel beat touch panels that misfire with gloves, hair or a wet hand. When you compare headphones, test whether you can pause, skip and change volume without looking.

## What the flat EQ mode is for

The headphones include a switch for a flat, more neutral sound mode, which the review describes as creator-friendly. A neutral profile aims to avoid boosting bass or treble, so you hear a recording closer to how it was made. That can help if you edit audio or video on the go.

Here's a simple way to test any flat mode, on these or other headphones:

1. Pick a track you know extremely well, ideally a high-quality file.
2. Listen in the default mode, then switch to flat.
3. Listen for whether vocals, guitars and drums sit in distinct places.
4. Play a second, very different track (a dense mix works well) and repeat.
5. If you edit audio, check a clip of your own work and compare it on a second pair of headphones or speakers.

Don't treat any headphones as the final judge of a mix. Cross-checking on a second device is good practice regardless.

## How to test for bass punch and separation

Brownlee's two sound complaints are specific, so you can check for them directly.

- **Bass punch:** Play a track with a clear kick drum or bass line. Does it hit, or does it feel soft and rounded?
- **Instrument separation:** Play a busy track, such as a band or orchestra. Can you pick out individual instruments, or do they blur together?

Run the same tracks on a pair of Sony, Bose, Beats or Apple headphones back to back. Same songs, same volume, same fit. The differences are easier to hear in direct comparison than from memory.

## Test the noise cancelling in the real world

A quiet store isn't a fair test. If you can, try the ANC somewhere representative of your day: a commute, an open office, a busy kitchen. Check both the cancelling and the transparency mode, which lets outside sound in so you can hear announcements or a conversation. Brownlee found Nothing's improved here, so it's worth hearing for yourself.

## Check the return policy first

Because sound is subjective, the safest move is buying from a retailer with an easy return window and spending a few days with them. Use your normal playlists, your normal commute and your normal calls. If the sound disappoints you the way it did Brownlee, send them back.

## Who should consider them

Based on the review, the Headphone 1 Pro makes the most sense if you value looks, build, tactile controls, ANC and battery life, and you're happy with a fun, less precise sound. If you want the strongest bass impact and instrument detail at around $400, audition the established flagships alongside it before you decide.

The takeaway: before spending $400 on any headphones, play the same three songs on each contender and buy the pair whose sound you still like after a week.
