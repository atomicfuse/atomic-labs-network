A text offers you a few hundred pounds for letting someone move money through your bank account. No hacking, no real work. It sounds like a favor. It may be a crime, and you could be the one who pays for it.

This guide shows you how money-mule recruitment typically works online, the red flags to check for, and what to do on your phone the moment something feels off.

## Why this matters: the numbers

Per an analysis reported by Phys, more than 8,900 people under 30 were arrested for money laundering offenses in England and Wales between 2020 and 2025. That is more than six in 10 of all money laundering arrests recorded in that period.

The analysis also notes that the U.K.'s 2025 national risk assessment flagged schools and universities as an emerging money-laundering risk. It argues that students, especially international students, are vulnerable because of online recruitment, financial pressure, and low awareness of the legal risks.

## What a money mule actually is

A money mule is someone whose bank account is used to move money for others. Organized crime groups use mules because it helps hide where the money came from. The account holder, not the criminal behind the scheme, is the person whose name sits on the bank records.

That is the core danger. You may never meet the people involved, but your identity is attached to every transaction.

## Red flags to check before you reply

The reporting highlights fake job ads, requests for bank account access, and approaches on social media as typical routes. Use this checklist on any offer that touches your money.

### The offer is too easy
Any job that pays well for little effort, no interview, and no experience deserves suspicion. "Easy money" is the hook.

### It asks for your bank details
Legitimate employers need your details only after a proper hiring process, usually through official paperwork. Be wary if a stranger, a chat-app contact, or a vague "recruiter" asks to use your account or wants your login.

### It arrives through social media or messaging
Unsolicited direct messages, group chats, and posts promising quick cash are common recruitment spots. Treat an approach from someone you don't know as a warning sign by itself.

### You're told to keep a cut and send the rest
Being asked to receive money and forward it elsewhere, keeping a percentage, is the classic mule pattern.

### You're new, short on cash, or unfamiliar with local rules
The analysis points to financial pressure and low legal awareness as vulnerabilities. If you have recently moved to a new country, the pressure to find income quickly can make a risky offer look reasonable. Slow down and ask someone you trust.

## Step-by-step: what to do on your phone

These steps work on any recent iPhone or Android phone and any major banking app. Menu names vary slightly by app version, so look for the closest match.

### Step 1: Don't reply or move money
Stop the conversation. Do not send a test payment or share a screenshot of your banking app.

### Step 2: Screenshot the evidence
Capture the ad, profile, and messages before they vanish. This helps if you need to report it.

### Step 3: Report and block
Use the report option inside the social platform or messaging app, then block the account.

### Step 4: Tell your bank if you shared anything
If you gave out login details or let someone use your account, contact your bank right away using the number on your card or in the official app, not one the contact gave you. Ask them to secure the account.

### Step 5: Lock down your logins
Change your banking password, turn on two-factor authentication, and change the password on any account that reuses it. A password manager makes unique passwords easy.

### Step 6: Talk to your university or the police
If you are a student, your university's support team can help you decide what to do next. If money has already moved through your account, seek advice promptly rather than hoping it goes away.

## Check incoming money, too

A mule scheme can start with an unexpected deposit followed by a message saying it was sent by mistake and asking you to forward it. Don't send it on. Contact your bank and let them handle the return.

## For parents and friends

The practical move is to have a plain conversation. Explain that letting someone else use your account is not a harmless favor, and that students are being targeted online. Agree on a simple rule: nobody gets bank details or app access without a check with someone else first.

## The legal risk is real

The figures above come from arrests for money laundering offenses, which shows that young people are not being treated as bystanders. The analysis stresses low awareness of legal risks among students. This article is general information, not legal advice, so if you are worried about your situation, speak to a qualified adviser.

## Universities are part of the story

The analysis also points to gaps in universities' anti-money-laundering responsibilities, and the 2025 risk assessment flagging education settings suggests the issue is on regulators' radar. In the meantime, personal habits do the most work: you control who sees your account.

## The one rule to remember

Never let anyone else use your bank account, for any amount, for any reason. If an offer needs your account to work, walk away and report it.
