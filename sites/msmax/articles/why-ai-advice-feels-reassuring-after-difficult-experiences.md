If you have ever felt judged by a salesperson, a bank clerk, or a receptionist and then turned to a chatbot instead, you are not alone. Research reported by Phys suggests that consumers who experience discrimination may prefer AI recommendations over human advice. This article explains why that happens, and gives you a simple routine for using AI advice without handing it your full trust.

## What the research describes

Phys reports on research built around an everyday scenario: you need to cook dinner on a 20-euro budget for a guest with a gluten intolerance. You could ask a shop assistant, or you could consult an AI-powered tool such as Hopla, which the report names as an example.

According to the coverage, consumers who have experienced discrimination may lean toward the AI option because it feels more neutral and less judgmental. The report describes embarrassment after unfair treatment as the trigger. It can turn "algorithm aversion" (the habit of distrusting automated advice) into "algorithm appreciation."

Note what this does and does not say. The finding is about how AI *feels* to people, not about whether AI advice is actually better or fairer.

## Why a chatbot can feel like a safe space

The reasoning, as summarized by Phys, is emotional rather than technical. After an unpleasant or unfair encounter, face-to-face advice carries a risk: you might be judged again. A chatbot removes the audience. Nobody sees your reaction, your budget, or your embarrassment.

That helps explain why AI assistants are showing up in shopping, banking, health care, and customer service. Brands pitch them as convenient. Some people may also use them to sidestep social judgment, which is a different reason entirely.

This is my analysis, not the researchers': if you have ever caught yourself preferring a self-service kiosk or an app over talking to a person, the pattern probably feels familiar.

## The catch: neutral-feeling is not neutral

Feeling unjudged is not the same as being treated fairly. An AI tool does not have a face or a tone of voice, so it can seem free of bias. But a tool can still produce skewed suggestions, and you may not notice, because nothing about it signals prejudice. The Phys piece frames the appearance of neutrality as something worth being cautious about.

So use AI for the comfort it offers, and keep your guard up for the part you cannot see.

## A five-step routine for using AI advice wisely

These steps work on any phone or computer and with any chatbot or shopping assistant. The exact app version does not matter much; the habits do.

### 1. Use AI for the low-stakes first draft

Ask for ideas, comparisons, or a starting plan. In the dinner example, you might type: "Dinner for two on 20 euros, one guest is gluten intolerant." Treat the answer as a draft, not a ruling.

### 2. Give it the details that matter

State your budget, dietary needs, and constraints plainly. Vague questions get generic answers. You do not need to share personal identifiers such as your full name, account numbers, or health records to get useful help.

### 3. Ask it to show its reasoning

Follow up with "Why did you recommend this?" or "What are two alternatives?" If an answer does not hold up when you probe it, that is useful information.

### 4. Check anything high-stakes with a person or an official source

For loans, medical questions, or legal matters, use AI to prepare your questions, then verify the answers with a qualified human or the provider's official documentation. For food allergies and intolerances in particular, always read the actual product labels.

### 5. Compare two sources

Ask a second tool, or search the same question independently. If the answers differ, dig into why. Disagreement is a signal to slow down.

## If you have been treated unfairly

Preferring AI after a bad experience is a reasonable coping move, but it does not have to be the only one. If an interaction with a business felt discriminatory, consider these options:

- Write down what happened, with date, place, and names, while it is fresh.
- Use the company's written complaint channel, which gives you a record that a chat window may not.
- Ask for a different staff member or a different branch if you still need the service.

AI can lower the barrier to getting help. It should not become a reason that unfair human behavior goes unreported.

## What businesses should take from this

The Phys report points to a broader consumer-trust issue as AI spreads into everyday services. If some customers choose a chatbot because they fear judgment, then companies offering AI have a responsibility to make sure it is fair, and to keep a human option available when the stakes are high. A chatbot that replaces accountability instead of supplementing it only moves the problem out of sight.

## The takeaway

Use AI when it makes you feel comfortable enough to ask the question, then verify the answer anyway. Comfort is a good reason to start a conversation with a chatbot; it is not proof that the advice is right.
