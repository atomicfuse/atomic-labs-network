If you have ever felt more comfortable asking a chatbot a question than asking a person, you are not alone, and new research helps explain why. You will leave this article knowing what that research found, why the comfort can be real, and a simple routine for using AI advice without handing it more trust than it has earned.

## What the research found

Phys reported on research examining how consumers choose between human advice and AI recommendations. Its example is an everyday one: you have 20 euros to spend on dinner and a guest with a gluten intolerance. You could ask a shop assistant, or you could consult an AI-powered tool such as Hopla, which Phys names in its description.

Per the report, consumers who have experienced discrimination may prefer the AI option. The reasoning is that AI feels more neutral and less judgmental than a person. The research also argues that embarrassment after unfair treatment can flip what is usually called "algorithm aversion" (the habit of distrusting automated advice) into "algorithm appreciation."

In plain terms: people may not pick AI because it is smarter. They may pick it because it feels safer.

## Why a chatbot can feel safer

The source describes the feeling as neutrality and a lack of judgment. Our read of that, which is analysis and not part of the research, is that a few ordinary things probably contribute:

- **No visible reaction.** A chatbot cannot sigh, glance at your clothes, or hesitate.
- **No audience.** You can ask a basic or personal question without anyone behind you in line.
- **No history to manage.** If a past interaction with a person left you embarrassed, a text box resets that pressure.

That comfort is legitimate. Choosing a lower-stress way to get information is a sensible choice, not a weakness.

## Where this shows up in daily life

The research points to a broader trend: AI assistants are moving into shopping, banking, health care, and customer service. Brands position them as convenient. The finding adds another possibility, which is that some customers use them to avoid social judgment altogether.

That matters if you are one of those customers, and it matters if you run a business offering these tools.

## The catch: neutral-feeling is not neutral

Feeling unbiased and being unbiased are different things. The research, as reported, is about how AI feels to consumers. It does not say AI advice is free of bias. Keep that gap in mind, because a tool you trust emotionally is a tool you may question less.

Here is a practical check you can run on any AI answer, on any phone or computer, in any chatbot app:

### Step 1: Ask for the reasoning

Add a line such as "Explain why you recommended this and what you assumed about me." Hidden assumptions often show up here.

### Step 2: Rephrase and compare

Ask the same question a second way, or in a fresh chat, and see whether the advice changes. Large swings are a signal to dig further.

### Step 3: Verify anything high-stakes

For money, medical, or legal decisions, treat the AI answer as a starting point. Confirm it with an official source or a qualified professional.

### Step 4: Keep personal details minimal

You can get useful advice without sharing your full name, account numbers, or health records. Check the app's privacy settings before you share more.

### Step 5: Know how to reach a person

Before you rely on a service's AI, find out how to escalate to a human. Look in the app's help menu or the company's contact page. If you cannot find one, that is worth knowing.

## If you offer AI to customers

The takeaway for businesses is not "replace the people." If some customers turn to AI because human interactions have felt unwelcoming, then pushing them back toward staff is not the fix, and neither is hiding the human option. A reasonable approach, in our view:

- Offer AI as a choice, not a gate.
- Make the path to a human easy and visible.
- Test the AI's recommendations for different groups of customers, so that a neutral tone does not mask uneven results.
- Train staff so the human option feels as safe as the automated one.

## Use the comfort, question the answer

If an AI assistant feels easier to talk to, use it; that comfort is a fair reason to choose it. Then make one habit stick: the next time a chatbot gives you advice that matters, ask it to explain its reasoning before you act.
