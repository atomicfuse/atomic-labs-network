Faye's final stretch in Netflix's *East of Eden* is not subtle about its cruelty, and Martha Plimpton isn't pretending otherwise. In a new interview with Vulture, the actress walks through playing the brothel madam whose trust and longing for family make her easy prey for Kate, Florence Pugh's villain in Zoe Kazan's adaptation of Steinbeck's novel. Here's the angle worth chewing on: Faye's death isn't just a shocking exit. It's the clearest picture the series gives us of what choice looks like, on both sides.

## The story: A Series Just Landed, and Faye Is Already the Talker

*East of Eden* has just premiered on Netflix, which has put Steinbeck's story back in the conversation. Kazan's adaptation arrives alongside plenty of chatter about prestige literary adaptations, complex female antagonists, and period dramas finding new ways to tell old stories. Pugh's Kate is the show's villain, and Plimpton's Faye is the person standing closest to her when that villainy fully shows itself.

According to Vulture, Plimpton built Faye as a warm, independent brothel madam. Her vulnerability comes from how much she wants family and how readily she extends trust. It's hard not to read that as the point: Faye isn't foolish, she's generous, and the story punishes her for it.

## A Madam With a Soft Center

Period dramas are not exactly short on brothel madams, but they tend to be sharp-elbowed, transactional types. Faye, as Plimpton describes her approach to Vulture, runs on warmth. That's a notable choice, and it's what makes the character's fate hurt rather than merely shock.

The description from Vulture frames Faye as independent, which matters. She isn't a damsel waiting to be rescued. She has her own business and her own authority. What she lacks is a family, and that yearning is the lever Kate pulls.

That's where the show's moral architecture gets interesting. Faye chooses kindness. Kate chooses cruelty. Steinbeck's novel is famously preoccupied with choice, and this adaptation, as Plimpton discusses it, stages that idea as a contrast between two women rather than an abstract theme.

## Why This Is Kate's Point of No Return

This next part is my read, not something Plimpton or Vulture says in those terms: Faye's death works as the moment Kate stops being a troubling presence and becomes a monster. Manipulation is one thing. Doing it to someone who has offered you shelter and warmth, then following through to a gruesome end, closes a door.

The cruelty lands harder because of how Plimpton plays Faye's trust. If the character were guarded or cynical, her end would read as a plot mechanism. Because she is open, it reads as a betrayal, and the audience watches it coming a beat before she does.

Pugh, meanwhile, gets the rare villain assignment where the worst thing the character does is to someone who was nice to her. That's more chilling than any grand scheme.

## What the Adaptation Is Doing With Steinbeck

Steinbeck's novel is a big, mythic story about inheritance, free will, and whether people can choose to be good. Kazan's version, per the framing around the series, reanimates that for a streaming audience. Faye is a smart place to see it at work. She's not a symbol; she's a person who makes decisions about how to treat others, and she keeps making them even when the evidence suggests she shouldn't.

That's the quiet thesis of her arc. Kindness here isn't naive weakness. It's a deliberate act, which is exactly why Kate's response to it is so damning.

## The Takeaway

It would be easy to remember Faye's final scenes only for how gruesome they are. Plimpton's account, as relayed by Vulture, points somewhere better: the horror lands because the character is so thoroughly alive. A warm, independent woman who simply wanted a family is dispatched by someone who looked at that warmth and saw an opening.

If *East of Eden* is asking whether people choose who they are, Faye's answer is on the screen the whole time. She chose generosity until the very end. The show lets Kate choose too, and that is the part you won't shake.
