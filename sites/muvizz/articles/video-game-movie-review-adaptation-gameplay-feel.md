The newest *Resident Evil* movie hit theaters Sept. 17, and according to a review from The Linfield Review, it does something adaptations of this franchise have long struggled with: it feels like sitting down with the games. The film follows a medical courier, and the review calls it a fast-paced, gory take on survival horror. The one real knock lands on the ending.

That's the angle worth chasing here. Plenty of game movies borrow the brand. The question is whether this one borrows the *experience*.

## A Franchise With Deep Roots (and a Lot of Sequels)

Per The Linfield Review, *Resident Evil* has anchored survival horror since the first game arrived in 1996. Since then, the franchise has racked up 31 video games, eight movies and even a stage play. That's a lot of mansions, a lot of mutations and, presumably, a lot of locked doors.

With that much history, a new film faces an obvious fork: serve the lore or serve the feeling. By the review's account, this one leans toward feeling.

## The Game Feel Is in the Details

The review points to several choices that nudge the movie toward gameplay rather than just backstory:

- **First-person camerawork.** Putting the audience behind the protagonist's eyes is the most direct way to mimic a controller-in-hand experience. It's hard not to see the logic: survival horror lives on limited visibility and the dread of not knowing what's around the corner, and a first-person view takes away the safety of the wide shot.
- **Inventory-style details.** The review highlights touches that echo the games' item-management feel. Anyone who has agonized over what to leave behind in a tiny inventory will recognize the impulse.
- **Jump scares.** The film leans on them, which suits a franchise that has trained players to flinch at a dog crashing through a window.
- **Franchise references.** The review notes nods to the series for longtime fans to spot.

The pace is brisk and the gore is, per the review, very much on the menu.

## Austin Abrams Holds the Controller

The review singles out Austin Abrams' performance as a highlight. In a movie built around subjective camerawork and constant threat, the lead has to sell panic and exhaustion without much room to coast, so it's a meaningful compliment. The film's courier premise also gives him an everyman footing, which fits a genre where the hero is usually just someone trying to get through the night.

## Why It Lands Now

Game-to-screen adaptations are under intense fan scrutiny, and audiences increasingly want to know whether a movie respects mechanics and lore or just rents the logo. That's the context this release enters. Going by The Linfield Review, the answer for the first two-thirds is a pretty confident yes.

## The Ending Problem

Here's where the review cools. The final stretch feels weaker than what came before, and the review specifically ties that to the film's handling of Bryan's transformation. I'll keep spoilers out, but the critique is a familiar one in horror: a movie can build dread beautifully and then lose steam when it has to deliver a big payoff.

It's also a funny echo of the games themselves. Plenty of *Resident Evil* titles are tense right up until the boss fight, where the mystery gets replaced with a giant thing to shoot. Whether that parallel is intentional is anyone's guess, and that's my own aside, not the reviewer's.

## Who Should Buy a Ticket

Based on the review's description, fans will get the most out of it, since the references and game-style touches are built for people who've spent hours in these worlds. Newcomers aren't shut out, though. A fast, gory, jump-scare-heavy horror movie with a strong lead doesn't require a lore degree, and the first-person style is accessible on its own terms.

## The Verdict

The new *Resident Evil* seems to understand that the franchise's real hook was never the plot. It was the feeling of being cornered with too few bullets and too little inventory space. By capturing that, it clears the bar many adaptations trip over. Stumbling at the finish line is a real flaw, but in this series, it's practically tradition.
