Zach Cregger's *Resident Evil* landed this week, and if reviewer Linda Cook's take is any indication, the director who quietly freaked everyone out with *Barbarian* has done something the franchise's entire previous film run couldn't quite manage: made a video game movie that plays just as well for people who have never touched a controller.

Cook, writing for AOL, calls it "the most fun you'll ever have at a movie inspired by a video game" — and given the crowded, often underwhelming history of that particular genre, that's not a small claim.

## The Non-Gamer Test, Passed

Here's the thing about video game adaptations: they have a habit of making the uninitiated feel like they showed up to a party where every inside joke went over their head. Easter eggs are fun if you've logged the hours; if you haven't, they're just confusing visual noise that reminds you this movie wasn't really made for you.

Cook's review addresses this directly and with some honesty — she notes she caught a couple of obvious nods to the games but had no way of knowing what Easter eggs she missed entirely. And here's why that actually matters: she still had a great time. The film's storytelling apparently doesn't lean on franchise literacy as a crutch. The Raccoon City setting and the Umbrella Corporation setup are established clearly enough on screen that newcomers can follow along without needing to have survived a single zombie outbreak on PlayStation first.

That's the bar that most game adaptations trip over. *Resident Evil* under Cregger, per Cook's account, clears it.

## Austin Abrams, Doing a Lot of the Heavy Lifting

Cook highlights Austin Abrams' performance as a core reason the film works on a human level. The everyman framing is intentional — Abrams plays the kind of character who functions as a surrogate for the audience member who walked in knowing nothing. When he's scared, you feel it. When the survival instincts kick in, you buy the escalation.

Abrams has been quietly building a compelling genre résumé, and a lead performance in a Cregger horror film is the kind of credit that tends to reframe how people look back at everything that came before it. If Cook's read is right, this could be the role that tips him into a different conversation entirely.

## What Cregger Brings to Raccoon City

The Cregger angle is where this gets genuinely interesting for horror fans. *Barbarian* announced him as a director with real instincts — the ability to weaponize genre expectation, to let comedy and dread occupy the same scene without one canceling out the other. That tonal balance is notoriously difficult to pull off, and it's also exactly what the *Resident Evil* property seems to demand.

The games themselves have always straddled camp and genuine horror. The original survival horror entries were terrifying; later entries leaned into action spectacle and, occasionally, memorable absurdity. A director who knows how to hold humor and fear in tension simultaneously is, in theory, a near-perfect fit for this franchise's DNA.

Cook's review describes the film as fast, funny, and gore-heavy — which sounds less like a compromise between those tones and more like Cregger found a way to run them in parallel. That's a harder trick than it sounds, and based on Cook's account, *Weapons* isn't the only reason his reputation is ascending right now.

## The Franchise Reinvention Problem

It's worth being clear about what Cook's review is and isn't saying. She notes upfront that this is "far from the original *Resident Evil* franchise" — meaning fans of the Paul W.S. Anderson films, or viewers with deep attachment to the games' specific lore, may find Cregger's version a deliberate departure rather than a continuation.

That's either a feature or a bug depending on what you walked in wanting. For anyone unburdened by prior attachment, it sounds like a straight-up win. For franchise diehards, the Easter egg hunt might be where the experience either clicks or frustrates — and Cook, by her own admission, wasn't positioned to fully audit that layer of the viewing experience.

There's an argument that a clean break is exactly what the property needed. The Anderson-era films had their devoted audience, but they never quite cracked the code of translating the games' atmosphere into cinema. Cregger reportedly isn't trying to pick up where those left off — he's building something with its own identity that happens to share a mythology.

## Why This Moment for Video Game Movies Feels Different

The timing here isn't accidental. Adaptations like *The Last of Us* on HBO and *The Super Mario Bros. Movie* at the box office have demonstrated that video game IP can hold mainstream audiences when the execution is right. The conversation has shifted from "can this work?" to "what does doing it well actually look like?"

Cregger's *Resident Evil*, based on Cook's review, appears to offer one answer to that question: make it for the room, not just for the fans. Give the mythology enough room to breathe that it functions as setting and atmosphere rather than prerequisite knowledge. Cast a lead who sells the fear, and trust that the horror is strong enough to carry viewers who didn't do the homework.

Cook's bottom line is that she had a great time, and she was watching from outside the fandom entirely. For a franchise that spent years being primarily a conversation among gamers and genre completists, that crossover accessibility might be the most significant thing about Cregger's version — more significant, even, than whatever Easter eggs she missed along the way.

For horror fans who've been waiting for someone to actually do right by Raccoon City on screen, the early word suggests the wait is over.
