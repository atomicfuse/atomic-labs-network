Everyone in Soldier Field expected a Hail Mary. What they got instead was a dump-off, a long run and a Patriots touchdown as the first-half clock hit zero. Rhamondre Stevenson's 48-yard score against the Bears on Thursday Night Football is a good lesson in how a routine end-of-half situation can fall apart. Here is what happened, and what it teaches you about watching these plays.

## What Happened

Per Fox News, Stevenson scored a 48-yard touchdown for the Patriots as time expired in the first half against Chicago. Fox reported that Stevenson had only a 4.3% chance of scoring after the catch. That figure is the headline of the story: by the numbers, this play was supposed to go nowhere.

The game was a primetime matchup in which the Bears were honoring Mike Ditka, so the stadium atmosphere was sentimental before the play turned it into a "how did that happen?" moment. New England also came in riding a three-game winning streak, and the score gave the Patriots a major swing heading into the locker room.

## Why a Dump-Off Beat the Expected Hail Mary

The interesting part is the contrast. When a team has the ball at the end of a half and is far from the end zone, the defense knows what is coming: a deep heave and a scramble in the end zone. Defenders usually line up to prevent that one thing.

A short throw to a running back runs against that script. Here is my read on why it can work, and this is analysis rather than anything Fox reported:

- **The defense is protecting depth.** Players deep in coverage are focused on the end zone, not on a back catching the ball underneath.
- **Urgency can fade.** When defenders believe the clock is about to expire, they may stop playing to the whistle, and a tackle that would normally be routine gets missed or never attempted.
- **Every yard after the catch is a bonus.** If the offense is only trying to get something, a ball carrier who keeps his feet can turn a harmless-looking play into a long one.

Fox's account doesn't spell out each defender's assignment, so it's fair to say we don't know exactly where the breakdown began. But the shape of the play, a catch near the line and a 48-yard score, tells you the Bears didn't tackle early.

## What the 4.3% Number Really Means

A 4.3% chance of scoring after the catch means that, on a typical play like this, the odds are overwhelmingly against the ball carrier. Roughly one time in 23 does it end in a touchdown. That is rare, not impossible, and it's why the play went viral.

When you see a statistic like that, remember what it measures: the probability from the moment of the catch. It doesn't say Stevenson was lucky. It says the situation looked bleak, and he got through anyway. Fox's coverage frames the play as a shocking momentum swing, and the odds explain why.

## Why the Timing Hurt Chicago So Much

Points scored as time expires in a half carry extra weight. The offense gets them without the defense having a chance to answer before the break, and the team that just gave them up walks off the field with nothing to fix in real time except frustration.

For a Bears team playing at home on a night built around honoring Ditka, the emotional whiplash is easy to imagine. That is my observation, not a reported detail. What Fox did report is that the moment turned a sentimental home game into a talking point for the wrong reason.

For New England, the play was a boost on a night when it was already carrying a three-game winning streak. Whether it ends up as the defining swing of the game depends on how the second half played out, which the coverage available here doesn't address.

## How to Watch End-of-Half Plays Differently

Next time a team has the ball with seconds left in a half, try these:

1. **Watch the defenders, not the quarterback.** Notice who is dropping deep and who is still playing underneath.
2. **Look for the check-down.** If the defense is sitting back, a short pass to a back or tight end is the offense's best alternative.
3. **Track the tacklers.** Missed tackles in these moments often decide whether a play is a 5-yard gain or a score.
4. **Count the clock.** A play can continue after time hits zero if it started before then, which is why a long run can still count.

## The Takeaway

The lesson from Stevenson's touchdown is simple: play every snap until the whistle. Defenses that assume the expected Hail Mary is the only threat can lose a half in a single dump-off. Next time the clock winds down, keep your eyes on the short route.
