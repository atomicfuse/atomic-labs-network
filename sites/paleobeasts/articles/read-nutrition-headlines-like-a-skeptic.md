Netflix's *The New Stanford Prison Experiment* has pushed the 1971 study back into conversation, and Phys recently revisited why it, and Milgram's obedience studies, still fascinate us. The piece describes major critiques of the studies' methods, ethics, and interpretations. Yet they endure, Phys argues, because of the disturbing question they pose: why do ordinary people choose harm when they could refuse?

This is a food site, so here is the practical angle. Your feed runs on the same machinery. A vivid story outlives the criticism of it. Whether it's a "caveman eating" claim, a miracle snack, or a rigid meal plan, you can borrow the skepticism psychology has had to learn. By the end, you'll have a five-step habit for sizing up any nutrition headline before it changes your grocery list.

## Why a Good Story Beats a Good Correction

According to Phys, the Stanford and Milgram studies stay culturally gripping despite the critiques, because the moral question underneath them is so unsettling. The takeaway people remember is the punchy one, not the messy footnotes.

Food claims work the same way. "Eat like our ancestors and get healthy" is a clean narrative with a hero, a villain (modern food), and a simple fix. A careful reading of the evidence is slower, hedged, and harder to share. That's my analysis, not a finding from the article, but it explains why the tidy version travels farther than the correction.

The lesson isn't that the studies are worthless. Phys frames them as still revealing something. The lesson is that a famous result and a settled result are different things.

## Step 1: Separate the Story From the Data

When you see a bold food claim, ask what was actually measured. A story says "this diet fixes your energy." Data says something narrower: who participated, for how long, and what changed.

Try this on the next headline you see:

- Write down the claim in one sentence.
- Write down what the study could realistically have measured.
- Notice the gap between the two. That gap is where hype lives.

The prison-experiment debate is largely a debate about this gap: what the setup actually showed versus the grand lesson drawn from it.

## Step 2: Check How the Study Was Run

Critiques of the Stanford and Milgram work, per Phys, target their methods. Methods matter in nutrition too. Before you trust a result, look for:

- **Sample size and who was in it.** A small group of one type of person tells you little about your household.
- **Self-reported eating.** Food diaries are famously hard to keep accurately. That's general knowledge, not something from the article, but it's a good reason for caution.
- **Duration.** A few weeks can't speak to a decade of eating.
- **Comparison group.** Compared to what? "Better than eating badly" is not the same as "better than a balanced alternative."

You don't need a statistics degree. Even two or three of these questions will filter out a lot of noise.

## Step 3: Ask Whose Interpretation You're Hearing

Phys notes that the critiques extend to how the studies were interpreted, not only how they were conducted. The same data can be spun in different directions.

In food media, interpretation often arrives packaged with a product, a cookbook, or a subscription meal plan. That doesn't make the advice wrong. It means you should ask who benefits from your believing it, and whether the claim would survive without the sales pitch attached.

## Step 4: Keep What Still Works

Here's where the psychology comparison is most useful. Phys doesn't conclude that these studies should be thrown out. It argues they keep their hold because they raise a real question. The same is true of a lot of food philosophies.

Take the Paleo Diet as an example of a framework worth sorting rather than swallowing whole or dismissing. You can take the practical parts that are easy to defend in everyday cooking:

- Build meals around whole, minimally processed ingredients.
- Fill half your plate with vegetables.
- Keep protein-rich snacks like nuts, hard-boiled eggs, or fruit with nut butter within reach.

Then treat the origin story, the idea that one ancestral way of eating was ideal for everyone, as a story rather than a proven rule. Eating more vegetables and less ultra-processed food doesn't depend on the myth being true. That's my take, and you should check your own needs, especially if you have a medical condition or dietary restriction, with a registered dietitian or doctor.

## Step 5: Watch for the Moral Hook

What made Stanford and Milgram stick wasn't statistics. It was the moral jolt: could I be the person who goes along with harm? Food claims have their own moral hooks, like guilt, purity, and fear. "Clean" versus "toxic." "Real" food versus everything else.

When a claim makes you feel ashamed or frightened, slow down. Strong emotion is what makes a story spread, and it's not evidence. A good meal plan should leave you with a shopping list and a workable week, not a sense of failure.

## A Quick Test You Can Run in Two Minutes

Next time a food headline lands in your feed, run through this:

1. What exactly was measured?
2. Who was studied, and for how long?
3. Who is interpreting the result, and what do they gain?
4. What part is still useful even if the big claim is overstated?
5. Is the message trying to make me afraid or ashamed?

If you can answer the first four and the fifth is a "no," you can feel fairly comfortable acting on it in small, reversible ways, such as trying a new recipe or swapping a snack.

## Why This Habit Pays Off

It's easy to turn debunking into its own pop story, where everything old is wrong and the new thing is right. The Phys piece avoids that trap by holding two ideas together: the methods and interpretations have been seriously criticized, and the studies still reveal something people want to understand. Treat food science the same way.

The next time a dramatic claim about eating makes the rounds, don't ask whether it's famous. Ask what it measured, then cook something good while you wait for better evidence.
