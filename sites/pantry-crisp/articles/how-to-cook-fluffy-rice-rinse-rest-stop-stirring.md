Rice is one of the cheapest, most dependable things in your pantry, and plenty of us still end up with a pot that's gummy in the middle or clumped together. According to a Fox News report that gathered advice from chefs, the problem is usually not the rice. It's a handful of small habits: skipping the rinse, treating every rice the same, stirring while it cooks, and fluffing too aggressively.

Here's how to fix each one, so the rice under your beans, tinned fish, or leftover vegetables actually turns out the way you want.

## Rinse first, especially basmati and jasmine

The step the chefs single out is rinsing. Per the report, rinsing basmati and jasmine before cooking is the key to fluffy, distinct grains that don't clump in the pot.

How to do it at home:

1. Put the dry rice in a bowl or fine-mesh strainer.
2. Cover with cold water and swish it around with your hand.
3. Drain, then repeat until the water looks clearer than when you started.

The cue is the water. Cloudy means keep going. Mostly clear means you're done. This takes about a minute and costs nothing.

## Don't use one method for every rice

The chefs in the report stress that rinsing, soaking, water ratios, and resting all depend on the variety of rice and the texture you want. That means a single "rice rule" will fail you sooner or later.

A practical way to use that advice:

- **Want separate, fluffy grains** (basmati, jasmine): rinse well. This is where the rinse matters most.
- **Want a softer, stickier result:** you may choose to rinse less, since the texture you're after is different.
- **Switching varieties:** read the package and treat the ratio as a starting point, then adjust next time based on what you got.

The report doesn't give exact water ratios or soaking times for each variety, so don't trust a universal number from memory. Use the ratio printed on your bag, and write down what worked.

## Leave the lid on and the spoon out

Stirring during cooking is another mistake the chefs flag, and it's one of the easiest to stop. Stirring can ruin the texture of the rice, per the report. Once the pot is covered and simmering, leave it alone.

It's tempting to peek or poke, particularly if you're worried about sticking. Resist that. Set a timer, keep the lid on, and trust the process.

## Let it rest before you touch it

Resting is listed among the steps that matter. When the timer goes off, take the pot off the heat and let it sit, covered, before you open it. The chefs' advice is that resting is part of cooking, not an optional extra. Since the report doesn't specify how long, use the timing on your package as your guide.

This is also a useful window on a weeknight. Use those minutes to warm a can of beans, flake tinned tuna, or toast some spices in a skillet.

## Fluff gently

The last mistake is fluffing too aggressively. After resting, use a fork or rice paddle and lift the grains instead of mashing or stirring them. Aggressive fluffing can break the grains and undo the work you did by rinsing and not stirring. Think of it as separating, not mixing.

## Quick fixes when rice goes wrong

The report's angle is prevention, so these are the habits to adjust next time rather than guaranteed rescues:

- **Gummy or clumpy rice:** rinse more thoroughly, skip the stirring, and fluff more gently.
- **Uneven or undercooked rice:** check your variety and its recommended water ratio, and make sure you let it rest covered instead of serving right away.
- **Rice that sticks together when you wanted it loose:** confirm you're using basmati or jasmine for that result, and rinse until the water runs clearer.

## A simple routine for tonight

Here's the whole method in doing order:

1. Rinse the rice in cold water until it looks clearer.
2. Cook using the ratio on your package for that variety.
3. Keep the lid on and don't stir.
4. Rest it covered off the heat.
5. Fluff gently with a fork.

None of it adds real time or cost, and it works with the bag already in your cupboard.

Next time you cook rice, change just one thing: rinse it until the water clears, and see how different the grains look in the bowl.
