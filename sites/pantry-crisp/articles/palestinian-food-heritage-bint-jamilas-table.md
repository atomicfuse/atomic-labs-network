Milwaukee food artist Muna Riyad Sharma grew up in Palestine and now honors that heritage through her tablescape and halal catering company, Bint Jamila's Table, per the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel (jsonline). The paper frames her cooking of Palestinian food as both cultural preservation and an act of resistance.

That framing is worth taking home to your own kitchen. You don't need a catering business to cook this way. You need a dish that carries meaning for you, and a simple method to make it well. Below is a short look at the idea behind her work, followed by a practical recipe built on a bread that shows up in many home kitchens: pita, turned into crisp homemade chips.

I can't speak to Sharma's menu or recipes, because the reporting I'm working from doesn't detail them. So the recipe here is mine, a pantry-friendly snack, not hers.

## What Sharma's work shows about food and identity

Per the Journal Sentinel's profile, Sharma works in two connected ways: a tablescape, meaning the deliberate styling of a table, and halal catering. Both treat a meal as more than something to eat. The table itself becomes part of the story.

Three takeaways for home cooks follow from that idea.

- **Dishes carry memory.** A recipe you learned from a relative is a record of where your family has been.
- **Presentation is part of hospitality.** Setting a table with care tells guests they were expected.
- **Cooking a heritage dish keeps it alive.** Every time someone makes it, it gets passed along.

You can apply all three on a Tuesday night with ingredients you already own.

## A pantry snack for a shared table: homemade pita chips

Pita chips are an easy way to practice the "set a table with care" idea. Put a bowl of them next to hummus, olives, or any dip, and a plain snack becomes a spread.

**Servings:** 4 as a snack
**Prep time:** 5 minutes
**Cook time:** 8 to 12 minutes

### Ingredients

- 3 pita rounds (whole wheat or white)
- 2 to 3 tablespoons olive oil
- 1/2 teaspoon salt
- Optional: 1 teaspoon dried thyme, sumac, or smoked paprika

### Steps

1. Heat the oven to 400°F (200°C).
2. Split each pita around the edge into two thin rounds. This is the step that makes the difference. Thin single layers dry out evenly and turn crisp, while thick double layers stay chewy in the middle.
3. Brush the rough side lightly with olive oil. Go light. Too much oil makes chips greasy instead of crunchy.
4. Sprinkle with salt and any seasoning.
5. Stack and cut into 6 to 8 wedges per round.
6. Spread the wedges in a single layer on a sheet pan. Crowding traps steam and softens them, so use two pans if needed.
7. Bake 8 to 12 minutes, flipping once halfway.

### How to tell they're done

The edges should be deep golden and the wedges should look dry and slightly curled. Pull one out and let it cool for a minute. A properly baked chip snaps cleanly. If it bends, return the pan to the oven for 2 more minutes. They keep crisping as they cool, so don't wait for dark brown in the oven.

### Substitutions and variations

- **Air fryer:** Cook in a single layer at 350°F (175°C) for 3 to 5 minutes, shaking halfway. Work in batches, and watch closely because small chips brown fast.
- **No olive oil:** Any neutral oil works. Cooking spray gives a lighter coat.
- **Stale pita:** Day-old or slightly dry pita is ideal. It's a good use for the bread at the end of the bag.
- **Spice blends:** Za'atar is a natural fit if you have it. Otherwise, garlic powder and black pepper do the job.
- **Storage:** Cool completely, then keep in an airtight container for up to 3 days. Re-crisp in a warm oven for a few minutes if they soften.

## Make the table part of the recipe

Take Sharma's emphasis on the tablescape as a cue. Serve the chips in a real bowl instead of the bag. Put out a dip you like, and a small dish of something bright, like olives or sliced cucumbers. Pour something to drink and invite someone over. None of this costs much, and it turns a snack into hospitality.

## Cook the dish that means something to you

Sharma's work, as the Journal Sentinel describes it, rests on a simple belief: what you cook and how you serve it can honor where you come from. Pick one dish tied to your own family or heritage this week, make it carefully, and put it on a table with a little thought. If you want an easy start, bake the pita chips and share them.
