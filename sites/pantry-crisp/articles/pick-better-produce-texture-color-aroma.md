Good dinners often get decided before you turn on the stove. Los Angeles-based cookbook author Carmen Perr, who wrote "Cook What Grows," says better home cooking starts at the market, according to Fox News. Her advice is to choose fruits, vegetables and herbs by paying attention to texture, color and aroma.

That's useful for a pantry-first kitchen like ours. Your canned beans, rice, pasta and lentils are the steady base. A few well-chosen fresh items are what make them taste like something you're excited to eat. Here is how to put the three senses to work, and how to turn what you buy into meals.

## Why the market matters more than the recipe

Perr's point, as Fox News reports it, is that quality ingredients do much of the work for you. A mediocre tomato can't be rescued by a great recipe, but a good one needs almost nothing. This matters even more when you cook from the pantry, because simple meals have fewer places to hide.

It also means you don't have to rely only on labels or a recipe's instructions. Your eyes, hands and nose are free, and they get better with practice.

## Use three senses, in this order

Fox News summarizes Perr's approach as texture, color and aroma. The details below are general shopping habits that fit those three cues, not direct quotes from her.

### Texture: pick it up

Hold the produce. Fruit and vegetables should feel like what they are: firm where they should be firm, with a little give where they should be tender. Limp, wrinkled or mushy spots are signs the item is past its best. Heft is also worth checking. Between two similar items, the heavier one is often juicier.

### Color: look for life

Look for color that is even and vivid rather than dull or patchy. Leafy greens and herbs should look perky, not slimy or yellowing. Color tells you about ripeness and freshness, but check it against texture, since a pretty surface can hide a soft spot.

### Aroma: smell it

Fragrance is a good signal for herbs and many fruits. Fresh basil, cilantro or mint should smell bright and clearly like themselves. If you smell nothing, the flavor may be faint too. A sour or musty smell means leave it behind.

## Turn a good haul into pantry dinners

Once you've picked well, keep the cooking simple. These pairings use the staples we lean on here.

### Herb-topped white bean pasta

Stir a can of drained white beans into hot pasta with a splash of the pasta water, olive oil and garlic. Finish with a handful of torn fresh herbs. This is where aroma shopping pays off, because the herbs are the last thing in and the most noticeable.

- **Serves:** 3 to 4
- **Time:** 10 minutes prep, 15 minutes cook
- **Swaps:** Any short pasta works. Use parsley, basil or chives, whichever smells best at the market.
- **Doneness cue:** The sauce should coat the pasta lightly and look creamy, not watery. Mash a few beans against the pan to thicken it.

### Tuna rice bowl with crisp vegetables

Top warm rice with tinned tuna, sliced cucumber or radish, and a squeeze of lemon. Firm, crunchy vegetables are the whole point here, so texture is your buying guide.

- **Serves:** 2
- **Time:** 15 minutes, using cooked rice
- **Swaps:** Use tinned salmon or sardines, and any crisp vegetable you found.
- **Doneness cue:** Nothing to cook but the rice. Serve it when the rice is hot and the vegetables are still cold and snappy.

### Sheet-pan potatoes and black beans

Cut potatoes into even pieces, toss with oil and salt, and roast at 425°F (220°C). Add drained black beans for the last 10 minutes and serve in tortillas.

- **Serves:** 4
- **Time:** 10 minutes prep, about 35 minutes roast
- **The step that makes the difference:** Pat the potatoes dry and give them space on the pan. Crowded or damp pieces steam instead of crisp.
- **Doneness cue:** Deep golden edges and a fork that slides in easily.

## A short checklist for your next trip

- Touch before you toss it in the bag. Skip anything mushy, wrinkled or limp.
- Compare two of the same item and take the one that feels heavier and looks livelier.
- Smell your herbs. If they have no fragrance, pick another bunch.
- Buy fewer fresh items than you think you need, and plan a pantry meal around each one so nothing wilts unused.

That last habit also supports the low-waste cooking Fox News links to Perr's approach: fresh produce you chose carefully and actually use beats a full crisper drawer you throw out.

## Make the first sniff and squeeze count

Next time you shop, pick one item and judge it with all three senses before it goes in the cart. Then build dinner around it with a can of beans, a pot of rice or a box of pasta. You'll taste the difference before you've opened a recipe.
