Fox News recently looked back at six vintage Thanksgiving dishes that once anchored American holiday tables: creamed pearl onions, oyster stuffing (or dressing), mincemeat pie, aspic, Cranberry Surprise, and hot Dr Pepper. Per the report, one chef says these dishes are fading away, and the list shows how much holiday cooking leaned on pantry staples, gelatin molds, oysters, and even heated soda.

If you want one of them on your table this year, you don't need a special shopping trip. Below is a quick read on which dishes fit a pantry cook, followed by a full recipe for the easiest one to rescue: creamed pearl onions with a crisp breadcrumb topping.

## The six dishes, sorted by how doable they are

Fox News names the dishes but, in the material I worked from, doesn't walk through how to make each one. So the sorting below is my take, not the report's.

- **Creamed pearl onions:** the most approachable. Onions, milk, butter, flour. Most of it is already in your kitchen.
- **Oyster stuffing or dressing:** a regular stuffing with an unusual add-in. If you like stuffing and like oysters, it's a small leap.
- **Mincemeat pie:** a bigger project, and the one most likely to divide the table.
- **Aspic and Cranberry Surprise:** the gelatin-era dishes. They fit the report's point about midcentury tables, and they take planning and a chilling window.
- **Hot Dr Pepper:** the easy one in effort, the hardest one to sell to guests.

That's why I'd start with the onions. Low risk, low cost, and the dish has a built-in upgrade.

## Crispy-Topped Creamed Pearl Onions

**Serves:** 6 as a side  
**Prep:** 10 minutes  
**Cook:** 25 minutes

This is my own modern take, not a recipe from the Fox News piece. Creamed onions are soft on soft, so the fix is a crunchy topping, the same idea as any good breadcrumb crust.

### What you need

- 2 bags (about 14 to 16 oz each) frozen pearl onions, thawed, or 2 jars drained
- 3 tablespoons butter
- 3 tablespoons flour
- 1 1/2 cups milk (any dairy milk works; warm it if you remember)
- 1/2 teaspoon salt, plus more to taste
- Black pepper
- A pinch of nutmeg (optional, but it tastes right here)

**For the topping:**

- 1 cup panko or plain dry breadcrumbs
- 2 tablespoons butter
- Pinch of salt

### Steps

1. **Dry the onions.** Spread thawed or drained onions on a kitchen towel and pat them dry. Wet onions thin out your sauce and leave it watery.
2. **Toast the crumbs first, separately.** Melt 2 tablespoons butter in a skillet over medium heat. Add the crumbs and a pinch of salt. Stir for 3 to 5 minutes until deep golden and fragrant. Move them to a plate. This is the step that makes the difference: crumbs toasted on their own stay crisp, while crumbs baked on top of a creamy sauce turn soggy.
3. **Make the sauce.** In a saucepan over medium heat, melt 3 tablespoons butter. Whisk in the flour and cook for 1 minute. Pour in the milk slowly, whisking as you go.
4. **Thicken.** Keep stirring for 4 to 6 minutes. It's done when the sauce coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through it leaves a clean line. Season with salt, pepper, and nutmeg.
5. **Add the onions.** Stir them in and simmer gently for 8 to 10 minutes, until the onions are tender when poked with a fork and heated through.
6. **Top and serve.** Spoon into a serving dish and scatter the toasted crumbs over the top right before it goes to the table.

### Substitutions

- **No pearl onions:** use small white or yellow onions, peeled and quartered, and simmer a few minutes longer until tender.
- **No fresh milk:** shelf-stable or evaporated milk works. If using evaporated, thin it with a splash of water.
- **No panko:** crush plain crackers or toast torn day-old bread in the skillet.
- **Make ahead:** make the sauce and onions a day ahead and rewarm gently. Keep the crumbs in a jar at room temperature and add them at the last minute.

## If you want to try oyster stuffing

I can't give you a tested ratio from the report, so here's only the practical logic. Oysters are a seafood add-in, which means you can treat them like any other mix-in to a stuffing you already know how to make. Start small, taste the mixture before baking, and let your guests see what's in it. If half the table is wary, bake a small separate dish rather than changing the whole pan.

## Why these dishes disappeared

Fox News frames these recipes as a window into how tastes, convenience foods, and family traditions have changed. That's a useful way to think about your own table. A dish usually fades because a more convenient or more familiar version replaces it, not because it was bad. Creamed onions are a good example: the ingredients are cheap and the method is simple, so the dish itself isn't what went out of style.

## A simple way to bring one back

Pick a single vintage dish, not all six. Put it next to the dishes everyone expects. Then ask the table which one they'd want next year. You'll learn more from one small experiment than from a menu overhaul.

This year, buy two bags of frozen pearl onions, toast a cup of breadcrumbs in butter, and find out whether your family has been missing creamed onions all along.
