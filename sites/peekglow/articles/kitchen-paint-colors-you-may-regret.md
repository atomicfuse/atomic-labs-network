Paint is one of the cheapest ways to change a kitchen, and one of the easiest to get wrong. Per House Beautiful, designers point to six colors that homeowners often regret: cool gray, black, bright yellow, stark white, red, and lime green. The common thread is shades that feel too cold, too stark, too saturated, or too hard to keep looking good.

Below, you'll find what makes each color tricky and a few ways to steer toward a kitchen that feels welcoming. The colors are House Beautiful's reporting. The practical swaps and testing tips are my own suggestions.

## Why kitchen color deserves extra thought

Your kitchen does double duty. It's where you cook, and it's where people end up standing around at every gathering. A color that looks sharp in a showroom photo still has to feel good at 7 a.m. and during a crowded dinner. House Beautiful's list suggests that harsh or high-maintenance shades are the biggest risk, especially as interiors lean toward warmer, softer looks.

## The six colors to think twice about

### 1. Cool gray

Gray has been a go-to neutral for years, but cool versions can read chilly, particularly in a room you want to feel inviting. If you like gray, try a warmer version with a hint of beige or taupe in it. Hold the sample next to your counters and flooring to see how the undertones behave together.

### 2. Black

Black looks dramatic, and designers caution that it can be a regret, according to House Beautiful. My take: dark finishes tend to show fingerprints, dust, and splashes, which matters in a room you touch constantly. If you love the moody look, consider limiting black to one piece, like a single island or a few accents, instead of every wall and cabinet.

### 3. Bright yellow

Yellow sounds cheerful, but a very bright shade can overwhelm a space where you spend lots of time. A softer, buttery tone gives you the sunny feeling without the glare. Try it on a small area first, such as a pantry door or the inside of open shelving.

### 4. Stark white

Crisp white seems like the safe choice, yet a stark one can feel cold and clinical. Designers flag it as a common regret. A white with a little warmth, like a soft cream or gentle off-white, still keeps things bright and clean while feeling more comfortable.

### 5. Red

Red is a strong, saturated color, and in a kitchen it can start to feel stressful over time. If you're drawn to it, bring it in through things that are easy to swap: a dish towel, a ceramic bowl, a set of stools. You get the energy without committing every wall to it.

### 6. Lime green

Lime is the boldest of the six and the hardest to live with day after day. If green appeals to you, a muted sage or deeper olive offers freshness with a calmer feel. Again, my suggestion rather than the designers' list, but it follows the same logic of dialing down the saturation.

## A simple pattern: dial down the intensity

Look at the list and one idea repeats. The trouble colors are either cold (cool gray, stark white), very dark (black), or very saturated (bright yellow, red, lime). When you're choosing, ask two questions:

- Does this feel warm when I picture standing in the room first thing in the morning?
- Will I be happy keeping it clean?

If either answer is a hesitant "maybe," soften the shade or shrink the area you plan to paint.

## How to test before you commit

These steps work for any color, not only the six above.

1. **Buy sample pots.** Paint a large patch, roughly a couple of feet wide, rather than a tiny swatch.
2. **Look at it at different times of day.** Morning light, afternoon light, and evening lamps can each change how a color reads.
3. **Place it near fixed elements.** Check it beside your counters, backsplash, flooring, and appliances, since those aren't changing.
4. **Live with it for a few days.** First impressions can fade quickly, in either direction.

## Add personality without the regret

If you still want a color with some punch, you have low-risk options. Paint a single accent, such as a door or a small nook. Use color in accessories, bar stools, or a runner. Choose a softer version of the shade you love. These keep your kitchen feeling like you while leaving an easy path to change your mind.

## Your next step

Before you open a can of paint, brush a big sample of your top choice onto the wall and leave it there for three days. If you still like it with your coffee in hand and the dishes in the sink, you've found your color.
