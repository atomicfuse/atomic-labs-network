If you've wondered why hunting keeps showing up in conversations about wellness, you're not alone. By the end of this piece, you'll understand what is drawing women to it, which claims deserve a skeptical eye, and how to bring some of the same slow, focused calm into your own week, no firearm required.

## The trend in numbers

Per reporting originally from the New York Post and picked up by WomenDevision, women now make up nearly 22% of U.S. hunters, more than double the share in 2016. Alongside that growth, organized all-female hunts have appeared, some blending meditation-style practices with time in the field. Organizations like Shoot Like a Girl are positioning hunting as a wellness and travel experience.

That framing is not universally comfortable. The original coverage asks a pointed question: is this a meditation hack, or are we simply putting a wellness label on killing? It's a fair question, and a thoughtful reader can hold both sides at once.

## What women say draws them in

The reporting points to a handful of motivations:

- **Mental health and mindfulness.** Sitting still outdoors, watching and listening, asks for the kind of attention many people struggle to give anything else.
- **Food sovereignty.** Some women want to know exactly where their meat comes from and take part in getting it.
- **Community.** All-female groups offer a space that has traditionally been male-dominated, with less pressure and more shared learning.

These are the stated reasons of the hunters profiled, not guaranteed outcomes. Hunting hasn't been shown here to treat anxiety, trauma, or burnout, and it's wise to treat any wellness claim, from any source, with a little caution.

## Where the wellness label gets tricky

Wellness travel is a business, and it markets experiences as self-care. That doesn't make the experience empty, but it's worth asking a few practical questions before you sign up for anything:

1. **What exactly is included?** Look for clear details on training, safety instruction, and licensing.
2. **What are the promises?** Be wary of language suggesting guaranteed calm, healing, or transformation.
3. **Does it fit your values?** Hunting involves killing an animal. If that sits right with you, great. If not, that's a perfectly good answer.

Hunting also comes with legal requirements and real safety considerations, so any first outing should go through proper, qualified instruction rather than a weekend impulse.

## The part anyone can borrow: slow, focused attention

You don't need a rifle to take the useful idea here, which is deliberate time outdoors with your attention on one thing. Try a small version:

### A 20-minute still sit

Pick a bench, porch, or patch of park. Leave your phone in your bag. For 20 minutes, simply notice: three sounds, two movements, one smell. When your mind wanders, bring it back.

### A weekly walk with a purpose

Choose one thing to look for each week, such as birds, seed pods, or the way light hits a certain tree. A small task keeps your attention anchored.

### Know where your food comes from

If food sourcing is what interests you, a farmers' market, a community garden plot, or asking a butcher about origin can scratch some of the same itch.

### Find your people

Part of the appeal is shared, low-pressure learning. A beginner birdwatching club, a hiking group, or a class at a local outdoor shop can offer similar community.

## So, meditation or marketing?

Probably a bit of both. The women quoted in the coverage describe real motivations, and the rise in participation is measurable. The wellness packaging is also a sales pitch. You can respect the first while staying clear-eyed about the second.

The takeaway: whatever draws you outdoors, choose the version that makes you slow down and pay attention, and be honest about what a given experience can actually deliver. Start with a 20-minute sit this week and see how it feels.
