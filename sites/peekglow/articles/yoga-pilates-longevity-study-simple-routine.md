Anti-aging advice is everywhere, and most of it comes with a price tag. A new look at the data suggests the habits worth your time may be the plainest ones: a yoga mat and a regular class.

Researchers at Arizona State University, including Connor Sheehan, studied more than 55,000 U.S. adults over a decade. Among complementary alternative medicine practices, yoga and Pilates were the only ones significantly associated with longer lifespans, per ASU's report. Yoga was linked to 27% lower mortality risk, and Pilates to 36% lower risk.

Here's what that means for your week, and how to turn it into a routine you'll keep.

## What the study found

The headline is about what *didn't* show up. According to ASU, other popular practices, including acupuncture, massage, and homeopathy, showed no meaningful association with longevity in the same data.

Sheehan's starting point, per the report, is that not every wellness practice promoted as a path to better health and longevity is backed by scientific evidence. Americans spend billions each year on wellness and anti-aging, and population-level data on which practices track with longer lives is rare. This study is a useful piece of that picture.

## What it can't tell you

This is an association, not a promise. The study doesn't show that yoga or Pilates *causes* a longer life. It's reasonable to wonder whether people who already tend to be healthier are simply more likely to show up to a class. That question is worth keeping in mind before you treat any percentage as a guarantee.

So treat these numbers as encouragement, not a prescription. Nothing here says a mat will add years to your life. It says the movement-based habits looked better than the exotic ones.

## Why movement practices may stand out

The study, as summarized, doesn't prove why yoga and Pilates came out ahead, so what follows is our own read. Both involve regular physical effort, and both are often practiced with other people. Compare that with a treatment you receive passively, and the difference is easy to spot: you do the work, and you tend to go back.

That's a hopeful takeaway for a lifestyle routine. The things that seem to matter are ordinary: moving your body, showing up consistently, and maybe doing it alongside others.

## How to start a simple routine

You don't need gear, a studio membership, or a big block of time. Try this:

### Pick one practice

Choose yoga or Pilates, whichever sounds more appealing. The one you'll actually do beats the one that sounds more impressive.

### Start with a small, fixed slot

Plan two sessions a week at the same times, such as Tuesday evening and Saturday morning. Putting it on the calendar makes it a habit instead of an intention. (The study doesn't specify how much practice is linked to the lower risk, so there's no magic number to chase.)

### Make your space easy to use

Leave a mat rolled in a visible corner of the room. A small cozy setup, like a soft rug, a lamp you like, and a blanket nearby for the cool-down, makes the space feel inviting instead of like a chore.

### Consider a class

Sheehan, per ASU, admits he doesn't follow his own findings: he does yoga alone with YouTube videos rather than in group classes. If that sounds like you, you're in good company. If you want to give the social side a try, a beginner class once a week is a gentle way to test it. Keep the online sessions for the days in between.

## Where to spend less

If your budget is tight, this study is a permission slip. Acupuncture, massage, and homeopathy showed no meaningful link to longevity here. That doesn't mean massage can't feel lovely or help you relax. Enjoy it as a treat, just not as a longevity plan. Meanwhile, a free video, a library DVD, or a community class costs far less than most wellness trends.

## The takeaway

Unroll a mat twice this week, on days you pick right now, and treat it as the plain, affordable habit it is. If the research holds up, it may be one of the better small investments you can make.

And if you have a health condition or haven't exercised in a while, check with your doctor before starting.
