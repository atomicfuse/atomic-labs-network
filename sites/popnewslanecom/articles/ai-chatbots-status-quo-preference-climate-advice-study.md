Ask a chatbot what to do about a big decision, and it may lean toward whatever already exists. That's the takeaway from a new University of Waterloo study, reported by Phys, which found that large language models (LLMs) show a status quo bias in climate-related advice. According to the report, the chatbots endorsed existing policies about twice as often as new proposals.

The researchers tested multiple LLMs across nearly 55,000 prompts. Per the report, the chatbots often recommended slower change than climate action requires, especially when the questions involved policy and consumer choices like electric vehicles.

## What the study actually found

Here's the reported picture, and I'm sticking to what the coverage supports:

- The study looked at multiple large language models, not just one.
- It ran nearly 55,000 prompts.
- When users asked for advice, the chatbots were more likely to make suggestions favoring the status quo over alternatives.
- Existing climate policies were endorsed roughly twice as often as new proposals.
- The advice often pointed to slower change than climate action requires.
- The pattern showed up in policy questions and consumer decisions, with EVs named as an example.

The summary I have doesn't spell out which specific chatbots were tested or how each one scored, so I won't guess at that. What it does make clear is the shape of the tendency: when in doubt, the bots lean toward what's already there.

## Why this matters beyond the lab

People increasingly use AI tools for shopping, policy research and everyday decision-making. That's the context here. If a chatbot's default is to nudge toward familiar options, that nudge could quietly reinforce high-emissions habits, since the advice reads as calm and reasonable rather than as a pitch.

This is my read, not the study's: a bias like this is sneaky precisely because it doesn't feel like bias. A chatbot recommending the thing you already have sounds like common sense. Nobody gets suspicious of the answer that says "maybe don't change much."

There's a pop-culture shorthand for it. Think of the friend who responds to every big idea with "well, the old way works fine." Helpful sometimes. Not who you'd want as your only advisor.

## How to pressure-test your chatbot

The study is about how chatbots behave by default, so the practical move is to stop accepting the first answer as the final one. These are my suggestions for doing that, not methods tested in the research.

### 1. Ask for both sides explicitly

Instead of "Should I buy an EV?", try asking the chatbot to lay out the strongest case for switching and the strongest case for staying put, side by side. If one side is noticeably thinner, that's worth noticing.

### 2. Make it argue for change

Try a prompt like: "Make the best possible argument for the bolder option, then tell me what would have to be true for it to be the right call." Forcing the bot into the change-friendly seat can surface points it skipped the first time.

### 3. Ask what it's assuming

Follow up with: "What assumptions are baked into your recommendation, and is the status quo one of them?" Chatbots can be surprisingly good at examining their own answers when asked directly.

### 4. Rephrase and compare

Ask the same question two different ways, once framed around keeping things as they are and once around trying something new. If the answers diverge a lot, you've learned something about how much the framing is steering the result.

### 5. Check the facts elsewhere

For anything that costs real money or affects real policy views, treat the chatbot as a starting point. Look up current local incentives, prices and official information before deciding.

## The bigger takeaway

It's easy to assume an AI is neutral because it has no stake in your choice. The Waterloo findings, as reported by Phys, suggest neutral-sounding and balanced aren't the same thing. A tool can have no agenda and still have a lean.

That doesn't mean you should ditch chatbots. It means using them the way you'd use any smart but cautious advisor: ask follow-ups, request the other side, and notice when the answer is suspiciously comfortable.

The best prompt to take away from this study might be the simplest one: "What would you tell me if you weren't defaulting to how things are now?" Worth a try next time a chatbot tells you everything's fine as it is.
