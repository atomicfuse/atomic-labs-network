A new study in PNAS, as reported by Phys, finds that major AI models can reproduce gender stereotypes when they write resumes and assess candidates. In other words, the bias can show up before an application is even submitted, and again once it lands in the evaluation pile.

If you've ever asked a chatbot to "punch up" your resume, this one is worth a look.

## The Problem Starts at the Blank Page

Per Phys, the scenario goes like this: a woman asks an AI system to help write her resume. The system chooses words, highlights qualities, and builds a profile. That's the helpful part. The study's concern is that the profile it builds can lean on gender stereotypes along the way.

This is the part that makes the story feel different from the usual "algorithms are biased" headline. We tend to picture bias as something that happens to your application after you send it. Here, the tool you recruited as your ally may be shaping how you come across from the very first draft.

It's hard not to see the irony. Plenty of people turn to AI precisely because they want a neutral, polished second opinion.

## "Fair on Average" Can Still Be Unfair

The most useful finding for anyone thinking about this stuff is about averages. According to the research, outcomes that look fair when you average across all jobs can hide penalties in specific professions that skew toward one gender. The study highlights women entering male-dominated fields as particularly exposed.

A simple way to picture it (this is my analogy, not the study's): imagine a school reporting a healthy overall grade average while one class quietly fails half its students. The headline number looks great. The people in that one class aren't comforted by it.

That matters because a company testing its AI tool with a broad, general check could come away reassured while the tool still disadvantages candidates in exactly the roles where stereotypes are strongest.

## Why the Timing Matters

Per the Phys report, job seekers and employers are increasingly using AI for resumes, screening, salary guidance, and HR decisions. When one tool touches that many steps, small biases don't stay small. They can compound into real barriers in the labor market, and the findings challenge the comfortable assumption that automation automatically makes hiring more objective.

The salary angle deserves its own mention. Because AI is also being used for salary guidance, a biased recommendation could shape what someone feels comfortable asking for. The study's coverage flags this as a risk, and it's easy to see why: an anchor set too low at the start can be hard to undo at the negotiating table.

## What Job Seekers Can Do

The study doesn't tell you to abandon AI tools, and neither will I. But a few sensible habits follow from what it describes:

- **Read the draft like an editor.** Look at which qualities the tool chose to emphasize. Do they match your actual strengths, or a generic idea of someone in your role?
- **Compare your drafts.** If you're curious, ask for the same resume in several styles and see what changes in tone or word choice.
- **Treat salary suggestions as one data point.** Cross-check with job listings, industry reports, and people you trust.
- **Keep your own wins front and center.** Specific numbers, projects, and results are harder for a template to flatten.

## What Companies Should Take From It

For employers, the "fair on average" finding is the headline. A prompt that simply says to be gender-neutral isn't the same as testing results. The study's coverage points toward audits that look at specific professions, especially ones dominated by one gender, rather than relying on a single overall score.

That's a more demanding standard, but it's also a more honest one. Checking only the average is a bit like a movie getting a great aggregate rating while one act of the film falls apart. The score says one thing; the viewing experience says another.

## The Takeaway

AI can still be a handy drafting partner. The lesson here is to treat it like a very fast intern who has absorbed a lot of the internet's assumptions: useful, quick, and in need of a careful read before anything goes out under your name. The tool may write the first draft, but you're the one who decides whether it sounds like you.
