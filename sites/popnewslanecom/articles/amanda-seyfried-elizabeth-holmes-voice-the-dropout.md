Elle's 2022 interview with Amanda Seyfried is back in circulation, republished around the release of the 2026 Holmes documentary *You Can See Everything*. It's a good excuse to revisit one of the most talked-about bits of TV acting in recent memory: that voice.

Per Elle, Seyfried approached Elizabeth Holmes's famously deep register less like an impression and more like an accent, something built from mouth shape, breath, projection, and a whole lot of repetition. She went on to win an Emmy for *The Dropout*, and the voice is a big part of why the performance stuck.

## An Accent, Not a Party Trick

Here's the distinction that makes the whole thing click. An impression is a greatest-hits reel: a few recognizable quirks, delivered on cue. An accent is a system. It changes where sound sits in your mouth, how you pace your breathing, and how you push air behind each word.

According to the Elle piece, that's the lens Seyfried used. The voice wasn't a gimmick layered over a performance. It was the foundation the performance stood on.

It's hard not to see why that matters on screen. A voice treated as a one-off bit gets tiring over a multi-episode series. A voice built like an accent can hold steady, slip, and recover, which is exactly what a character performing a persona needs to do.

## Repetition Is the Secret Ingredient

The unglamorous part of the process, per Elle, is the repetition. Mouth shape, breath, and projection are physical habits, and physical habits only become automatic through practice. That's the same reason a musician runs scales and an athlete drills the same movement over and over.

The payoff is that the voice stops feeling like effort. When an actor has to think about every syllable, the audience can sense it. When the mechanics are second nature, the performance gets to be about the character instead of the technique.

## The Voice Was Part of a Costume

The Elle piece also ties the voice to the rest of Holmes's public image: the black turtleneck uniform and the carefully controlled persona. Together, the article frames them as a calculated performance of power in a male-dominated Silicon Valley.

That framing is the interesting part. Voice, wardrobe, and presentation work like a package. Each one sends a signal about authority before a single business claim gets made. (Anyone who has watched a keynote and noticed the presenter's outfit has seen this principle in action.)

Once you notice that the voice is doing the same job as the turtleneck, the performance reads as layered. Seyfried isn't just playing Holmes. She's playing Holmes playing a role.
