Open shelves are the home-decor equivalent of a stage: everything on them is in the show, whether you meant it to be or not. Per a Washington Post piece on using open storage without adding to clutter, too many items or mismatched pieces on open shelves and racks can make a room look disorganized and messy. That's the problem. The fix, as this guide sees it, is to treat the shelf like a lineup rather than a landing zone.

A quick note on sourcing: the Post's full list of 12 tips isn't something I have in front of me, so I won't pretend to walk you through all twelve. What the reporting does support are the core themes: avoid overloading, avoid mismatched items, and use editing, grouping, color coordination and containers to make storage look intentional. Those four moves are the backbone below.

## Why open storage goes wrong so fast

Open storage is popular for a reason. It shows up in small-space living, rentals, kitchens and closets, and it's a staple of social-media-ready interiors. It's easy to see the appeal: no doors to install, everything within reach, and a pretty display when it works.

The catch is that it demands styling discipline. A closed cabinet forgives a lot. An open shelf forgives nothing. Per the Post, the trouble starts when shelves get packed too full or filled with items that don't relate to each other.

It's hard not to wonder how many of us bought the shelf first and decided what to put on it second. That order of operations is how a clean-looking rack becomes a visible junk drawer.

## Step one: edit before you arrange

The most useful idea in this whole topic is also the least glamorous: take things off. If overloading is what makes open storage look messy, then removing items is the first and cheapest upgrade.

A simple way to run the edit:

- Empty the shelf completely. Seeing everything on the floor or counter makes it obvious how much you were asking one rack to hold.
- Put back only what you use or genuinely like looking at.
- Relocate the rest to closed storage, or let it go.

This is my read, not a rule from the Post: a shelf with breathing room looks deliberate, while a full one looks accidental. Empty space is not wasted space; it's what makes the things you kept look chosen.

## Step two: group like with like

Mismatched items are the other culprit the Post flags. The antidote is grouping. Instead of scattering one mug here and one bowl there, cluster related things together so the eye reads them as a set.

Some easy groupings:

- Kitchen: everyday glasses together, bowls together, cooking tools in one spot.
- Closet: folded knits with knits, bags with bags.
- Living room: books in one stretch, decorative objects in another.

Grouping turns a pile of individual things into a few clear visual units. Three tidy clusters read as a design. Thirty loose objects read as a yard sale.

## Step three: pick a color story

Color coordination is the quickest way to make mismatched stuff look like it belongs together. You don't need a perfectly matching set of anything. Sticking to a limited palette, say whites and woods, or a couple of neutrals with one accent, can calm a shelf down fast.

This is also where a rental dweller can win. You can't repaint the walls or replace the cabinets, but you can control what sits on the shelves. A consistent palette makes an ordinary rack look styled without a single permanent change.

## Step four: let containers do the heavy lifting

Some things are never going to be beautiful: cords, packets, loose supplies, small odds and ends. That's where containers come in. Baskets, bins and boxes corral the messy bits into one tidy shape, so your eye sees a neat container rather than its contents.

A few practical tips, offered as my take:

- Choose containers in the same material or color family so they read as a set.
- Use them for the small stuff, and keep your nicer objects out in the open.
- Label or designate each one so the system survives past the first week.

Containers are the compromise between fully open and fully closed storage. You get the easy access of a shelf with some of the visual calm of a cabinet.

## The before-and-after test

The content-friendly version of all this is the makeover: photograph the shelf as it is, then run it through the edit, group, color and container steps and photograph it again. The difference is usually dramatic, and it's a handy way to check your own work. If the after photo still looks busy, you probably have more items than shelf.

That's the honest takeaway from the Post's topic. Open storage isn't inherently messy; it just has no place to hide. Edit hard, group sensibly, keep the colors in the same family, and give the small stuff a container. Do that, and your shelves stop looking like storage and start looking like a decision.
