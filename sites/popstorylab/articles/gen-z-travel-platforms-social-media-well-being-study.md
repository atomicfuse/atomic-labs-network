Your trip-planning app may be doing more than helping you find a hotel. According to a study reported by Phys, social media communication on online travel platforms can contribute to customer well-being when it encourages participation and positive emotional experiences. The research surveyed 210 Generation Z users in Greater Jakarta.

That's a small, specific sample, and it's worth keeping in view. But the finding lands on something anyone who has spiraled through a comment section before booking a flight already suspects: the feeling a platform gives you may matter as much as the clicks it collects.

## What the study found

Per Phys, the study concludes that social media communication on travel platforms can improve customer well-being when it drives three things: participation, value co-creation, and positive emotional experiences.

"Value co-creation" is academic-speak, but the idea is familiar. Users aren't just consuming a platform's content. They help make it, through reviews, comments, recommendations and shared experiences. Those are exactly the things Gen Z users reportedly rely on before they book.

That's the quiet shift here. Travel platforms increasingly function like social networks, and the research points attention away from engagement metrics like clicks and toward the emotional quality of what happens on them.

## The booking app as group chat

Here's my take: this is the logical end point of a long internet habit. Anyone who has ever fallen down a rabbit hole of "things I wish I'd known before visiting" posts knows that planning a trip is no longer a transaction. It's a hangout.

A review isn't just data. It's somebody saying, "I went, here's what happened, you'll be fine." A comment thread turns into a tiny community of strangers swapping tips. The study's framing suggests that when this kind of exchange feels good, users may come away better off, not merely better informed.

The study doesn't say that every scroll through a travel feed is a wellness practice, and I'm not claiming it does. What it suggests is narrower and more interesting: the emotional tone of the interaction is part of the product.

## Why clicks are a lousy measure of joy

The click has long been the currency of social media. It's easy to count and easy to sell. But a click can mean delight, curiosity, anxiety or the fourth consecutive hour of comparing resort photos at midnight.

The research, as described by Phys, nudges the conversation toward whether users feel informed, excited and connected. It's hard not to wonder what that means for platform design. A feed that makes someone feel overwhelmed or pressured to book might post great numbers and still leave people worse off. A feed that encourages sharing and positive emotion might do the opposite.

That's a useful lens for anyone who has watched travel influencer culture evolve. The dreamy itinerary and the "don't miss this" countdown can inspire people, or they can leave them feeling behind. The study's emphasis on positive emotional engagement points toward the first version.

## What travel brands could take from it

The takeaways below are my reading of the findings, not conclusions the researchers are quoted as making.

- **Treat users as co-authors.** If participation and co-creation are tied to well-being, then reviews and shared experiences aren't filler around the real content. They're central to it.
- **Aim for the emotion, not the tap.** Content that makes people feel excited and connected fits the study's framing better than content engineered for urgency.
- **Respect the planning phase.** Part of the fun of a trip is the anticipation. A platform that supports that, rather than rushing it, seems to be on the right side of this research.

## A grain of salt, politely

A survey of 210 people in one metropolitan region can't speak for every Gen Z traveler everywhere. Nothing in the reporting suggests it does. What it offers is a data-backed nudge on a question platforms tend to answer with dashboards: how does this actually feel to use?

The internet spent years optimizing for attention. For Gen Z, who treat travel apps like social spaces, the more interesting metric may be the one nobody can put on a chart: whether you close the app feeling excited about the trip, or just tired of scrolling past everyone else's.
