Vulture has published its guide to the best new and returning TV of 2026, and the six titles it singles out make an oddly coherent mix: a Netflix adaptation of *East of Eden*, a Northern Irish series called *Trespasses*, HBO's *Youth*, the third season of Australian sitcom *Colin From Accounts*, the second season of FX's *Adults*, and Netflix's *One Hundred Years of Solitude*.

The outlet's own description frames the highlights as "a coming-of-middle-age gem, a maturing Gen-Z sitcom, and a timeless intergenerational saga." That's the thread worth pulling. Read together, the list is less a random pile of recommendations than a map of life stages, and the stage getting the most attention is the one TV has long treated as a punchline: the messy middle.

## The Angle: TV Finally Looks at the Middle

Vulture's tagline for the list name-checks a "coming-of-middle-age gem." Pair that with a returning Gen-Z sitcom that has, in Vulture's words, matured, and a "timeless intergenerational saga," and you get something like a whole lifespan on one page.

It's hard not to read that as a signal. For years the default TV protagonist was either a twentysomething figuring it out or a prestige antihero in full crisis. Characters in the sandwich zone, the ones with a foot in each generation, were usually supporting players. A critic's list leaning into midlife isn't proof of an industry-wide shift, but it suggests where the conversation is heading.

### Colin From Accounts: Sitcom Longevity, Down Under

*Colin From Accounts* landing on the list with a third season is its own small story. Sitcoms that survive to season three are the ones that figured out their engine early. Per the angles flagged around the list, the show is connected to Sharon Horgan's return to autofictional comedy, which is a phrase that tells you plenty about the register: comedy drawn from lived-in, slightly-too-real territory rather than high-concept setups.

### Adults: The Gen-Z Sitcom Grows Up

Then there's *Adults* on FX, back for season two. Vulture's description of it as a "maturing Gen-Z sitcom" is a neat bit of framing. The title alone is a joke about the gap between being legally one thing and functionally another, and a second season is where a show like that has to prove it can grow past its premise. The angle here is how it pushes sitcom conventions into more absurdist and vulnerable territory, which is the sort of tonal stretch comedies attempt when the pilot-year energy runs out.

What's fun about the pairing with *Colin From Accounts* is the contrast. One comedy tracks people who are just starting to feel the weight of responsibility; the other, per Vulture's framing, leans into the grown-up version of that anxiety. Watch them back to back and you get a very efficient before-and-after.

## The Literary Heavyweights

Two Netflix titles anchor the list's more classical end, and both are adaptations of famously enormous books.

### East of Eden

Steinbeck's novel is the kind of source material that tempts adapters to go epic and reverent. The angle surfaced around the list points to Zoe Kazan and how she reframes the classic through fresh character perspectives. I can't tell you from the available material exactly what choices she makes, so I'll stick to the interesting part: the pitch is that the story gets a new vantage point rather than a museum-quality re-creation. That's the right instinct for a book this familiar.

### One Hundred Years of Solitude

This is the other big swing. The "timeless intergenerational saga" in Vulture's blurb is presumably this one, and the challenge is built into the title: balancing magical realism with sweeping historical scope across generations. It's a book that has long carried a reputation for being hard to put on screen, which makes its presence on a best-of list notable on its own. Whether or not it fully conquers that reputation, a critic's early nod signals it's being taken seriously.

*Trespasses*, the Northern Irish series, is where the list gets its performer spotlight. The angle attached to it highlights Lola Petticrew's range, a good reminder that some of the most interesting TV acting turns happen in shows that never get billboard-sized marketing.

## Why This List Matters Now

Vulture's framing is about navigation: audiences are facing a crowded field, and critics' early picks help signal which shows might dominate cultural conversation and awards season. That's a fair read, with one caveat. Early picks are early picks, and the history of TV is full of buzzy titles that faded and quiet ones that took over the group chat three months later.

But the shape of this list is the real takeaway. Adaptations of enormous books, sitcoms with staying power, a young-adult comedy trying to age gracefully, and a midlife story getting top billing: that's a spread covering nearly every stage of life.

The quiet punchline? The most binge-worthy genre of 2026 might simply be "people at every age, still figuring it out."
