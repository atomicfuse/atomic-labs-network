If you're thinking of tacking New Zealand onto an Australia trip, the authors of the newly published Frommer's New Zealand have a better idea: give it its own trip. Pauline Frommer sat down with the guidebook's authors, Naomi Arnold and Kate Evans, for a conversation about all things Kiwi, reported by Yahoo. Here is what that advice means for how you plan, how you move around, and how you engage with Māori culture, including at the table.

I can only pass along what that conversation covered, so this is a planning guide built on the authors' themes rather than a restaurant list. Where food comes in, I'll point you toward the questions worth asking.

## Don't Treat New Zealand as a Side Trip

The core message, per the coverage, is that New Zealand differs from Australia and deserves a trip of its own rather than a rushed add-on. The two countries are neighbors, and that's exactly why travelers lump them together.

The practical consequence is simple: if you only have a few days to spare at the end of an Australian itinerary, you'll end up skimming. Choose one region of New Zealand, or save it for a dedicated trip, rather than trying to see everything in a hurry.

## Plan for a Compact Country That Drives Slowly

The authors describe a country that is compact on the map but slow to drive. Treat those two facts together. Distances look short, but the landscapes make getting between places take longer than you'd expect.

Three ways to plan around that:

- **Pick fewer bases.** Staying put for several nights beats packing up every morning.
- **Count driving as part of the day.** If a leg takes real time, build your meals and stops around it instead of treating it as dead time.
- **Choose an island first.** Deciding between the North and South Island highlights, rather than doing both quickly, is one of the easiest ways to avoid overpacking your itinerary.

## Choose by Travel Style, Not by Checklist

One of the useful questions raised in the discussion is which island fits the way you like to travel. Rather than copying a greatest-hits route, ask yourself what you want from the trip: nature, culture, or time to linger. Let that answer pick your island and your pace.

If you're drawn by food and place, a slower plan serves you better. Regional dishes and local ingredients reward time spent in one area, where you can return to the same market or café and start recognizing what's local and what's seasonal.

## Go Beyond the Hāngī for Māori Culture

The authors highlight Māori tourism as a reason to make the trip meaningful, and they point to modern experiences that go beyond traditional performances. The hāngī, the traditional Māori method of cooking food in an earth oven, is the best-known culinary touchpoint for visitors. It's worth seeking out, but the authors' emphasis is on a broader, more current picture of Māori culture.

How to approach it respectfully:

1. **Look for Māori-led experiences.** Choose offerings run by Māori people so the stories and the benefit stay with the community.
2. **Look past the show.** Seek out experiences that reflect how Māori culture lives and evolves today, not only as a staged performance.
3. **Treat food as culture.** When a meal is part of an experience, ask about where the ingredients come from and the traditions behind how it's prepared.
4. **Be curious and creative.** The guidebook authors frame engagement as something that can be inventive, so don't limit yourself to the first option you see advertised.

## Build Your Trip Around Places, Not Pins

Nature-rich travel with Indigenous cultural depth is what draws many people to New Zealand now, and that kind of trip is built differently from a sightseeing sprint. Instead of collecting famous stops, anchor each stretch of the journey in one place, learn what it's known for, and eat accordingly.

A simple template:

- Choose one island and a small number of bases.
- Add one Māori-led cultural experience in each area you stay.
- Leave open afternoons for local markets, cafés, and whatever dish people there tell you to try.

## The Takeaway

Give New Zealand its own trip, pick one island, and book at least one Māori-led experience that goes beyond the hāngī. Then slow down: the country is small, but it rewards the traveler who stays.
