WWE legend "Stone Cold" Steve Austin is skipping the big-bird routine this Thanksgiving. Per Fox News, Austin plans a low-key holiday with his wife, and the menu may feature South Texas tamales instead of turkey. He also called turkey "overrated" and spoke up for canned cranberry sauce. His wife is vegetarian, according to the report, and she is planning the menu.

You don't need a wrestling résumé to borrow the idea. Here is how to think about a tamale-centered Thanksgiving, what the swap does for your table, and how to keep it from feeling like a compromise.

## Why tamales make sense on a holiday table

The reporting doesn't go into Austin's recipes, so what follows is general food logic, not his plan. Tamales fit the holiday for practical reasons: they are individually wrapped, they hold well when kept warm, and they suit a crowd where not everyone eats the same way. A mixed table of meat eaters and vegetarians is exactly the situation Austin's household reportedly faces.

That is the real lesson of the story. Thanksgiving doesn't have to be organized around one centerpiece protein. It can be organized around a dish with roots in where you live or where you're from.

## Think regionally: what "South Texas tamales" signals

Austin's source material points to South Texas, and that's the hook for a food-and-place site. Tamales are a regional tradition with their own rhythms, and in many Texas households they are a gathering food, often made in batches with several hands involved. If you want your table to feel rooted somewhere, buy or make them with a place in mind rather than grabbing a generic frozen box.

Practical ways to do that:

- **Buy local.** Look for a neighborhood tamale maker, a market stall, or a family-run shop. Order ahead; holiday demand is heavy and many small sellers cap their orders.
- **Ask what's inside.** Fillings vary by cook. If you have a vegetarian guest, ask specifically about the fat in the masa, since some makers use lard, and about fillings like cheese, beans, or vegetables.
- **Reheat gently.** Steam them instead of microwaving at full power, so the masa stays tender instead of turning dense.

## Build the rest of the plate

A tamale-led Thanksgiving still wants sides. Keep them simple so the tamales stay the star.

### Something bright
Salsa, pickled onions, or a crisp slaw cut through the richness of masa.

### Something beans or rice-based
These round out the plate and are easy to make vegetarian.

### Something sweet and tangy
Here you can follow Austin's lead on one point: he reportedly praised canned cranberry sauce. If you grew up with the ridged cylinder sliding out onto a plate, nobody at your table needs to apologize for it. Serve it as is, or stir in a little orange zest if you want to dress it up.

## Is turkey really overrated?
That's Austin's take, and he's entitled to it. My own view: turkey isn't the problem, the pressure is. A whole bird demands hours, a big oven, and precision, and it often lands as the least exciting thing on the table. If it's the dish your family loves, keep it. If it's there out of habit, Austin's approach suggests a useful test: ask each person what they actually look forward to eating, and build from those answers.

## How to run your own anti-traditional Thanksgiving

1. **Poll the table.** Find out which two or three dishes people truly want.
2. **Pick one regional anchor.** Tamales, in Austin's case, but it could be any dish tied to your family or your region.
3. **Cover dietary needs up front.** With a vegetarian in the house, plan meat-free options as full dishes, not afterthoughts.
4. **Keep one nostalgic item.** Canned cranberry sauce, if that's yours, gives the meal continuity.
5. **Order early.** If you're buying tamales, place the order days ahead.

## The takeaway

Thanksgiving is a meal about gathering, and the menu is yours to set. This week, ask the people at your table what they would genuinely miss if it vanished, then find a local maker for one dish with real regional roots and put it in the middle.
