Thanksgiving is days away, and you may not have the time, the budget, or the oven space for a from-scratch spread. Fox News reports that several big grocery chains are selling Thanksgiving meal kits, ingredient bundles, or heat-and-serve dinners for under $70. ## First decision: ingredients or heat-and-serve?

This choice shapes your whole day, so make it before you compare prices.

### Ingredient bundles
You get the components and do the cooking. This suits you if you like the process, own the pans, and have oven time to spare. It's usually the route that feels most like a traditional Thanksgiving, since the house smells like dinner.

### Heat-and-serve dinners
These cut prep time and hosting stress, which is the main selling point in the Fox News roundup. This suits first-time hosts, small apartments with one oven, and anyone who wants to spend the afternoon with guests instead of the stove.

If you're unsure, ask yourself one question: what is the scarcest resource this week, money, time, or oven space? Pick the format that protects it.

## Do the cost-per-person math yourself

A low headline price only helps if the kit feeds your table. Before you commit, check the serving count on the package or store page, then divide.

A quick example using the report's ceiling: a $70 kit that serves 10 works out to $7 per person. The same $70 kit that serves 4 is $17.50 per person. Same price, very different deal. (These numbers are an illustration of the math, not figures from any specific store's kit.)

Compare these three things for each option:

- **Stated servings.** Match it to your actual guest count, and add a person or two if you want leftovers.
- **What's included.** Confirm whether the turkey, sides, and dessert are all in the price, or whether you'd need to add items.
- **Prep required.** Check whether it's fully cooked, partly cooked, or raw ingredients.

## Small gatherings versus big tables

A kit built for a handful of people can be a strong value for a small table, where buying a whole turkey and every side separately would leave you with waste. For a larger crowd, run the cost-per-person math, and consider whether one kit plus a few extras beats buying two kits. Your own analysis here beats any ranking, because the best deal depends on your headcount.

## Shortcuts that save money without feeling cheap

A store-bought base doesn't have to taste store-bought. These are practical moves, not claims about any specific kit:

- **Add one homemade dish.** A single scratch item, such as a side or dessert you're proud of, makes the whole meal feel personal.
- **Own the leftovers.** Plan sandwiches or a next-day plate so nothing goes to waste, which stretches the value of the kit.
- **Skip duplicates.** If the kit includes a dessert or rolls, don't also buy them. Check your cart against the contents list.
- **Warm things in staggered times.** With heat-and-serve food, plan oven and stovetop order on paper the night before so everything lands hot together.

## Why the competition helps you

Fox News frames this as a value story: food costs are still top of mind for shoppers, and big retailers are fighting for holiday business with low-price bundles. For you, that means it's worth checking more than one store. Look at the sites or apps for ALDI, Walmart, Amazon Grocery, Target, Wegmans, and Publix, since availability and pickup windows may vary by location.

## A short checklist before you order

- Confirm your guest count and the kit's serving size.
- Divide price by servings.
- Check what's included and what's missing.
- Look at pickup or delivery timing, since the holiday is close.
- Decide on your one homemade upgrade.

## The takeaway

Don't pick the cheapest kit. Pick the cheapest per-person price that matches your guest count and how much cooking you actually want to do, then add one dish you make yourself.
