The winners of the 2026 Great American Beer Festival were announced at the outdoor festival on October 11, 2026. Forbes covered the results, describing the event as the largest commercial beer competition in the United States. This article won't recite the medal list, because the details I have don't include the individual winners. What it will do is show you how to take any GABF winners list and turn it into a trip: a short, doable plan that sends you to specific places to taste specific beers.

## Start With the Official Winners List

The awards are only useful if you work from the real results. Pull up the full 2026 list from the festival's own announcement or the Forbes coverage, and treat it as your menu. Don't rely on a recap, a social post, or someone's memory of who won what.

Once you have it, read it twice. The first pass is for names you already know. The second is for breweries and places you've never heard of, which is where the best trips come from.

## Pick One Region, Not the Whole Country

A national competition produces a national list, and nobody can drink their way across it in one weekend. Choose a single region or a single city and filter the winners down to breweries within reach.

A good rule: aim for three to five breweries you can visit within a day's drive of each other, or in one metro area. That is enough variety to compare, and few enough that you'll actually finish.

## Build a Tasting Checklist

The most practical way to use the awards is as a checklist you carry with you. For each stop, write down:

- The winning beer's name and style, copied exactly from the list
- The brewery's address and taproom hours
- One comparison beer from the same brewery, such as their flagship or whatever the bartender recommends

That last item matters. A medal tells you a beer impressed judges in a competition. Tasting it next to the beer locals order every week tells you something about the place.

## Call Ahead Before You Go

Award-winning beers aren't always on tap when you arrive. Batches sell out, seasonal beers rotate, and some winners may be limited releases. Check the brewery's website or social channels for the current tap list, or call the taproom.

If the winning beer isn't available, ask what's closest in style. Brewers and taproom staff are usually happy to point you to it, and that conversation is often the best part of the visit.

## Taste Like You're Judging

You don't need formal training to get more from a good beer. Try this simple routine at each stop:

1. **Look.** Note the color and clarity before you taste.
2. **Smell.** Take a short sniff before the first sip.
3. **Sip small.** Let the first taste sit, then notice what comes after.
4. **Compare.** Ask yourself how it differs from the everyday beer on your checklist.

Order flights or small pours when you can. You'll taste more beers, make better comparisons, and stay clear-headed enough to enjoy the drive to the next stop.

## Pair It With the Local Food

This is where a beer trip becomes a food trip. Ask each taproom what to eat nearby, or whether they have a kitchen or food truck on site. Regional dishes often pair naturally with the beers brewed in the same place, and the staff usually know the combinations that work.

Write down what you ate next to what you drank. Months later, that note will bring the whole stop back faster than a photo of the glass.

## Look for Patterns Across Your Stops

After a few breweries, you'll start to notice things: which styles keep showing up on the winners list, which ones a region seems to favor, and which beers you liked more than the medals suggested. That is the real payoff of using a competition as a guide. You're forming your own view of what's good rather than taking the judges' word for it.

Medals are a starting point. Your own palate gets the final say.
