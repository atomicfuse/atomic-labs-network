The GlenDronach distillery has spent two centuries doing one thing exceptionally well: ageing Highland single malt in sherry casks. So when the distillery chose its 200th anniversary to release a whisky finished in red wine casks, it wasn't a small creative decision. It was a statement.

The release in question is the Boynsmill House Edition — a 14-year-old single malt that sits at the intersection of everything the distillery has always stood for and something it has never quite done before. If you're curious about what a two-hundred-year-old sherry house does when it decides to experiment, this bottle is worth your attention.

## Two Centuries of Sherry, Then a Pivot

The GlenDronach built its reputation on sherry cask maturation — specifically Oloroso and Pedro Ximénez casks that give its whisky the rich, dark-fruit character the brand is known for. That house style isn't accidental; it's the result of a long, consistent commitment to a single method of maturation that most other distilleries have moved away from over the decades.

For a distillery so closely identified with sherry, introducing red wine cask finishing is genuinely unexpected. Sherry and red wine are not the same thing. Sherry casks contribute dried fruit, chocolate, and spice in a way that's been refined through generations of cooperage tradition. Red wine casks bring a different kind of fruit — fresher, tangier, with tannins that behave differently on the palate. Combining the two in a 14-year expression requires a careful hand, because the distillery's base spirit needs enough age and weight to hold both influences without either one overwhelming it.

The fact that GlenDronach chose this moment — its bicentennial — to try it says something about confidence. You don't reach 200 years by playing it safe, but you also don't celebrate 200 years by abandoning what made you.

## What's Actually in the Glass

The Boynsmill House Edition is a 14-year-old single malt, which puts it squarely in the range where GlenDronach's spirit tends to hit its stride. The distillery's core expressions have long shown that the spirit benefits from extended time in wood, and 14 years is enough for the sherry cask influence to fully integrate before the red wine finish comes into play.

Per the brief, the flavor profile combines the distillery's signature sherry cask maturation with the red wine cask finishing — meaning the red wine element is a finishing layer rather than the primary maturation vessel. That distinction matters. The whisky spent the bulk of its life in sherry wood, so the foundation is still very much a GlenDronach: expect the dark fruit, the richness, the depth. The red wine finish then adds something brighter and more structured on top of that base.

Think of it less as a departure and more as an evolution — the same house, redecorated rather than demolished.
