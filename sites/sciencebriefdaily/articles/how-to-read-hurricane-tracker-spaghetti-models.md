Forecasters are watching a system in the Gulf this week. The Weather Channel reports it could become Tropical Storm Isaias later this week, and WESH reports the National Hurricane Center (NHC) says a tropical depression could form. USA Today asked the question many people are typing into search bars: will it affect the US?

Nobody can answer that yet from a headline. But you can learn to read the tools behind the headlines. By the end of this article you'll know what a spaghetti plot actually shows, what to check first on a tracker, and how to avoid the most common misreadings.

## Start With the Official Source, Not the Squiggles

The NHC is the agency actively tracking this system, per the outlets above. Its outlook and advisories are the baseline everything else gets compared against. Before you look at any model chart, check what the NHC says about the system's chance of development and where it's expected to go.

Why this order matters: spaghetti plots are raw ingredients. The official forecast is the finished meal, built by forecasters who weigh many of those ingredients together.

## What a Spaghetti Plot Shows

A spaghetti plot draws many possible storm paths on one map. Each line comes from a different computer model, or from the same model run with slightly different starting conditions. WESH's coverage of this Gulf system references spaghetti models, which is where you'll likely see them this week.

Here's how to read one:

- **Tight bundle of lines:** the models broadly agree, so confidence in the track is higher.
- **Wide fan of lines:** the models disagree, so the path is uncertain. This is common for systems that haven't fully formed yet.
- **One line far from the rest:** an outlier. Don't fixate on it, whether it points at you or away from you.

### Why early plots are the least reliable

A system that hasn't yet organized into a tropical depression or storm gives models less to work with. Early in a system's life, expect lines to scatter. That scatter is not a prediction of a wild storm. It's a measure of what forecasters don't know yet.

## Know the Labels

Coverage of this system uses several terms, and they mean different things:

- **Invest:** a disturbance the NHC is flagging for closer study. WESH's link references "invest 92L," which is this kind of label.
- **Tropical depression:** an organized system with a defined circulation, at the weakest named stage.
- **Tropical storm:** a stronger stage, and the point at which a system gets a name. Reports say this one would be Isaias.

A system "could form" means it hasn't yet. Treat that wording literally: it's a watch-this-space signal, not a landfall forecast.

## Check These Four Things on Any Tracker

When you open a tracker, skip the dramatic colors and look for:

1. **The timestamp.** Forecasts update regularly. An old screenshot on social media can be badly out of date.
2. **The cone, if there is one.** It shows the likely range for the storm's center, not the area that will feel impacts. Wind and rain can reach well outside it.
3. **The development chance.** For systems not yet formed, this tells you how seriously to take the "could become a storm" language.
4. **The source.** Is the map from the NHC, a news outlet, or a random account? Prefer the first.

## What to Do While You Wait

If you live along the Gulf Coast or have travel plans there, the useful move now is low-effort preparation, not panic. Reports describe a system that is still developing, so you have time to do the dull things well:

- Check your travel booking's change or cancellation policy.
- Confirm you know your local emergency alert sources.
- Refill needed prescriptions and check that phones and power banks are charged.
- Look at the NHC outlook once or twice a day instead of refreshing constantly.

None of these cost much if the system fizzles, and all of them help if it doesn't.

## Avoid These Common Misreadings

**Treating one model as the forecast.** A single line on a plot is one possibility.

**Reading the cone as the damage zone.** It tracks the center, not the impacts.

**Assuming a name means doom.** A named storm can be weak or stay offshore. A lack of a name doesn't guarantee a quiet outcome, either.

**Doomscrolling stale posts.** Dramatic maps travel faster than updates.

## The Takeaway

The next time a storm headline sends you to a tracker, open the NHC page first, read the timestamp, and treat spaghetti lines as a measure of uncertainty rather than a verdict. Then check back on a schedule instead of all day.
