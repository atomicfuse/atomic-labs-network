If a machine hands you a proof no human can easily read, who actually knows whether it's true? That question is now hitting mathematics head-on, and the answer says something useful about every field AI is entering: the valuable skill may shift from finding answers to making sense of them.

## The claim that set it off

Per a report originally published by Phys, mathematics is having an existential crisis. On Nov. 20, 2026, a mathematician at the AI company Anthropic claimed that, while working with the model Claude, he had solved the generalized Riemann hypothesis. That is one of the most famous open problems in the field.

The claim is just that, a claim, and this article can't verify it. But the Phys report uses it, along with recent advances from Anthropic and OpenAI, to make a bigger point. AI models appear able to produce mathematics at a scale and speed that humans cannot easily check or understand.

## From "solving" to "digesting"

The article's central argument is a change in what mathematicians value. For centuries, prestige went to the person who solved the problem. The piece suggests the emphasis is moving toward "digesting": understanding a proof, simplifying it, and finding out what it's good for.

Think of it like a restaurant. If a machine can cook ten thousand dishes an hour, the scarce skill is no longer cooking. It's knowing which dishes are worth serving, what's in them, and who they're for.

## The 250,000-page problem

The source piece raises a worry that sounds almost comic until you sit with it: what happens when an AI answer is simply too big to trust? A proof that runs to something like 250,000 pages can't be read by one person in a career, let alone checked line by line over a weekend.

That's where the human job changes. A result nobody can follow is hard to build on, hard to teach, and hard to believe. Someone has to boil it down into ideas people can hold in their heads. That boiling-down is the digesting.

## What MRI machines reveal

The article points to compressed sensing in MRI as an example of why human insight still matters. Compressed sensing is a mathematical idea used in medical imaging. Its value didn't come only from a theorem being true. It came from people understanding the idea well enough to see how it could be applied to a real problem: getting useful images out of an MRI.

That's the pattern the piece wants mathematicians to notice. A correct answer sitting in a file does nothing. An answer that someone understands can be carried into a hospital.

## What changes for training and teaching

The article says this raises urgent questions for research, Ph.D. training, and education as AI begins to automate once-prestigious intellectual work. If a doctoral student's traditional rite of passage is solving a hard problem, what happens when software can often do that part?

The implication, which is my reading rather than a finding from the report, is that training could lean more on skills like:

- Reading and explaining other people's (or machines') proofs
- Simplifying a messy argument into a clear one
- Spotting where a result could be used
- Judging whether a claimed result deserves trust

None of these are new. They just move from the sidelines to center stage.

## Why this matters outside math

Math is an early test case for a wider shift. The same pattern could reach science, coding, education, and creative work: AI produces the output, and people are left to interpret it, check it, and decide what it means. If that holds, the person who can explain why an answer matters becomes more useful, not less.

It also means you don't need to be a mathematician to prepare. Whenever a tool gives you a confident answer, practice the digesting step: restate it in your own words, ask what would make it wrong, and name one place you could use it.

## The takeaway

Next time an AI gives you an answer, don't stop at "is it right?" Ask "can I explain it to someone else?" That second question is the skill mathematicians are now being asked to sharpen, and it's likely to be useful to the rest of us too.
