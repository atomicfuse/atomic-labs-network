When the universe was less than 700 million years old, the black hole powering a quasar called J0313-1806 had already reached about 3 billion times the mass of the sun, per Phys. That is one of the earliest giants known, and it raises an awkward question: how did anything get that big that quickly?

A new study in Astronomy & Astrophysics, reported by Phys, offers a surprising answer. The black holes may have grown fast because their feeding was disorganized. By the end of this article, you'll understand why a messy meal can be a faster meal, and what telescopes could soon prove or disprove the idea.

## The speed limit problem

Black holes grow by pulling in gas. But that gas doesn't fall straight in. It swirls into a hot, glowing disk first, and the glow pushes outward on the incoming material. That pressure limits how quickly a black hole can feed.

Start with a modest black hole left over from a dead star, and there simply may not be enough time to reach billions of solar masses under that limit. So astronomers have proposed two ways around it:

- **Heavy seeds:** the black holes were born already enormous, perhaps from rare processes.
- **Super-Eddington feeding:** the black holes briefly or continuously feed faster than the usual limit allows.

The new work argues for a third path that needs neither: ordinary, stellar-mass seeds, plus what the researchers call chaotic accretion.

## What chaotic accretion means

Imagine a black hole being fed by one steady disk of gas, always spinning the same direction. Every bit of gas adds spin, and the black hole spins faster and faster, like a playground roundabout getting pushes in the same direction.

In chaotic accretion, the gas disks arrive randomly oriented. One feeding episode spins the black hole one way, and the next may push at a different angle or against it. The spin never builds up. Per the study as reported by Phys, this keeps the black hole's spin low.

## Why low spin means faster growth

Here is the counterintuitive part. A fast-spinning black hole is an efficient engine: it converts a larger share of the infalling matter into radiation. That sounds good, but the radiation is exactly what pushes back on incoming gas and holds growth in check.

A slow-spinning black hole is less efficient. More of the matter gets swallowed and less escapes as light. Less radiation means less outward pressure, so the black hole can keep eating at a high pace and put more of its meals into mass. In this picture, inefficiency is a feature.

The model suggests this could explain the earliest known giant black holes without rare heavy seeds or long-term super-Eddington feeding.

## Where the little red dots come in

The question has become more pressing because of JWST. It and upcoming surveys are revealing surprisingly massive black holes, along with compact "little red dot" galaxies in the early universe, per the summary of the research. These finds have intensified the debate over how cosmic giants formed so quickly. If early black holes are common and big, a rare-event explanation looks less comfortable, and a growth mode that works with ordinary starting points looks more attractive.

The study's reporting does not settle what the little red dots are. Treat any link between them and chaotic feeding as an open question rather than a conclusion.

## How the idea could be tested

A good model makes predictions that can fail. This one predicts that early supermassive black holes should be slow spinners, products of chaotic feeding. Future missions, including Euclid, Roman, and NewAthena, could soon test that, according to the report. If early giants turn out to spin fast, the model is in trouble. If they spin slowly, productive chaos moves up the list of suspects.

## Three explanations, one still standing

Heavy seeds, speed-limit-breaking feeding, and chaotic accretion are all still candidates. This study does not eliminate the others; it shows that the simplest starting ingredients might be enough if the feeding is messy in the right way.

The next time you hear that something in the universe grew too fast to explain, remember that the explanation may not be more power, but less order. Watch for spin measurements of early black holes. That is the number that will decide this one.
