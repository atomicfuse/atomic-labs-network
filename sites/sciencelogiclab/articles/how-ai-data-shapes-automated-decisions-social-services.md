A University of Iowa study, reported by Phys, argues that social service agencies using AI in child welfare, intimate partner violence, and elder abuse cases need safeguards to prevent biased or harmful decisions. By the end of this article, you will understand how a "neutral" algorithm can still produce unfair results, and what protections researchers say should come standard.

The timing matters because AI is moving into public-service systems now. Per Phys, that includes Iowa's new Google-partnered child welfare platform, called VISION. The stakes are high: algorithmic decisions can affect family separation, policing, benefits, and access to support.

## How "neutral" data becomes biased automation

An algorithm learns from past records. That sounds objective, but records reflect past decisions, including who got reported, who got investigated, and who got watched most closely.

The Phys report highlights a concern that these systems can reproduce existing surveillance of poor families and families of color. If those families were already more likely to appear in agency files, a system trained on those files may learn that they look "riskier." It then flags them more often, which creates more records, which feeds the next round of training.

That loop is what we can call biased automation. The math may be flawless, but it is faithfully repeating a skewed starting point, only faster and at larger scale.

A simple analogy: if a security guard only ever checks bags from one entrance, he will find more problems at that entrance. A program trained on his reports would conclude that entrance is the dangerous one.

## Why the stakes are higher in social services

A bad movie recommendation costs you an evening. A bad flag in a child welfare, abuse, or benefits case can mean an investigation, a family separation, or lost support. The Iowa researchers focus on three areas: child welfare, intimate partner violence, and elder abuse. In each, people are often already in crisis, and a mistaken or biased decision lands on someone with little power to push back.

## The safeguards researchers recommend

Per the report, the study's recommendations include four main protections. Here is what each means in plain terms.

### Survivor input

People who have lived through these systems should help shape the tools. They know where a system could misread a situation or cause harm, and designers often do not.

### Bias audits

An audit checks whether a tool treats groups differently, for example whether it flags poor families or families of color at higher rates. The point is to look for the pattern before the tool shapes real lives, and to keep checking afterward.

### Contestable decisions

If an algorithm helps drive a decision, the affected person should be able to challenge it. That requires the process to be understandable enough that someone can say what went wrong. A score nobody can explain cannot be appealed in any meaningful way.

### Resources, not just surveillance

The study also argues for using AI to connect families with resources rather than to increase surveillance. This is the most interesting shift. The same technology that could flag a family as a risk could instead help match that family with the support it needs.

## Surveillance tool or matching tool?

The difference comes down to the question the system is built to answer.

- A surveillance-style tool asks: who should we watch or investigate?
- A resource-matching tool asks: who needs help, and what help fits?

Both can use similar data. But the first can deepen the loop described above, while the second points the system toward support. The researchers' recommendation suggests that how a tool is aimed matters as much as how accurate it is.

## Questions worth asking about any agency algorithm

If you work in or interact with social services, the study's recommendations translate into practical questions:

1. Were people with lived experience involved in designing the tool?
2. Has anyone audited it for bias, and will they keep doing so?
3. If it flags someone, can that person see why and challenge the result?
4. Is it being used to find families support, or mainly to monitor them?

If an agency cannot answer these, that is useful information in itself.

## The takeaway

When an agency says its AI is objective, ask what data it learned from and who checked the results. Fair outcomes come from safeguards built in before the system goes live, not from the assumption that math is neutral.
