Picture a lunar habitat sitting through a moonquake. On Earth, a quake's shaking typically fades within a minute or two and weakens quickly with distance. On the moon, according to new research, the shaking can last nearly an hour and stay strong across more than 1,000 kilometers. By the end of this article, you'll know why the moon behaves this way and why it matters for anyone planning to build there.

## The news: a moon-specific shaking model

Per a report from Phys, researchers have developed the first lunar-specific ground motion model for shallow moonquakes. A ground motion model is a tool engineers use to predict how hard the ground will shake at a given distance from a quake. It is the starting point for deciding how strong a building needs to be.

The team combined seismic data recorded during the Apollo missions with physics-based simulations of the lunar environment. The result suggests that shaking on the moon can remain strong over distances far beyond what Earth-based models would predict.

## Why you can't just borrow Earth's rules

Civil engineers looking ahead to lunar construction face an obvious question: do ground motion models developed for Earth work on the moon? The answer from this research is that they don't do the job well. Models built from Earth's quakes bake in Earth's geology, and the moon's geology is very different.

An Earth-based model would tell you that shaking drops off at some familiar rate as you move away from the source. On the moon, the shaking doesn't drop off that way. A structure that looks safely far from a quake's origin by Earth standards could still be in a zone of strong shaking.

## Why the shaking lingers: a cold, cracked moon

The physics is the surprising part. Two features of the moon help seismic energy travel farther and linger longer: its cold interior and its fractured surface.

A useful way to picture this is a bell. Strike a bell and it rings for a long time, because little of the vibration energy gets absorbed. Strike a pillow and the thud dies instantly. The research points to the moon behaving more like the bell than Earth does, with energy that carries a long way and takes a long time to settle. (That bell comparison is my analogy for the idea, not the researchers' wording.)

The fractured surface adds to the effect. Rather than the shaking being quickly damped out, the energy keeps moving through the broken-up upper layers, which helps explain why a single event can keep the ground moving for close to an hour.

## What the numbers mean for a building

Two figures from the research stand out:

- **Duration:** shaking can last nearly an hour.
- **Distance:** strong shaking can extend more than 1,000 kilometers.

For a designer, duration matters as much as intensity. A structure that can survive a sharp jolt may still be stressed by shaking that goes on and on, because repeated loading is hard on materials and joints. And a distance of over 1,000 kilometers means a base can't count on simply being far from known fault activity.

## Apollo data is still paying off

The Apollo missions placed seismometers on the lunar surface, and their records are the foundation of this model. Decades later, that data is being turned into practical guidance. It's a good reminder that a well-collected dataset can keep answering questions its original designers never asked, such as how to build a habitat.

The Apollo record is limited, so the researchers also leaned on simulations of lunar physics to fill in how shaking behaves. Pairing real measurements with physics modeling is what lets the team make predictions about conditions the old instruments never fully sampled.

## Why it matters now

Governments and private companies are planning lunar bases, habitats, and infrastructure. Those projects need realistic seismic hazard assessments, the same kind of analysis that guides construction on Earth. Until now, there was no moon-specific model to base those assessments on. This work is the first step toward one.

It also hints at a larger design shift. "Moonquake-proof" buildings may need different rules than earthquake-resistant ones on Earth, built around long-lasting, far-reaching shaking rather than the short, local bursts that Earth codes assume. The specific design standards are still to be worked out, and the research is a model for hazard assessment, not a finished building code.

## The takeaway

When you hear that something works on Earth, ask what Earth is quietly contributing. The moon's cold, cracked crust changes how shaking behaves, and any plan for living there has to start from the moon's own physics, not our own planet's habits.
