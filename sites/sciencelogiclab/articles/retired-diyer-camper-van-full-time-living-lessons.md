A retired man named Steve built a camper van with no prior experience, and he has lived in it full-time for five years. Autoevolution profiled his 2021 Ram ProMaster 2500 build, and it offers a useful answer to a question many people ask: can you really live comfortably in a van without a luxury budget or a professional builder? Going by his five years on the road, the answer appears to be yes. Here is what his setup shows about making a small space work.

## The Van: A 2021 Ram ProMaster 2500

Steve's home is a 2021 Ram ProMaster 2500. Autoevolution describes the finished build as affordable, with a warm, cabin-like interior, practical storage, solar power and off-road-friendly upgrades.

The choice of base vehicle matters. The ProMaster is a popular camper platform, and the outlet's profile points to space, price and practicality as the reasons it works for a build like this. For a first-timer, a van that gives you a roomy, simple shell to work in lowers the barrier to entry.

## Why the Cabin Look Is More Than Decoration

The most striking feature is the interior. Rather than a cold, utilitarian metal box, Steve's van is styled like a small cabin, according to Autoevolution. That warm look is not just a matter of taste.

When a space is your only home, rather than a weekend rental, how it feels matters every day. A van that feels like a cozy room is easier to live in than one that feels like a work vehicle with a bed in it. That is our reading of the design choice, not a claim from the report, but it fits what a five-year stay suggests: people stay where they feel comfortable.

## The Features That Make Full-Time Living Possible

Autoevolution highlights a handful of practical elements, and they are the ones that matter most for living in a van rather than visiting in one.

### Storage

The profile calls out practical storage. When you live in a van, every item needs a home, or the space becomes unlivable within days. Storage is what separates a camper from a cluttered cargo van.

### Solar power

Solar power is part of the build. For someone living in the van full-time, generating your own electricity means fewer stops to plug in and more freedom to park where you like.

### Off-road-friendly upgrades

The van also carries upgrades meant for rougher terrain. Those let Steve reach places that a standard van might avoid, which is a big part of the appeal for an adventurer.

## No Experience Required, Within Reason

The headline detail is that Steve built this home without any prior experience, per Autoevolution. He has reportedly lived in it for five years without any issues.

That does not mean building a camper is effortless. The profile does not detail how many hours the project took or what it cost beyond calling it affordable, so we can't offer numbers. What it does show is that a beginner can end up with a durable, comfortable home. Five years of full-time use is a far more convincing test than a weekend trip.

## Why Retirees Are Asking This Question

Autoevolution frames the story against ongoing interest in housing costs, retirement budgets and minimalist travel. A self-built van is one answer to all three: a place to live that you own, designed around how you actually want to spend your days.

That said, a single success story is just that. Steve's experience shows what is possible, not what is guaranteed for everyone. Climate, health, local parking rules and personal temperament all affect whether van life suits you, and the profile does not address them.

## What You Can Borrow From His Build

You don't need to quit your lease to use the ideas here.

- **Start with a practical base.** A spacious, reasonably priced van like the ProMaster gives you room to learn.
- **Plan storage first.** If everything has a place, small spaces feel bigger.
- **Make it feel like home.** A warm interior is part of function, not an extra.
- **Add power you can generate yourself.** Solar supports independence.

## The Takeaway

Before you price out a build, decide what would make a tiny space feel like home to you, then design around that. Steve's five-year experiment suggests that comfort, not cost or experience, is what makes a van livable.
