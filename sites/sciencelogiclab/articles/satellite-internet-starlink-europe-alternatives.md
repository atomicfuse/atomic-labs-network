A recent Starlink slowdown showed how much Ukraine leans on a single network. According to the BBC, Ukraine relies on SpaceX's Starlink and Starshield services for battlefield communications, drone operations and wider defense work. Europe has alternatives in development, the BBC reports, but none can yet match Starlink's speed, scale, portability or military usefulness.

By the end of this article, you'll understand why satellite internet matters so much in modern conflict, and why swapping providers is far harder than switching your home Wi-Fi plan.

## Why a satellite network matters on the battlefield

Modern military units depend on a steady data connection. Drones, command centers and troops in the field all need to share information quickly. Where ground networks are damaged or simply absent, satellites can fill the gap.

That is the role the BBC describes Starlink playing in Ukraine: a kind of invisible backbone for communications and drone warfare. When it works, few people notice it. When it slows down, the effects show up fast.

## What the recent slowdown revealed

The BBC says the recent Starlink slowdown exposed how vulnerable Ukraine is when a critical military capability is controlled by outside political and commercial actors. The network is owned by a private company, and its owner is Elon Musk. So a tool that matters to national defense sits outside the control of Ukraine's government.

This is our analysis rather than a claim from the BBC, but the lesson is easy to see. Any system you rely on but don't control is a risk, however well it performs day to day.

## Why Europe can't simply replace it

The BBC's headline question is whether Europe can plug the gap. Its answer, for now, is no. Its description puts it bluntly: a decent alternative to Starlink doesn't exist yet.

The BBC points to four qualities where current European options fall short:

- **Speed:** how quickly data moves.
- **Scale:** how many users the network can serve.
- **Portability:** how easily equipment can be carried and used in the field.
- **Military usefulness:** how well the service fits real defense needs.

A replacement would need to match Starlink on all four at once. That is a tall order for systems that are still being built.

## Europe's push for its own space infrastructure

Europe is reassessing its defense autonomy, per the BBC, and satellite connectivity has become a frontline security issue. One of the angles in the coverage is Iris², Europe's effort toward space-based strategic independence. The aim is to have sovereign infrastructure in place before the next crisis hits.

The key word is *before*. Networks like this are not switched on overnight, and the gap between a plan and a working system is exactly where Ukraine is exposed today.

## What this means for you

You don't need to follow every defense headline to take something from this story. Infrastructure you can't see often decides how well everything else works. The next time you hear about a satellite launch or a space-policy debate, ask a simple question: who controls the network, and what happens if they change their mind?

That question, more than any single gadget, is what Europe is now trying to answer.
