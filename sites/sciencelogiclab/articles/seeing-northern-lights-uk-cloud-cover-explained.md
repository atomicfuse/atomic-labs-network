Stargazers across the UK were treated to the vivid colours of the aurora borealis on Saturday night, Halloween, according to the BBC. The display is reportedly set to be visible again on Sunday night, particularly in Scotland, Northern Ireland and Northern England. But the same report says cloud cover and reduced solar activity may limit what you can see compared with the night before.

That last detail is the useful one. Here's how to think about it, so you know what to look for and when to give up and try another night.

## Why Sunday may look different from Halloween

The BBC's report points to two things working against Sunday's show: cloud and lower solar activity. They are separate problems, and it helps to keep them apart.

- **Solar activity** is the fuel. The aurora is driven by the Sun, so when activity drops, the display can fade or sit further north.
- **Cloud cover** is the curtain. The aurora happens far above the weather. Clouds don't weaken it. They simply hide it from you.

That means a strong aurora under thick cloud can look like nothing at all, while a modest one under a clear sky can still reward you.

## Why cloud is the real enemy

The lights are a high-altitude event, while clouds are a low-altitude one. If clouds sit between you and the sky, there is no way to see through them, however good your eyesight or your camera.

This is why the most practical skill in aurora hunting isn't astronomy. It's reading a weather forecast. A clear patch over your location matters more than a perfect prediction of solar activity.

## Where in the UK to look

The BBC names Scotland, Northern Ireland and Northern England as the areas best placed to see the lights on Sunday night. If you live further south, the odds are lower, and the report doesn't suggest otherwise.

If you're in one of those regions, a few simple choices help:

1. **Face north.** The aurora is typically seen low on the northern horizon from the UK. That's general stargazing guidance rather than something from the BBC report, but it's a sensible place to start.
2. **Get away from streetlights.** Light pollution washes out faint glows.
3. **Check your local cloud forecast** before heading out, not just the aurora alert.
4. **Give your eyes time.** Dark adaptation takes a while, so put your phone away for a bit.

## Why a camera can help

Phones and cameras can often pick up colour that your eyes struggle to see in the dark. A faint glow that looks greyish to you may show up as green or pink in a photo. If you're not sure whether you're seeing the aurora, a quick night-mode shot is a handy way to check. Hold the phone steady, or rest it on something solid.

## Why this is a rare treat

Aurora sightings in the UK are uncommon, which is why Halloween's display caught so much attention. The lights are more familiar to people much further north, so seeing them from British skies feels special. Even a modest display can be memorable if you go in with realistic expectations.

## What to do tonight

Check your local cloud forecast first. If the sky is clear and you're in Scotland, Northern Ireland or Northern England, head somewhere dark, face north and give your eyes a few minutes. If it's cloudy, don't write off the aurora. Nothing is wrong with it. It's just hidden, and another clear night may bring another chance.
