## The real clash: credentials versus evidence

When a former official talks about UFOs, the title carries weight. People assume access to secret files means access to answers. When a Harvard scientist pushes back, the name carries its own weight.

But here's the surprising part: neither credential is evidence. A job history tells you where someone worked. It doesn't tell you what was found. Science treats a claim the same way no matter who says it, and that's the lens worth borrowing.

## Why this topic keeps coming back

Per the BroBible report, UFO and UAP (unidentified anomalous phenomena) claims stay in the spotlight because government hearings, whistleblower allegations, and scientific investigations keep pushing the subject into mainstream debate. That mix matters. Each new claim lands in a world where people already suspect something is being withheld, so even vague statements get attention.

## First, separate three different claims

Much of the confusion comes from blending ideas that are very different in strength.

### 1. "There are things in the sky we can't identify."

This is the modest claim, and it's almost trivially true. "Unidentified" means only that someone hasn't worked out what it was. It says nothing about where it came from.

### 2. "Those things are not human-made."

This is a bigger step. It requires ruling out aircraft, balloons, instrument glitches, and other ordinary explanations. Ruling things out takes data, not just a witness's impression.

### 3. "Those things are alien."

This is the biggest leap. It asks you to accept a specific, extraordinary explanation. Skeptics usually object here, because "we don't know what it is" and "it's aliens" are not the same sentence.

When you read a story like this one, ask which of the three claims is actually being made. Often it's the first, dressed up as the third.

## A five-question test you can use anywhere

You don't need a physics degree. Run any alien claim through these questions:

1. **Can anyone else check it?** Real evidence can be examined by independent people, not just described by one.
2. **Is there a physical object or raw data?** Clear sensor records or a recovered sample beat a recollection.
3. **Have ordinary explanations been ruled out?** If nobody has tried, the claim isn't finished.
4. **Would it hold up if the speaker were anonymous?** Strip off the title. Is the evidence still impressive?
5. **Is the claim specific enough to be wrong?** Vague claims can't be tested. Testable ones can be confirmed or knocked down.

A claim that passes all five deserves serious attention. A claim that fails most of them may still be sincere, but it's a story, not a finding.

## Why "burden of proof" isn't unfair

Skeptics sometimes get accused of closed-mindedness. But the principle is the same one that applies to any big claim: the person making it supplies the support. If someone told you they had a new cure for a disease, you'd want trial results, not just a confident tone. The more extraordinary the claim, the stronger the evidence needs to be.

This cuts both ways. Asking for proof isn't the same as saying aliens are impossible. It simply says the question is open until something solid arrives.

## How to read the next headline

The media cycle around Pentagon UFO insiders tends to reward drama: insider versus academic, secrets versus skeptics. That framing is entertaining, but it can hide the plain question underneath. Watch for these habits:

- **Headlines that stress who said it** rather than what was shown.
- **Words like "claims" and "alleges"**, which are doing honest work. A claim is not a result.
- **Missing details.** If a story never names the evidence, that absence is itself information.

Evidence that moves the debate would be shareable, checkable, and open to independent analysis. A dramatic statement, however confident or well-credentialed, doesn't do that on its own.

## The takeaway

Next time an insider or an academic makes headlines about aliens, ignore the résumé for a moment and ask one question: what could I, or any independent scientist, actually check? If the answer is nothing yet, the right response isn't belief or mockery. It's patience.
