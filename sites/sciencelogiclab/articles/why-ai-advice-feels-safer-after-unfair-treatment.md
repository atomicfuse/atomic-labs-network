Picture this: you need a dinner plan for a guest with a gluten intolerance, and you have 20 euros to spend. You could ask a shop assistant. Or you could ask an AI-powered tool like Hopla. Research reported by Phys suggests that for some people, the choice depends on something unexpected: whether they have recently been treated unfairly.

By the end of this article, you'll know why that happens, what researchers call the shift, and how to use AI advice without mistaking "feels neutral" for "is neutral."

## The surprising finding: unfair treatment can flip your trust

Psychologists and marketers have long talked about "algorithm aversion," the tendency to distrust automated recommendations and prefer a human's judgment. Per the Phys report, the research finds that this can reverse for consumers who have experienced discrimination. Aversion becomes what the researchers describe as "algorithm appreciation."

In other words, the same person who would normally lean on a human expert may lean on a chatbot instead, not because it gives better answers, but because of how they feel after being treated badly.

## Why a chatbot can feel safer than a person

The reported explanation is emotional. Discrimination can leave people embarrassed, and embarrassment makes the next social interaction feel risky. Asking a human for advice means exposing yourself to a possible raised eyebrow, a snap judgment, or another unfair moment.

An AI doesn't seem to do any of that. It feels neutral and less judgmental. There's no face to read, no tone to second-guess, and no one to whom you owe an explanation.

Think of it like the difference between asking a question in a crowded room and typing it into a search box at midnight. The information may be similar. The social cost is not.

## Where this shows up in everyday life

AI assistants are moving into shopping, banking, health care, and customer service. Those are exactly the places where people can feel judged: about money, about bodies, about who they are. If an automated option is available, someone who has been burned before may reasonably choose it.

That makes this more than a quirk of consumer psychology. Brands often pitch AI as convenient. This research hints that for some customers, the real appeal is protection from social judgment.

## The catch: feeling neutral isn't the same as being neutral

This is the part worth slowing down for. The finding is about perception. The research, as described by Phys, is about how AI feels to people who have faced discrimination. It doesn't show that AI advice is free of bias.

An algorithm can still reflect flawed data or design choices. The danger is an illusion of neutrality: if people trust a system precisely because it seems impartial, they may be less likely to question it. That's my analysis rather than a finding from the study, but it follows naturally from the idea that comfort can lower your guard.

## How to use AI advice wisely

You don't have to give up the comfort. A few habits keep it useful:

- **Treat the answer as a starting point.** For the gluten-free dinner, an AI suggestion is a great draft menu. Check ingredient labels yourself.
- **Cross-check high-stakes advice.** For banking or health decisions, confirm with a second source, ideally a human professional or an official resource.
- **Notice why you chose AI.** If the reason is "I didn't want to be judged," that's valid. It's also a cue to be a little extra skeptical, since comfort isn't evidence of accuracy.
- **Ask who is accountable.** If the system gets it wrong, can you reach a person who can fix it?

## What businesses should take from this

Offering AI as an option can make some customers more comfortable. But it shouldn't replace human accountability. If customers turn to automated tools to escape unfair treatment, the underlying problem is still the unfair treatment. A chatbot is a workaround, not a fix.

The healthiest setup gives people a choice: an AI that's easy to use, and a human who's reachable, respectful, and responsible when it matters.

## The takeaway

Next time you reach for a chatbot instead of asking a person, ask yourself one quick question: am I choosing it because it's better, or because it feels safer? Either answer is fine. Just don't let "safe" stand in for "correct."
