An AI tool edited a photo of Rhiannon Morgan for a work newsletter, and she was shocked by the result, according to the BBC. The tool had changed how she looked. It had not been asked to erase who she was, but that is effectively what happened.

Her story is one of several in a BBC report on how AI image tools treat visible differences such as scars, birthmarks and medical conditions. By the end of this article, you will understand why a photo editor might "fix" a face that was never broken, and what you can do to avoid it.

## What the BBC found

Per the BBC, people with visible differences say it is offensive and dehumanising when AI image tools erase scars, birthmarks and medical conditions in response to a request to make someone look "normal."

BBC Wales also ran its own test of major AI tools. The results were mixed:

- Some tools altered visible differences.
- Others refused, citing consent or identity grounds.

That split matters. It shows the behavior is not inevitable. Some tools were built with guardrails that stop them from rewriting a person's face, while others were not.

## Why "make them normal" is a loaded instruction

An image tool cannot know what you mean by "normal." It has to guess, and the guess comes from patterns in the data it learned from.

That is an explanation of how these systems generally work, not something the BBC report spelled out, but it helps make sense of the results. If a tool learned from huge collections of images, it picked up the look that appears most often and is most often polished: smooth skin, symmetrical features, no marks. Ask it for "normal" and it may simply move your photo toward that average.

The word "normal" does the damage. It treats a birthmark or a scar as an error to be corrected, rather than a feature of a real person.

## Why this is about consent, not just a glitch

It is tempting to file this under "software bug." The people in the BBC's report describe it differently. For them, having a visible difference erased is an issue of identity.

The fact that some tools refused on consent or identity grounds supports that view. Those companies treated changing a person's face as something that needs a reason, not a default. A photo of a colleague in a newsletter is a good example. The person pictured may never have agreed to be altered, and the edit can say something about them they did not choose.

## Why it matters more as editing gets built in

AI editing is no longer a specialist tool. It is becoming part of everyday photo apps, workplace design software and social media. That means the beauty norms inside these tools shape how many people are represented, often without anyone noticing.

The story also lands amid wider scrutiny of AI bias, consent and disability representation, and of who gets to define "normal."

## What better photo editing looks like

The BBC report does not offer a step-by-step guide, so these are practical suggestions drawn from its findings.

### Ask for specific changes

Vague requests invite the tool to decide what needs fixing. Instead of "make this look better" or "make them normal," say what you want: crop tighter, brighten the background, fix the lighting. A specific request leaves less room for the tool to reshape someone's face.

### Get the subject's say first

If you are editing a photo of someone else, such as a colleague for a newsletter, ask them. The tools that refused to edit on consent grounds are modeling the right habit.

### Compare before you publish

Put the original and edited versions side by side. Rhiannon Morgan's experience shows that changes can be large enough to shock the person in the picture. If the person would not recognize themselves, do not publish it.

### Treat changes to a face as a choice, not a default

Retouching that removes a scar or birthmark should happen only if the person wants it. Their lived experience is not a flaw in the file.

## What the companies can do

One of the angles raised in the coverage is that AI companies should include disabled and visibly different users when testing their tools. That is a reasonable test of any editor: if it quietly changes a face with a scar or birthmark when given a vague prompt, the people who would notice that were probably not in the room.

## The takeaway

Before you let an AI tool touch a photo of a person, tell it exactly what to change, and leave the face alone unless its owner has said otherwise.
