A giant manta ray can grow to 30 feet long and weigh up to 4,409 pounds. Nobody starts out that way. A new study in Frontiers in Fish Science, covered by Popular Science, shows how baby giant manta rays (Mobula birostris) in Central America survive the small years: they stay close to shore, where sharks and orcas are less of a threat.

By the end of this article, you'll know where young mantas hide, why size changes everything for ocean survival, and why the safest nursery in the sea doesn't protect them for life.

## Why small manta rays can't live like big ones

Baby mantas, called pups, don't have the bulk that protects adults. In the open ocean they face numerous sharks and orca (Orcinus orca). Their answer, per the study, is to stay on the coast.

"Mature adults can rely on their massive body size and their ability to dive deep to evade threats. But juveniles don't have that luxury," said Jeffry Madrigal-Mesén, a study co-author and project leader of Mantas Costa Rica.

Think of it as a school zone for the ocean. Adults can handle the highway. Juveniles stay where the traffic is lighter.

## What the shallows offer a growing manta

According to the research, shallow coastal refuges give young rays three things at once:

- **Distance from predators.** The pups' safe spots don't overlap with their predators' hunting grounds.
- **Plenty of food.** The coast supplies what a growing body needs.
- **Hiding places and cleaning.** Reefs offer cover and essential services like cleaning.

The study counted young mantas as those with a body of about 10 feet or less. All of them stuck to these shallow, nearshore waters.

## How researchers tracked them

Between 2018 and 2025, the team studied mantas at five locations using three tools together:

1. **Acoustic telemetry**, which uses underwater sound pulses to monitor movements. It tracked 59 individual rays.
2. **Satellite telemetry**, which uses transmitters to follow large-scale movements. It covered 12 individuals.
3. **Photo identification**, which identifies individual rays.

Together they produced a dataset of more than 12,000 detections. "If we hadn't used all three technologies together, we simply wouldn't have been able to piece this puzzle together," Madrigal-Mesén said.

One detail stands out: the same young rays showed up again and again. "We see the same individuals more often than once a year," he said, adding that some were recently sighted at the exact spot where they were tagged.

## What changes when manta rays grow up

The study shows a dramatic shift. "Giant manta rays in Central America undergo a dramatic shift in space use as they mature," Madrigal-Mesén said. Juveniles stayed strictly in shallow nearshore water, while most tracked adults made long-distance migrations along the continental shelf break.

Nine mature mantas in the study took these expansive journeys. But leaving the nursery doesn't mean cutting ties with the coast.

### Meet Mickey Mouse, the loyal manta

One manta, nicknamed Mickey Mouse for a spot pattern on his belly, was unusually approachable for his species. That let the team tag him several times over the years and build a continuous record. In 2020 he logged 462 acoustic detections at a coastal hub called El Secreto. In 2023 he returned to the same shelf and stayed for 38 days.

The researchers say this suggests adults stay loyal to particular sites, too. Madrigal-Mesén explained that nearshore cleaning stations "act as primary social hubs where courtship and reproductive interactions take place." Returning to a predictable coastal spot, he said, lets mature rays take care of social and cleaning needs before long regional migrations.

## The catch: the nursery is protected, the journey isn't

Coastal areas are often legally protected marine reserves. The open ocean is another story. Per the study, adult mantas leaving the coast lose that legal protection and are exposed to industrial fisheries and vessel strikes.

The team concludes that marine protected areas alone aren't enough for a species this mobile as it swims across Central America. Manta rays are endangered, and a patchwork of national rules doesn't match an animal that crosses borders.

## What "conservation swimways" would do

The researchers suggest multi-national "conservation swimways." The idea is to link the juvenile coastal refuges, which sit within individual countries, with the adult migratory corridors. In plain terms, protection would follow the animal's whole life instead of one stage of it.

Madrigal-Mesén said the study "opens a new chapter for manta ray research in Central America," and that the team hopes to draw in future collaborators for planned conservation projects.

## The takeaway

When you hear that an animal is "protected," ask where and at what age. For manta rays, a safe childhood on the coast is only half the story, so the next time you read about ocean conservation, look for plans that cover the full journey, not just the nursery.
