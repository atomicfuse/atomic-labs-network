Netflix's new series *The New Stanford Prison Experiment* has pushed the 1971 study back into public conversation. If you've heard the story, you probably remember the headline version: ordinary students handed power turned cruel. By the end of this article, you'll know why that version stuck, why it deserves a skeptical look, and what these studies still do well.

The argument comes from a piece originally reported by Phys, which looks at the Stanford study alongside Milgram's obedience experiments. Its core point is simple: these studies haven't lasted because they're airtight. They've lasted because of the question they raise.

## The question that makes them unforgettable

Per Phys, the lasting power of both studies comes from a disturbing moral puzzle: why do ordinary people choose harm when they could refuse?

That question is personal. It doesn't ask about monsters. It asks about people like you, your coworkers, your neighbors. A study that seems to answer it, even roughly, is hard to forget.

That's why the Stanford prison experiment and Milgram's work get taught, retold, and dramatized decades later. They offer a story about human nature that fits in a single sentence.

## What the critiques say

The Phys article notes that both studies have drawn major critiques of their methods, their ethics, and the way their results were interpreted. Those are three separate kinds of problem, and it helps to keep them apart:

- **Methods:** Was the study designed in a way that could actually test the idea?
- **Ethics:** Was it acceptable to put participants through it?
- **Interpretation:** Do the results really support the lesson people draw from them?

A study can fail on one count and hold up on another. The popular retelling, though, tends to skip all three and go straight to the moral.

## Why a tidy lesson beats a messy truth

This part is my analysis, not the article's. A clean story travels faster than a qualified one. "Power corrupts ordinary people" fits on a slide, a poster, or a trailer. "The design had problems and the interpretation is disputed" does not.

A few things likely help the myth along:

1. **It flatters our curiosity.** We get to feel we've glimpsed something hidden about ourselves.
2. **It's dramatic.** A mock prison and an authority figure giving orders make for strong visuals, which is partly why they suit film and TV.
3. **It offers an excuse and a warning at once.** If situations make people cruel, we can explain bad behavior, and we can also resolve to resist it.

## What they still reveal

Discarding a study's conclusions doesn't mean discarding its question. Phys frames the enduring interest around how authority, group identity, and institutions shape behavior. Those forces are real topics of research and everyday life, even if one famous experiment is a shaky way to prove them.

The useful takeaway isn't "people are secretly cruel." It's that the question of *choice* matters. The article's emphasis is on people choosing harm when refusal was possible. That keeps responsibility in the picture instead of blaming the setting alone.

## Milgram and the phrase "just following orders"

Milgram's obedience studies are the other half of the story. They're tied in popular memory to the idea of "just following orders," the notion that people go along with harm because an authority tells them to.

Like the Stanford study, they carry both a powerful lesson and a set of critiques. Treat them the same way: take the question seriously, and treat the neat conclusion with caution.

## How to watch the Netflix series, or any retelling

When a dramatization of a famous study lands, you can keep your footing with a few quick checks:

- **Ask what was actually measured.** A vivid scene isn't the same as data.
- **Separate the story from the science.** The retelling may be gripping and still be incomplete.
- **Look for the critiques.** If a study is famous, there's usually a debate about it.
- **Notice the moral.** If the takeaway feels too neat, that's a cue to dig further.

## The takeaway

Next time a classic experiment is presented as proof of human nature, hold two thoughts at once: the study may be flawed, and the question behind it may still be worth asking. Then ask it of yourself: when refusing was possible, what would make you choose otherwise?
