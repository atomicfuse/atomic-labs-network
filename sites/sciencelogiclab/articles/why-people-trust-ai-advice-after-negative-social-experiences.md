Picture asking for dinner ideas for a 20-euro budget with a guest who can't eat gluten. You could ask a shop assistant, or you could ask an AI-powered tool like Hopla. New research, as reported by Phys, suggests the choice may depend on something you wouldn't expect: whether you've recently been treated unfairly.

By the end of this article, you'll understand why some people lean toward chatbots after discrimination, and why that comfort deserves a second look.

## The surprising finding: unfair treatment flips the usual script

Researchers have long described "algorithm aversion," the tendency to distrust automated advice and prefer a human. Per the Phys report, the new research suggests that for consumers who experience discrimination, this can turn into "algorithm appreciation."

In other words, the same person who might normally want a human opinion may reach for an AI assistant after being treated badly.

## Why AI feels safer: no judgment in the room

The explanation reported by Phys centers on embarrassment. After unfair treatment, a person may feel exposed or wary of being judged again. An AI recommendation feels more neutral and less judgmental than a human one.

Think about what that looks like in practice:

- A chatbot doesn't raise an eyebrow at your budget.
- It doesn't react to your accent, appearance, or name.
- You can ask a question you might feel awkward asking aloud, and nobody watches your face while you do.

The key point is that people may not choose AI because they think it is smarter. They may choose it because it feels like a lower-risk social situation. That's our reading of the finding, and it fits the emotional logic the research describes.

## Where this shows up

AI assistants are moving into shopping, banking, health care, and customer service, according to the Phys coverage. Those are all places where people can feel judged: about money, about their bodies, about whether they belong.

For someone who has already faced discrimination, an AI option in those settings can work like a quiet side door. It offers help without the possibility of another uncomfortable human encounter.

## The catch: feeling neutral isn't the same as being neutral

Here is where it pays to stay sharp. The research is about how AI *feels* to consumers. A recommendation can seem free of bias without actually being free of it.

Algorithms are built by people and can reflect the data and choices behind them. So the sense of safety is real as an experience, but it shouldn't be mistaken for proof of fairness. This is our analysis rather than a claim from the study, but it's the natural question to ask whenever a tool is trusted because it feels impartial.

## What this means for you

If you find yourself preferring a chatbot after a bad experience with a person, that's a common human response, not a flaw. A few practical habits help:

1. **Use AI as one input.** Treat its suggestions as a starting point, especially for money or health decisions.
2. **Check important answers.** Compare a recommendation against a second source before acting on it.
3. **Notice why you chose it.** Ask yourself whether you picked the AI because it's better for the task or because it felt easier to avoid people.
4. **Keep a human route open.** If something goes wrong, you want someone accountable to talk to.

## What this means for brands

Companies often pitch AI as convenient. This research suggests another reason customers may use it: to avoid social judgment. That raises a responsibility. If AI is becoming a refuge for people who have been treated unfairly, replacing human service entirely would remove the option for those who do want a person, and it would leave no one answerable when an algorithm gets something wrong.

The sensible approach is to offer AI alongside well-trained, accountable humans, not instead of them.

## The takeaway

Next time an AI assistant feels like the easier choice, pause for a moment and ask what is making it easy. If it's convenience, great. If it's relief from being judged, that's worth understanding, and worth double-checking the answer you get.
