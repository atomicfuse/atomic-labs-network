You probably know someone smart who believes something that doesn't hold up. Maybe you've been that person on some topic. By the end of this article, you'll understand why irrational ideas hold such appeal, and you'll have a few practical ways to respond to them, in others and in yourself.

The short version: these beliefs are rarely about missing facts. They're about needs. That's the core theme of a piece from Skeptic on the appeal of the irrational, which looks at pseudoscience, UFO claims and cult thinking and asks why they keep attracting people despite lacking strong evidence. Here is how that explanation plays out.

## Belief often answers an emotional need, not a factual one

The Skeptic piece centers on the psychological and social appeal of extraordinary explanations. Based on that framing, four needs show up again and again:

- **Certainty.** A clear answer feels better than an open question.
- **Belonging.** A shared belief comes with a community.
- **Wonder.** The idea that something amazing is out there is simply more exciting than a mundane explanation.
- **Distrust of institutions.** If official sources feel unreliable, alternatives feel more honest.

Notice that none of these is stupid. They're ordinary human wants. That's why intelligence offers limited protection: a bright person is just better at defending a conclusion they already wanted.

## Why pseudoscience can feel better than real science

Real science tends to come with hedges. "Probably," "the evidence suggests," and "we don't know yet" are honest phrases, but they're not very comforting.

Pseudoscience usually offers the opposite: a tidy story with a clear cause and a confident answer. Picture two explanations for a strange light in the sky. One says, "We need more data." The other says, "Here's exactly what it is." The second one feels satisfying even if the evidence behind it is thin.

This is a useful thing to know about yourself. When an explanation feels especially satisfying, that feeling is not evidence. It's worth asking whether you like the claim because it's well supported or because it ends the discomfort of not knowing.

## Why UFOs hold a special pull

UFO belief combines several of those needs at once. It offers wonder (we might not be alone), a bit of mystery that rewards curiosity, and often a sense that someone in authority is holding information back. That last piece taps the distrust of institutions that the Skeptic piece identifies as part of the appeal.

That doesn't mean every question about unexplained sightings is irrational. Curiosity is healthy. The trouble starts when "I can't identify this" quietly becomes "it must be extraordinary," a jump the evidence hasn't earned.

## How belonging can override skepticism

Cult thinking is the sharpest example of the social side. When a group gives you friendship, purpose and identity, questioning its core belief starts to cost you something real. Doubt stops being an intellectual exercise and becomes a risk to your relationships.

Online communities can create a milder version of the same dynamic. Fringe belief systems spread quickly in those spaces, and a feed full of people who agree with you makes a claim feel more established than it is.

A good self-check: if disagreeing with a claim would cost you friends, status or a sense of identity, your beliefs are being held up by more than evidence. That's a signal to slow down.

## A quick skeptic's checklist for any big claim

You don't need a science degree. Try these questions when something sounds amazing:

1. **What would change my mind?** If nothing could, it's not a conclusion, it's a commitment.
2. **Is there a simpler explanation?** Ordinary causes should be ruled out before extraordinary ones.
3. **How strong is the evidence, really?** One vivid story is not the same as repeated, checkable evidence.
4. **Who benefits from me believing this?** Sellers, followers and communities can all have a stake.
5. **How do I feel about it?** Strong excitement or relief is a cue to look harder, not less.

## Why debunking alone rarely works

If beliefs fill emotional needs, then handing someone a fact-check doesn't remove the need. They may feel attacked, and the group around them may pull them closer. The Skeptic piece's framing points the same way: understanding the appeal matters more than simply refuting the claim.

So if you want to talk with someone you care about, try this:

- **Start with the need.** Ask what draws them to the idea. Curiosity beats correction.
- **Skip the mockery.** Ridicule confirms the story that outsiders look down on them.
- **Offer something in return.** Real science has plenty of wonder, and real communities can offer belonging.
- **Model uncertainty.** Saying "I don't know, let's find out" shows that not knowing can be comfortable.

## Turn the question on yourself

The most useful move isn't spotting other people's blind spots. It's asking which of your own beliefs you hold because they feel good. Pick one this week and run it through the checklist above. If it survives, you'll believe it more firmly. If it doesn't, you'll have learned something, which is the whole point.
