You probably know someone smart who believes something that doesn't hold up. Maybe you've been that person on some topic. By the end of this article, you'll know why that happens, what these beliefs offer that facts often don't, and a few practical ways to respond without turning a conversation into a fight.

The starting point comes from a Skeptic piece on the appeal of the irrational, which looks at why pseudoscience, UFO claims and cult thinking keep attracting people despite weak evidence. Its focus is the psychological and social pull of extraordinary explanations. I'm working only from that framing here, so think of what follows as a guide to the main ideas rather than a tour of the studies.

## Irrational beliefs aren't about missing information

The common assumption is that people believe odd things because they haven't been told the facts. If that were true, a good fact-check would fix everything. It rarely does, because these beliefs are doing a job.

The emotional needs behind fringe beliefs tend to fall into four buckets:

- **Certainty:** a clear answer feels better than "we don't know yet."
- **Belonging:** a belief can come with a community that shares it.
- **Wonder:** the idea that something extraordinary is out there is thrilling.
- **Distrust of institutions:** if official sources feel unreliable, alternatives look more honest.

None of those needs is silly. They're human. That's why intelligence alone doesn't protect anyone: a smart person is simply better at building arguments for what they already feel drawn to.

## Why pseudoscience can feel better than uncertainty

Real science is comfortable saying "probably," "it depends" and "we're still testing that." That honesty is its strength, but it can feel unsatisfying.

Pseudoscience often offers the opposite: a single clean cause, a confident answer, a sense that you've been let in on something. Picture two explanations for a health problem. One says the research is mixed and individual results vary. The other says there's one hidden cause and one simple fix. The second one feels like relief, even if the first is more accurate.

The practical lesson: when an explanation feels *good*, treat that as a cue to check it, not as evidence it's true.

## The pull of UFO belief

UFO claims combine several of those needs at once. They offer wonder (we might not be alone), a sense of insider knowledge, and an explanation for mysterious sightings that otherwise stay unresolved. They also tie in with distrust of institutions: if authorities seem to be hiding something, the story gains emotional energy.

That's worth separating from the evidence question. The Skeptic piece's point is that these ideas persist despite lacking strong evidence, and that the appeal comes from what the stories give people. An unexplained light in the sky is a genuine unknown. Jumping from "unknown" to "alien spacecraft" is a leap, and the leap is where the emotional payoff lives.

A useful habit: let an unknown stay unknown. "I don't know what that was" is a complete and respectable conclusion.

## How cults use belonging to override doubt

Cult thinking shows the social side most clearly. When a group becomes your friends, your purpose and your identity, questioning the belief means risking all of that. Doubt stops being an intellectual exercise and becomes a social threat.

This helps explain why skepticism can lose to belonging. It's less about weighing evidence and more about asking what it would cost to leave. You don't need to be in a formal cult to feel a milder version of this. Online communities built around a shared belief can work similarly, rewarding agreement and making dissent uncomfortable.

A good self-check: could you disagree with your group on this topic and still be welcome? If not, pay attention to that.

## A quick skeptic's checklist

You can apply these questions to almost any extraordinary claim. They are my own practical suggestions, in the spirit of the skeptic approach, not a quoted list from the article.

1. **What would change my mind?** If nothing could, it's a commitment, not a conclusion.
2. **Is the evidence strong, or just exciting?** Stories and strong feelings aren't the same as data.
3. **What need does this belief meet for me?** Certainty, community, wonder? Naming it loosens its grip.
4. **Who benefits if I believe this?** Sometimes the answer is nobody. Sometimes it's someone selling something.
5. **Can I say "I don't know"?** If the alternative to the claim feels unbearable, that's a flag.

## Why debunking alone rarely changes minds

If a belief fills an emotional need, taking away the belief without addressing the need feels like a loss. Mockery makes it worse, because it adds social cost to changing your mind.

What tends to work better, as the piece's framing suggests, is understanding the need first. Ask what draws the person to the idea. Acknowledge the real part: wanting answers, wanting community, being curious about the universe. Then offer better routes to those same things, like real astronomy for wonder, or trustworthy communities for belonging.

That doesn't guarantee anyone will change their mind. But it keeps the conversation open, which is the only condition under which minds ever change.

## Why this matters more online

Fringe ideas spread quickly in online communities, which can supply belonging and certainty on demand. That makes media literacy a daily skill rather than a classroom topic. The same instincts that make a claim shareable, such as surprise, confidence and a clear villain, are often the ones worth doubting.

## The takeaway

Next time a claim thrills you or a friend's belief baffles you, ask one question before anything else: what does this idea give them, or me, besides an answer? Start there, stay curious, and you'll be harder to fool and easier to talk to.
