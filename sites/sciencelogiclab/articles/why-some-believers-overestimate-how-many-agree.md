If you've ever been baffled by someone who insists "everybody knows" a fringe theory is true, new research offers a clue. Per PsyPost, which reported the findings, people who believe conspiracy theories tend to overestimate how many others agree with them. By the end of this article, you'll know what that effect is called, why it keeps fringe beliefs feeling normal, and how to check your own sense of "what everyone thinks."

## What the research found

PsyPost reports that believers in conspiracy theories overestimate how widely their views are shared. The pattern points to what psychologists call a "false consensus" effect: the tendency to assume more people share your beliefs than actually do.

The reported implication is that this misjudgment may reinforce fringe beliefs by making them feel more socially accepted. A belief held by a small minority can feel like common knowledge if you think the majority quietly agrees.

That's the core finding. The rest of this piece explains why that mix-up is so easy to fall into, drawing on general reasoning about how it plays out rather than on additional study details.

## What "false consensus" actually means

False consensus is a mental shortcut. You know what you think, and your own view is the most vivid data you have. It's easy to treat it as a sample of public opinion: "If it seems obvious to me, it must seem obvious to most people."

This isn't unique to conspiracy believers. Most of us do a version of it, whether the topic is a favorite movie or a political opinion. What the research highlights is that it shows up in conspiracy thinking too, and that it matters there for a particular reason.

## Why it matters more for fringe beliefs

Beliefs held by few people usually come with a social cost. Friends push back, relatives roll their eyes. Those signals tell you that you're out of step.

False consensus dulls those signals. If you believe a large share of people secretly agree, the pushback feels like an exception rather than the rule. In our reading, that makes the belief feel safer to hold and easier to defend, which is how a minority view can feel like a silent majority.

## How online spaces can feed the illusion

The research is part of a bigger conversation. Conspiracy thinking remains a major force in politics, health, and online culture, and social platforms can amplify the sense that many people agree.

Here is a plausible way that works, offered as our analysis rather than a finding from the study:

- **You choose who you follow.** Over time, your feed fills with people who share your outlook.
- **Agreement is visible, disagreement is quiet.** Likes, shares, and replies from like-minded accounts are easy to see. The many people who scroll past a post leave no trace.
- **Loud voices look like big crowds.** A few active accounts can make a topic seem to be everywhere.

The result is an echo chamber that can be mistaken for public opinion. What you see is a sample of your own corner of the internet, not a poll of everyone.

## Why this helps explain how misinformation communities last

Understanding why believers feel validated helps explain how misinformation communities grow and persist. If members believe they're in the majority, they have less reason to doubt, and more reason to recruit, share, and stay.

It also reframes the problem. A believer may not simply lack facts. They may be working from a distorted picture of who else agrees. That's a different challenge from a missing piece of information.

## What this means for talking with believers

The research reported by PsyPost doesn't prescribe a conversation strategy, so treat the following as reasonable inference, not proven advice:

- **Skip "everyone thinks you're wrong."** It's likely to feel like an attack, and believers may simply not believe it.
- **Ask how they know others agree.** Where does that impression come from? Often the answer is a particular group or feed.
- **Separate the claim from the crowd.** A belief's popularity, real or imagined, says nothing about whether it's true.

## Check your own sense of consensus

Because false consensus is a general human habit, it's worth testing yourself. Try these quick steps:

1. **Name the source of your impression.** Is "everyone agrees" based on a survey, or on the people you talk to and follow?
2. **Look outside your feed.** Search for how people who disagree describe the issue.
3. **Guess, then verify.** Before looking up reliable polling on a topic, write down your estimate of how many people agree. Then compare.
4. **Notice the silent majority.** Remember that people who don't post are still people, and you don't know what they think.

## The takeaway

Feeling like "everyone knows" something is not evidence that everyone does. Before you trust that feeling, on any topic, ask where the crowd in your head actually came from.
