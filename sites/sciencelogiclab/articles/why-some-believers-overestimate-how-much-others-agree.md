If you've ever wondered why someone can hold a fringe belief with total confidence, new research offers a surprisingly ordinary answer. Per PsyPost, which reported the findings, people who believe conspiracy theories tend to overestimate how many other people agree with them. By the end of this article, you'll understand why that happens and how to spot the same trap in your own thinking.

## The finding: believers think they're in the majority

PsyPost reports that people who endorse conspiracy theories misjudge how widely their views are shared. They guess that more people agree than actually do. The pattern points to what psychologists call a "false consensus" effect.

That effect may help reinforce fringe beliefs. If a view feels socially accepted, it feels less fringe. And a belief that seems common is easier to hold onto, defend, and repeat.

I'm working from PsyPost's summary here, so details like sample sizes and methods are best checked in the original report.

## What "false consensus" actually means

False consensus is the habit of assuming other people think the way we do. It isn't unique to conspiracy believers. Most of us do a version of it, whether we're guessing how many people like our favorite movie or share our politics.

The research, as PsyPost describes it, suggests this ordinary mental shortcut shows up strongly around conspiracy beliefs. Here, the shortcut has a consequence: it can make an unusual belief feel like common knowledge.

Think of it as a mental mirror. You look out at the world, and what you see is partly a reflection of what's already in your head.

## Why it feels like "everyone knows"

The appeal is easy to understand. Belief is more comfortable when it comes with company. If you think millions of people see the same hidden truth, then disagreement from the people around you looks like a gap in their awareness rather than a problem with the idea.

This is my analysis, not a finding from the study, but it fits the pattern. Perceived agreement works like social proof. We use it constantly to decide what's reasonable, from restaurants to news stories. When that signal is inflated, the belief it supports feels sturdier than it is.

## Why online spaces make it worse

The coverage notes that conspiracy thinking remains a major force in politics, health, and online culture, and that social platforms can amplify perceived agreement. That amplification is the key link.

Here's how it plausibly works in practice:

- **You see what you engage with.** If you click on a topic, you tend to see more of it.
- **Like-minded people cluster.** Groups form around shared interests, so the voices you hear most often are the ones who agree.
- **Loud isn't the same as large.** A small group posting constantly can look like a crowd.

The result is an echo chamber that is easy to mistake for public opinion. Your feed becomes your sample of the world, and it's a lopsided one. These mechanisms are widely discussed explanations for the effect, not results reported from this particular study.

## Why this matters for misinformation

PsyPost's coverage frames the finding as a way to understand how misinformation communities grow and persist. If believers feel validated by imagined majorities, they may be less likely to question their views and more likely to share them confidently.

It also suggests a different approach to correcting false claims. Arguing over the facts of a single theory is one tactic. Another is addressing the sense that "everyone agrees." Showing that a belief is actually uncommon may take away part of its social support. Whether that works well is a question for further research; the reported study identifies the overestimation, not a proven fix.

## How to check your own sense of consensus

You don't have to believe in conspiracy theories to benefit from this. Anyone can fall for inflated agreement. A few quick habits help:

1. **Ask where your sample comes from.** Is your sense of "what people think" based on your feed, your friends, or broad surveys?
2. **Separate volume from numbers.** Many posts from a few accounts are not many people.
3. **Look for polling.** Well-run surveys of general populations beat your impression every time.
4. **Seek out a disagreeing source.** Notice how different the picture looks when you read people who see it another way.
5. **Notice the phrase "everyone knows."** It's often a sign someone is guessing at agreement rather than measuring it.

## The takeaway

The most useful lesson here isn't about other people. It's that our sense of how many others agree with us is a guess, and an easily skewed one. Before you treat a belief as common knowledge, find a real number.
