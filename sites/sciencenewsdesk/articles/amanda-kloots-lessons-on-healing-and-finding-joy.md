If you're grieving and waiting for the day it finally ends, this reframe may help: the goal isn't to get over loss. It's to build a life big enough to hold it next to something else, including joy.

That's the core message of Amanda Kloots' recent conversation on *The Future You*, recapped by Women's Health. Kloots, whose husband Nick Cordero died of COVID-19, talked about how grief reshaped her life and why she's launching a podcast called *Good Grief*. Per the recap, her point is that grief doesn't disappear. You learn to live with it while still making space for movement, community, love, and joy.

This piece takes one angle from that conversation: what it looks like to stop trying to "finish" grief and start making room around it.

## Why "getting over it" is the wrong target

Many of us absorb a quiet script: grief has stages, a timeline, and an exit. When months or years pass and the loss still hurts, it can feel like failing.

Kloots' framing, as relayed by Women's Health, pushes against that. Grief isn't a problem you solve and put away. It's something that stays with you, and your relationship to it changes.

That shift matters in practice. If the goal is "be done," every wave of sadness reads as a setback. If the goal is "live alongside it," a hard day is just a hard day.

The recap also points to a broader cultural move toward more honest, less linear portrayals of healing. Expect good stretches, bad ones, and no straight line between them.

## Joy after loss isn't betrayal

One of the heaviest parts of grief can be guilt: if I laugh, enjoy dinner, or feel excited about something, does that mean I've moved on from the person I lost?

The message of Kloots' story is no. Joy and grief can sit in the same life, and making room for one doesn't erase the other. You can miss someone deeply and still enjoy a good morning.

If guilt shows up when you feel happy, try treating it as part of grieving rather than evidence you're doing it wrong. You don't have to argue with it. You can notice it, let the good moment happen anyway, and keep going.

## Build room around grief with four things

The recap names four ingredients for making space: movement, community, love, and joy. You don't need all of them at once. Here's how you might start with each.

### Movement

Kloots is known as a multi-hyphenate with a fitness background, and movement is part of how she describes living with grief. You don't need a studio or a plan. A daily walk at the same time can become a small ritual: something predictable when much else isn't.

### Community

Grief can be isolating, especially when other people's lives seem to have moved on. Kloots' new podcast reflects the idea that talking about loss openly helps. Look for people who don't flinch at the topic: a friend who lets you repeat yourself, or a grief group.

### Love

Loss doesn't cancel your capacity to love; it often reveals it. That can mean leaning on family, friends, or children, or letting new relationships exist without treating them as a replacement for anyone.

### Joy

Start small and specific. A song you like, a meal you enjoy, a funny friend. Treat these as allowed, not earned.

## Survival mode versus learning to thrive

Early grief is often pure survival: get through the day, handle the logistics, stay upright. That's legitimate, and nobody should rush out of it.

The longer-term question is how you eventually move from surviving to living more fully. Kloots' story suggests that this isn't a switch you flip. It happens as you add things back in, one at a time: the walk, the call, the laugh, the new plan.

A useful check-in: instead of asking "Am I over it yet?", ask "What is one thing that makes today a little bigger than the loss?"

## Why therapy and grief communities help

The recap highlights the power of grief communities and therapy as part of living with loss. While the details of Kloots' own support aren't spelled out here, the broader idea is straightforward: you don't have to carry this alone, and being around people who understand can make feelings feel less strange.

If your grief feels like it's getting heavier rather than changing shape, or it's getting in the way of eating, sleeping, or functioning, reach out to a licensed therapist or your doctor. Asking for help isn't a sign you're grieving badly.

## Where to start this week

Pick one of the four ingredients and make it small enough to do in the next few days: a ten-minute walk, a text to someone who knew the person you lost, or ten minutes with a song you love. You're not trying to get over anything. You're making a little more room.
