Armies burn fuel, flatten cities and then help trigger enormous rebuilding efforts. Yet when countries tally their climate impact, much of that carbon often goes unreported. By the end of this article, you'll understand why that gap exists, why it matters, and what one expert says needs to change.

## The call: make military emissions reporting mandatory

Ahead of the U.N. Climate Change Conference (COP31), set for Antalya, Turkiye, in November 2026, an expert writing in The BMJ's climate issue argues that accurate data on greenhouse gas emissions during armed conflict is essential. Without it, per the report from Phys, militaries and governments can't be held accountable for war's climate impact.

The article points to large emissions from conflicts in Iran, Ukraine and Gaza, and calls for mandatory military emissions reporting.

## Why the data gap is the real problem

According to the report, military emissions remain largely voluntary and underreported. That is a blind spot in global climate policy: if a sector's emissions aren't counted, they can't be included in pledges, targets or enforcement.

Think of it like a household budget that leaves out one big bill. The totals look better than reality, and nobody can plan to fix what they can't see.

## Making the scale tangible

Numbers help here. The coverage draws a striking comparison: emissions from the war in Ukraine are described as equaling France's annual emissions. We can't independently verify that figure here, and it comes from the reporting on the BMJ piece, but it shows why the issue draws attention. War is not a rounding error in the climate ledger.

## Why this is surfacing now

Two things are driving the timing, per the report:

- **COP31 is approaching.** Pressure is growing to bring militaries into climate accountability frameworks.
- **A 2025 International Court of Justice opinion** affirmed governments' duty to protect the climate system. The argument follows naturally: if governments have that duty, the emissions of their armed forces are part of the picture.

It's our reading, not a claim from the report, that this legal backdrop gives data demands more weight than a purely voluntary approach ever did.

## What accountability would need

The central point is simple: accountability starts with measurement. Based on the argument described, that means:

1. **Mandatory reporting** rather than voluntary disclosure.
2. **Coverage of conflict-related emissions**, not only peacetime military operations.
3. **Inclusion in climate frameworks** so these numbers sit beside other sectors' totals.

The summary we have doesn't spell out the technical methods for measuring wartime emissions, so we won't guess at them. It's a detail worth watching as negotiators discuss the topic.

## What you can do with this

You can't audit an army from your couch, but you can read climate pledges with sharper eyes. When a country announces a national emissions target, ask whether military emissions are included. Then watch whether COP31 in Antalya treats military reporting as a side note or a requirement.

The takeaway: when you see a climate number, ask what was left out of it, because for war, the answer may be a lot.
