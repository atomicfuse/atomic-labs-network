If you write sustainability messages, or you're just curious why some eco-appeals stick and others slide past you, here's a useful finding: the order of your message matters. Research reported by Phys says green messages are more persuasive when they move from a negative environmental problem to a positive, achievable solution. By the end of this piece, you'll know what that sequence does to an audience and how to apply it.

## The finding in one sentence

Per Phys, the study shows that a message that starts with the environmental problem and then turns to a solution outperforms one that doesn't follow that arc. The researchers looked at tourism contexts, where travel brands, destinations, and platforms increasingly nudge people toward greener choices.

## What the sequence does, step by step

According to the Phys report, the effect unfolds in a particular order:

1. **Attention goes up first.** The negative opening, the problem, gets people to notice the message.
2. **Emotional evaluation improves.** Moving on to a positive, achievable solution shifts how people feel about what they've read.
3. **Willingness to act rises.** Better feelings and focused attention go along with a greater readiness to do something.

That's the useful part: each stage has a different job. The problem earns the attention, and the solution earns the action.

## Why this beats fear alone

Phys notes the study offers a way to make sustainability messaging more effective without relying on fear alone. That's a meaningful point. Messages that stop at the problem can leave people with a bad feeling and nowhere to put it. Our read: if the only emotion you offer is worry, the easiest response is to look away. The sequence gives the worry a destination.

The opposite failure is just as common. A purely feel-good message, with no problem named, has less reason to grab attention in the first place. The study's takeaway, as reported, is that you need both parts, in that order.

## How to apply it in your own messaging

The research was framed around tourism, but the structure is easy to borrow. Here's a practical way to use it.

### Start with one specific problem

Name the environmental issue plainly and briefly. You're buying attention, not delivering a lecture. Keep it to a sentence or two.

### Pivot quickly to something achievable

The solution should be positive and within reach of the person reading. The word "achievable" in the research summary is doing real work: a solution that feels huge or distant won't carry the emotional lift.

### Make the ask match the solution

If the solution is something a traveler can do at the moment of booking or arriving, put the prompt right there. Digital touchpoints are where much of this messaging now lives, per Phys, so the ask can sit in the same place as the message.

### A before-and-after illustration

This example is ours, not from the study. A weak version: "We care about the planet. Choose green options." A sequenced version: "Tourist crowds are wearing down this coastline. You can help by choosing a guided low-impact route that keeps the trail open for the next visitor." The second names a problem, then hands over a concrete, positive action.

## Who should care

Travel brands, destinations, and booking platforms are the obvious audience. But anyone communicating about sustainability, from managers to marketers to eco-conscious consumers trying to persuade friends, can use the same arc. The study itself centers on tourism, so how well the effect carries to other fields is something to test rather than assume.

## What we don't know

The summary we have doesn't give details like sample size, effect sizes, or how "willingness to act" was measured, and it doesn't say whether people actually changed behavior afterward. Treat the sequence as a well-supported starting point, then check it against your own audience.

## Your next move

Take one sustainability message you've written or seen lately and rewrite it in two beats: a brief, honest problem, then a positive step the reader can take today. Compare the two versions side by side and notice which one makes you want to act.
