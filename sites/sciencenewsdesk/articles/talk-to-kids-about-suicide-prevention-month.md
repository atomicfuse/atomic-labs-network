September is Suicide Prevention Month, and it offers a natural opening for a conversation many parents put off. By the end of this article, you'll have a plan for asking your kids direct questions, noticing when something seems off, and lining up support before you ever need it.

Per WTOP, which reported expert advice on the topic, parents can make a real difference, but only if they're willing to be proactive. The outlet cites a figure that should get your attention: 1 in 5 kids contemplates suicide at some point. That makes this a common concern, not a rare one, and a good reason to talk early.

## Ask directly, because directness can protect

The core message in WTOP's reporting is to ask direct questions. Many parents worry that raising suicide will plant the idea. The guidance here points the other way: open, plain conversation is part of prevention, and it signals that nothing is off limits at your kitchen table.

You don't need a perfect script. Some plain openers:

- "How have you been feeling lately, really?"
- "Have things ever felt so heavy that you thought about hurting yourself or not being here?"
- "If you ever felt that way, would you tell me? If not me, who?"

Keep your tone steady. If your child says yes, listen first. Stay calm, thank them for telling you, and don't rush to fix everything in that moment. Then get professional help involved. If you believe your child is in immediate danger, contact emergency services or a crisis line right away. In the US, you can call or text 988.

## Ask about their online life, too

WTOP's coverage highlights the pressures of social media, deepfakes, chatbots, and other technology. Kids spend a lot of their lives in these spaces, so a check-in about mood should include a check-in about screens.

Try questions that invite stories rather than one-word answers:

- What are you seeing online that makes you feel good, and what makes you feel worse?
- Has anyone shared or made something fake or embarrassing about you or someone you know?
- Do you ever talk to a chatbot or app when you're upset? What's that like?

Approach these with curiosity, not suspicion. If your child fears you'll confiscate their phone the moment they confess a problem, they'll stop telling you about problems. Make it clear that coming to you is safe.

## Notice when something feels "off" without overreacting

You know your child's baseline better than anyone. The practical move is to watch for changes from that baseline and follow up on them rather than waiting for certainty.

A workable approach:

1. **Name what you've noticed, without judgment.** "You've seemed quieter this week, and I wanted to check in."
2. **Ask one question, then listen.** Resist stacking five questions in a row.
3. **Come back later.** If they brush you off, that's not the end. A second, low-pressure check-in a day or two later tells them the offer stands.
4. **Act on your gut.** If the unease persists, contact a pediatrician, school counselor, or mental health professional.

One caution from me, not the experts: a single bad day isn't a crisis, but a lingering pattern deserves attention. You can take something seriously without treating it as an emergency.

## Build your support network before a crisis

WTOP's reporting stresses building support ahead of time. In a hard moment, you don't want to be searching for phone numbers. Spend an evening on this now:

- Save 988 in your phone and show your teen where it is.
- Find out who your child's pediatrician is and whether they can help with mental health concerns or referrals.
- Learn who the school counselor is and how to reach them.
- Identify one or two trusted adults, such as a relative, coach, or family friend, whom your child likes and could confide in.
- Tell those adults you're keeping an eye on your child's well-being, so you can compare notes if worry arises.

This list takes less than an hour to assemble, and it turns a frightening situation into one where you already know the next step.

## Help kids find connection beyond technology

Since so much of kids' social world now runs through screens, one protective step is making sure they also have strong in-person ties. That doesn't require banning devices. It means making room for the alternatives:

- Regular family meals or walks where phones are away.
- Sports, clubs, music, volunteering, or any activity where your child sees the same people consistently.
- One-on-one time with you that isn't tied to chores or grades.

The goal is for your child to have several places where they feel known, so that no single app, feed, or chatbot becomes their main source of comfort.

## Make it a habit, not a one-time talk

Suicide Prevention Month is a handy reminder, but one conversation won't cover it. Short, regular check-ins work better than a single dramatic sit-down. Car rides and bedtime are good moments because they feel low-pressure.

This week, pick one question from the lists above and ask it. Then save 988 in your phone. Those two small steps put you ahead of the crisis, which is exactly where the experts say parents should be.
