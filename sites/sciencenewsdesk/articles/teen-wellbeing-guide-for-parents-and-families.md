If your teenager has asked for help with their mental health and come away without a diagnosis, new research suggests you are far from alone. It also points to what you can do about it: understand where the gap sits, and prepare for the assessment step that often decides what care comes next.

## What the study found

A study from King's College London, reported by Medicalxpress, estimates that fewer than one in 15 south London 17-year-olds who are thought to have a mental disorder received a diagnosis from NHS mental health services.

There is some progress. Per the report, the share of young people with a diagnosis rose from 2.6% in 2009 to 4.4% in 2024. But the study describes major gaps, especially for girls and for young people from Black, Asian, mixed or other ethnic backgrounds.

The summary available to us doesn't include details such as sample size, the specific conditions counted, or how the researchers estimated who has a disorder. So treat the headline figure as a signal about scale, not a precise count for any one family.

## Why asking for help isn't the same as being assessed

The study highlights a bottleneck that is easy to miss. Plenty of attention goes to whether teens ask for help. Less goes to what happens once they are inside the system. Per the findings, even many teens who seek help are not getting the diagnosis needed to access disorder-specific treatment.

That distinction matters. A conversation with a GP or a school counsellor is a first step. A formal assessment is what can name a condition and, in many cases, open the door to treatment designed for it.

The research also lands amid concern that demand for youth mental health care continues to outpace NHS capacity, alongside renewed calls to invest in assessment and diagnosis. Our reading: when capacity is stretched, assessment is the step where teens are most likely to wait, or to fall through.

## Who is most likely to be missed

The researchers flag two groups with the largest gaps.

- **Girls.** The study reports that girls are among those with the biggest shortfalls in diagnosis.
- **Young people from Black, Asian, mixed or other ethnic backgrounds.** These teens also show major gaps, per the report.

The summary doesn't explain why, and we won't guess at causes. What it does tell parents is practical: if your child is in one of these groups, it is reasonable to be more persistent, not less, about getting a proper assessment.

## Why a diagnosis can matter

A diagnosis isn't a label for its own sake. According to the study's framing, it is often the gateway to disorder-specific treatment. Without it, a teen may get general support at best, which can be valuable but may not match what their condition needs.

It can also make needs visible to others. When a teen's difficulties are seen but never formally identified, families and schools are left to improvise. A clear assessment gives everyone a shared starting point.

## What you can do if your teen asks for help and gets no diagnosis

The study doesn't offer a parent's checklist, so what follows is practical suggestion from us, not findings from the research. Check any step against current NHS guidance or your own GP.

### Keep a simple record

Write down what you and your teen notice: sleep changes, mood, school attendance, withdrawal, anxiety episodes, with dates. Specifics are easier for a clinician to act on than "they've been struggling."

### Ask directly about assessment

At appointments, ask whether your teen can be referred for a formal assessment, and what the next step is. If the answer is no, ask why, and what would change it.

### Ask what happens while you wait

If there is a waiting list, ask what support is available in the meantime and who to contact if things get worse.

### Bring in the school

Teachers and pastoral staff often see patterns you don't. Their observations can add to your record, and the school may be able to provide support while formal processes catch up.

### Don't treat "no diagnosis" as "no problem"

Given the study's estimate that most teens with a disorder in this area lack a diagnosis, an absent label is not proof that nothing is wrong. If your concern persists, keep raising it.

## What the findings can't tell you

This is a study of south London 17-year-olds, so the exact numbers may not map onto other regions. And a diagnosis rate is not a measure of how many teens are suffering or how well they are supported. The 4.4% figure for 2024 tells you how many were diagnosed, not how many needed help.

## The takeaway

If your teen has reached out and nothing has been formally identified, ask for the assessment by name, keep notes, and keep asking. The research suggests the gap is not in teens speaking up. It is in what happens next.
