Giant manta rays can grow up to 30 feet long and 4,409 pounds. They don't start that way. A new study in *Frontiers in Fish Science*, reported by Popular Science, shows how baby giant manta rays (*Mobula birostris*) in Central America survive their small years: they stay close to the coast, in shallow water where sharks and orcas are less of a problem.

By the end of this piece, you'll know how researchers worked this out, why size changes everything for an ocean animal, and why the findings point to a new way of protecting these endangered rays.

## Small bodies, big predators

The open ocean is full of sharks, and orcas (*Orcinus orca*) hunt there too. Manta pups, called pups, can't rely on size to protect them.

Adults can. According to study co-author Jeffry Madrigal-Mesén, project leader of Mantas Costa Rica, "Mature adults can rely on their massive body size and their ability to dive deep to evade threats. But juveniles don't have that luxury."

So the young rays pick a different strategy: avoid the danger zone altogether.

## What the shallows offer a growing ray

The team found that young mantas, defined here as those with a body of about 10 feet or less, stick to shallow coastal refuges. Per the report, these spots offer several things at once:

- A safe place that doesn't overlap with predators' hunting grounds
- Plenty of food
- Places to hide
- Cleaning services from reefs

That combination makes the coast a kind of natural nursery. It's a place to eat, grow and stay out of trouble until the ray is big enough for the open ocean.

The loyalty is striking. Madrigal-Mesén said the researchers see the same individuals more than once a year, and that some were recently sighted again at the exact same tagging location. "Juveniles remained strictly within shallow nearshore waters that serve as coastal refuges," he said in a statement.

## How researchers pieced it together

Between 2018 and 2025, the team tracked and identified mantas at five locations using three methods:

- **Acoustic telemetry**, which uses underwater sound pulses to monitor fish movements. It tracked 59 individual rays.
- **Satellite telemetry**, where transmitters track movement and behavior from a distance. It followed large-scale movements of 12 individuals.
- **Photo identification**, which helped identify individual rays.

Together they produced a dataset of more than 12,000 detections. Madrigal-Mesén said, "If we hadn't used all three technologies together, we simply wouldn't have been able to piece this puzzle together."

## Growing up means leaving

The story changes for adults. Of the mature mantas in the study, nine went on expansive migrations along the continental shelf break. Madrigal-Mesén described a "dramatic shift in space use as they mature," and said most tracked adults made long-distance transboundary migrations through Central America.

Still, adults don't simply abandon the coast. One male nicknamed Mickey Mouse, for a spot pattern on his belly, was unusually approachable for a manta and let the team tag him several times over several years. In 2020 he logged 462 acoustic detections at a coastal hub called El Secreto. In 2023 he returned to the same shelf and stayed 38 days.

The researchers read this as continued site loyalty. Madrigal-Mesén explained that nearshore cleaning stations "act as primary social hubs where courtship and reproductive interactions take place," and that returning to a predictable coastal spot lets mature rays handle social and cleaning needs before long regional migrations.

One caveat: Mickey Mouse is a single animal, so he's best read as an illustration of the pattern the team describes rather than proof of it.

## The protection gap

Here's where the science becomes a conservation problem. Coastal areas are often legally protected marine reserves. The open ocean generally isn't. Once adult mantas leave the coast, they lose that legal protection and face industrial fisheries and vessel strikes, per the study team.

Their conclusion: marine protected areas alone aren't enough for a species this mobile as it swims across Central America.

## What "conservation swimways" would change

The researchers suggest multinational "conservation swimways." The idea is to link the domestic coastal refuges where juveniles live with the migratory corridors adults use. Protecting one end of a ray's life doesn't help much if the other end is exposed, and a migration that crosses several countries needs protection that crosses them too.

The team says this approach could help keep the endangered rays safe. It's a proposal, not an established policy, but the tracking data gives it a concrete footing.

## What this tells you about wildlife protection

The broader lesson reaches beyond mantas. Animals don't use one habitat for their whole lives, and a reserve that works for a juvenile may do nothing for an adult. Madrigal-Mesén put it this way: "manta rays' lifestyle isn't a one-size-fits-all behavior."

If you want to act on that, the most useful step is to look for conservation efforts that cover a species' whole life cycle and route, not just one patch of water. And if you ever dive or snorkel near a coastal reef where mantas gather, remember that you may be visiting a nursery. Keep your distance and give the rays room to feed and get cleaned.
