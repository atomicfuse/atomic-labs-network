The African Cup of Nations is going back to where it started. Per the BBC, Caf has reversed its December decision to stage Afcon every four years, and the tournament will return to its traditional two-year cycle after a review of that plan.

That's the headline. The more useful question for you as a fan is why a governing body would abandon a major scheduling change within months, and what the reversal says about where African football sits in a crowded global calendar. This piece focuses on that: the pressure points behind the U-turn.

## What actually changed

The facts, as reported by the BBC, are short:

- In December, Caf decided Afcon would be held every four years instead of every two.
- A review of that decision followed.
- Caf has now restored the biennial format.

The BBC's report also covers the revenue and scheduling factors behind the reversal, along with a confirmed host timeline for upcoming editions running from 2027 to 2033. Check the BBC's original report for the full list of hosts and dates, since those details are best taken straight from the source.

## Why the four-year plan was always going to be contentious

A four-year cycle has an obvious logic on paper. Afcon sits awkwardly in the football calendar, and spacing it out would put it in line with other major continental showpieces and ease pressure on schedules.

But the plan ran into a hard reality: Afcon is the centrepiece of African football's commercial and emotional life. Halve how often it happens and you halve the number of times federations, broadcasters and sponsors get that moment.

The BBC's reporting points to revenue and scheduling as the factors behind the U-turn. Our read, and it is only that, is that the two pulled in opposite directions. Scheduling relief argued for fewer tournaments. Revenue argued for more. Revenue appears to have won.

## The business case for every two years

Think about who depends on Afcon arriving every other year.

### Federations

For many national associations, a major tournament is one of the most significant income events they have. A gap of four years between editions means a long wait between those payouts and between those moments of national attention.

### Broadcasters and sponsors

Commercial partners buy into rhythm. A tournament that returns every two years gives them repeat inventory and regular fan engagement. A four-year gap makes the product rarer, which can add prestige, but it also thins out the calendar of events they can sell around.

### Fans

For supporters, there's a simpler argument: tradition. A two-year cycle means a generation of players can realistically feature in several tournaments, and fans get more than a handful of chances to see their country compete for the continent's top prize.

## The pressure Afcon still faces

The reversal doesn't make the scheduling problem disappear. The tournament's place in the global calendar remains under pressure from three directions: European club seasons, the expanded Club World Cup, and the 2030 World Cup.

### European club seasons

Afcon pulls African players away from their clubs, many of them in Europe, in the middle of a season. That's the long-running source of friction between clubs and national teams. Keeping a two-year cycle means that friction returns every other year rather than every fourth.

### An expanded Club World Cup

A bigger Club World Cup adds more fixtures and more demands on the same elite players. The more crowded the calendar becomes, the harder it is to find space for any continental tournament, let alone one held twice as often.

### The 2030 World Cup

With the 2030 World Cup also on the horizon, every governing body is competing for the same finite window of player availability. Afcon's biennial return puts it right in the middle of that negotiation.

## What it means for players and clubs

For players abroad, the two-year cycle means a predictable disruption: a mid-season trip to the continental finals, often with a long return journey and a congested fixture list afterward. For clubs, it means planning squads around absences every other year.

There's a flip side. A player who loses out on one tournament through injury or form doesn't have to wait four years for another shot. For ambitious young players trying to break into national squads, that matters.

The review that led to this decision presumably weighed these workload concerns. The outcome suggests Caf judged the cost to the African game of fewer tournaments as greater than the cost of keeping the calendar tight. That's our interpretation, not a stated Caf position.

## Afcon versus new competitions

Proposals for other competitions, such as an African Nations League, have been floated as ways to reshape the continental calendar. The logic of those ideas is that they could add structure and value without relying entirely on one flagship event.

The U-turn raises an interesting question: if Afcon stays biennial, where would any new competition fit? Squeezing another tournament into an already stretched calendar would only worsen the workload issue that made a four-year Afcon attractive in the first place. For now, the answer appears to be that Afcon's value is hard to replace.

## What to watch next

Three things will show whether this decision holds up:

1. **The host timeline.** The confirmed hosts for editions between 2027 and 2033 give fans and federations a long runway to plan around. Read the BBC's report for the specifics.
2. **Club reaction.** European clubs have long grumbled about releasing players. Watch whether the return to two years revives that debate.
3. **Calendar negotiations.** With the Club World Cup and 2030 World Cup in the mix, expect continued pressure on where Afcon sits within the football year.

## The takeaway

If you follow African football, treat this as a reset rather than a resolution: Afcon is staying on its familiar rhythm, but the fight over its place in the calendar is not over. Mark the confirmed 2027 to 2033 host timeline, and keep an eye on how clubs and federations respond as the global schedule keeps filling up.
