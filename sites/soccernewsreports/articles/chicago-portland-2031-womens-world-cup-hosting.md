Chicago said no to hosting men's World Cup matches in 2026. Now it wants in on the 2031 Women's World Cup. That's the headline from the New York Times, which also reports that Portland has entered the race to host games at the tournament.

The details are thin so far, and this piece sticks to what has been reported. But the story raises a question worth working through if you follow the game: why might a city skip the men's tournament and chase the women's one?

## What has been reported

Per the New York Times, two U.S. cities are positioning themselves to host matches at the 2031 Women's World Cup:

- **Chicago**, which declined to participate in the men's tournament, wants to host games.
- **Portland** has also entered the race.

The race to host 2031 games is only beginning to take shape. That means no host-city lists, stadium plans or budgets are part of this reporting, and nothing here should be read as a confirmed selection. Interest is not an award.

## Why the Chicago reversal stands out

Saying no to one World Cup and then raising a hand for another looks like a contradiction at first. It isn't necessarily one. The two events are different propositions, and cities weigh them on cost, visibility and legacy benefits.

The reporting doesn't spell out Chicago's reasoning, so any explanation here is analysis, not fact. Still, the pattern invites a few reasonable questions:

- Does the women's tournament carry a different cost profile than the men's event?
- Does it offer a different kind of visibility, one that fits a city's goals better?
- Is the legacy case, meaning what the city is left with afterward, easier to make?

We don't know which of these, if any, drove Chicago's thinking. It's fair to note that the city's change of tone says something about how hosting is being judged: as a calculation, not a trophy.

## What hosting a match means for a city

When U.S. cities weigh major soccer events, the trade-offs tend to fall into three buckets, and they match the ones framing this story.

### Costs

Hosting means committing resources. Cities look hard at what they would be asked to spend and what they would get back. For a city that has already walked away from one tournament, this math clearly matters.

### Visibility

Matches put a city in front of a global audience. A tournament stop is a chance to show off a stadium, a downtown and a fan base.

### Legacy

The lasting benefit is often the hardest to measure and the most persuasive in a pitch. Think of what remains after the final whistle: infrastructure, momentum for local soccer, and a stronger relationship with fans.
