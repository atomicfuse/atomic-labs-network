In a wellness world that sometimes seems to demand a cold plunge, a sunrise run and a color-coded supplement drawer before breakfast, Pilates instructor Bryony Basse has a refreshingly low-drama thesis. In a "day in the life" piece as told to Rose Beer and reported by AOL, Basse says transformation doesn't come from reinventing yourself. It comes, in her words, from "building trust with yourself through small things you say you will do and then actually do."

That's the headline. The rest of her day is the evidence.

## A Morning Built Around a Nine-Month-Old

Basse wakes between 6am and 7am. She has a nine-month-old baby, so, she says, she has "learnt to embrace flexibility." She uses an alarm but keeps her phone away from the bed and never snoozes.

First up: tongue scraping and brushing her teeth, then something warm to drink. She says she's a fan of Chinese medicine and avoids cold drinks. Most mornings she journals, looking back at the previous day and writing down what she's visualising, plus one thing she'll do for her mind and one for her body.

It's hard not to notice the structure there. Even her journaling is a tiny two-item to-do list, one mind promise and one body promise. That's the whole philosophy in miniature, before she's even eaten.

## Food Without the Rules

Basse says she doesn't fast or follow a restrictive diet. At home she likes eating "quite monotonously" during the day because it removes some decision-making, then is more flexible at dinner. She doesn't believe in foods being "off limits," and she has dark chocolate every day.

It's a small detail with a nice logic: spend your decisions where they're fun. Lunch on autopilot, dinner on adventure.

## Pilates and Weights, Both on the Calendar

Her movement is scheduled because she's fitting it around a baby and running a business. She does four Pilates by Bryony classes a week and two weight-training sessions at the gym.

Why both? Per Basse, Pilates gives her strength, control, mobility and mind-body connection, while gym sessions let her work with heavier loads. She calls movement her number-one tool for managing stress and mental fatigue, and says that when she's very in her head, getting into her body changes her state "incredibly quickly."

## Recovery Without the Gadget Aisle

Basse is a big believer in rest. She loves saunas and cold water, and sea swims when she can (cold water makes her feel mentally clear), but she says recovery doesn't have to involve lots of expensive tools. Her foundations: walking, sleep, gentle movement and a proper rest day.

That's a useful corrective in a category that can feel like a shopping cart with a pulse. The sauna is a bonus; the rest day is the plan.

## The Nerdier Side of Her Routine

There's some curiosity-driven tinkering too, all attributed to Basse:

- **Cycle awareness:** Since having her daughter, she's become more interested in how her energy and mood shift through her cycle. She recently did a DUTCH hormone test to explore it, which made her more curious about spotting patterns.
- **Meditation and visualisation:** She meditates daily and is particularly interested in visualisation, which she uses like mental rehearsal. If something coming up makes her nervous, she mentally walks through it beforehand.
- **Creatine:** She takes five grams daily, initially for strength and recovery, and notes there's emerging research around creatine and cognitive function.
- **Blue Zones:** She's fascinated by research on them, and points out that many fundamentals of healthy ageing are "remarkably simple": movement, food, purpose, connection and a sense of belonging.

Note the pattern: she follows curiosity, but frames wellness as something that "can become complicated and commercialised." Hormone tests and creatine sit next to walking and sleep rather than replacing them.

## Winding Down

Basse starts winding down around 10pm and tries to fall asleep soon after. The household dims the lights, lights candles and puts on a calming playlist. She might have mint tea and read, stays off her phone for 30 to 60 minutes before bed, and takes magnesium.

## The Real Takeaway: Promises Small Enough to Keep

Here's where the day-in-the-life format earns its keep. Basse says most people don't need a complete life overhaul. They need "one or two promises to themselves that are small enough to keep." Her examples: 10 minutes of Pilates in your pyjamas before work, or putting your phone away for the first 20 minutes of the morning.

Once you trust yourself to do that, she says, build from there: "Consistency builds evidence, and that evidence builds self-belief."

There's something quietly subversive about that. The usual January-style pitch is that you're one dramatic reinvention away from your best self. Basse's version is almost boring in the best way: keep a tiny promise, then another. Her own day, with a baby, a business, six workouts a week and a daily square of dark chocolate, suggests the system works because it bends. Flexibility isn't a flaw in the routine. It's the routine.

And if the 20-minute phone-free morning feels like too much? Start with the tongue scraper. Nobody ever failed a wellness journey by starting too small.
