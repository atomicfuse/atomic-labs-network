European governments that publicly condemn the Taliban's human rights record are quietly seeking its help to send Afghan refugees back to Afghanistan, according to a New York Times report. The Times' central argument is that the Taliban is using Europe's anti-migration pressure to gain legitimacy and diplomatic leverage.

This is a story about a contradiction, and about who benefits when a government decides it needs a favor from a regime it officially criticizes.

## The Awkward Handshake

The Times reports that the countries in question are the same ones that criticize the Taliban government over its human rights abuses. Those same governments are engaging with the Taliban to make deportations of Afghan refugees possible.

Deportations are logistical work. They require someone on the receiving end to cooperate. That need is what puts the Taliban in a position it has not typically enjoyed: being asked for help by governments that have kept it at arm's length.

## Why Europe Is Asking

The Times frames the backdrop as a political one. Migration has become a defining issue across Europe, pushing governments toward tougher deportation policies, even when those policies collide with stated human rights values.

It's hard not to read that as a pressure cooker. When voters treat migration as the issue that decides elections, a government's stated principles about a foreign regime can start to look negotiable. That is our interpretation, not a finding from the report, but it fits the tension the Times describes: domestic politics pulling one way, refugee protection pulling the other.

## The Leverage Flip

Here is the angle worth sitting with. In ordinary diplomacy, a government seeking recognition is the one asking for something. In the arrangement the Times describes, the dynamic runs the other way. Europe wants deportations to happen, and the Taliban holds the key to making them workable.

The Times argues that this gives the Taliban legitimacy and diplomatic leverage. Even a purely practical conversation about deportation logistics is still a conversation, and it is one the Taliban might otherwise struggle to get. We can't say from the reporting what the Taliban has asked for in return, and we won't guess. The structure of the exchange is the point: whoever is needed gains bargaining power.

## Rhetoric Meets Paperwork

Politically, there is a neat trick available here. A government can keep up the language of condemnation while its officials handle the practical talks elsewhere. The Times describes the result as governments that criticize the Taliban's record yet seek its help, which is a tension rather than a secret scandal. The condemnation and the cooperation are both real, and they sit awkwardly side by side.

That awkwardness matters because refugees are the people in the middle. These are Afghans who left a country governed by the very authorities now being asked to help receive them back. Anyone reading about deportation deals should keep that human fact in view, even when the coverage is mostly about diplomacy and who holds leverage.

## What to Watch

The piece raises questions that deserve follow-up as the story develops:

- Does engagement on deportations quietly become broader engagement with the Taliban government?
- Do European governments keep their public criticism at the same volume while the talks continue?
- What happens to the Afghans who are sent back?

The Times' reporting does not hand us the answers in the material available here, so these remain open questions rather than conclusions.

## The Sharpest Takeaway

Foreign policy is supposed to flow from values and interests together. The Times' account suggests that fear of migration is increasingly doing the steering, and that the regime Europe condemns has noticed. When a government needs a favor badly enough, its critics can become its counterparties. Whether Europe has fully priced in what that costs is the question this story leaves hanging.
