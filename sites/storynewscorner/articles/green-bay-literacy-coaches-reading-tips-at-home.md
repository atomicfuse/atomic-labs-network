Literacy coaches in Green Bay have a message for parents: you don't need a teaching degree to raise a stronger reader. Per a report from Fox11online, the coaches are urging families to build reading comprehension at home through daily reading, conversation, routines, and library visits. The article also stresses that literacy starts before a child ever walks into a classroom.

## Why the Timing Matters

The report points to a sobering number: nearly 40% of U.S. fourth graders read below basic level. That's the kind of statistic that sends parents scrolling for answers at 11 p.m.

The coaches' answer is refreshingly unglamorous. No app subscription, no flashcard empire. Simple habits, repeated, build vocabulary, oral language, and confidence, per the report. Studies cited in the piece show that reading daily can also help strengthen critical thinking and writing.

Holiday breaks, the article notes, are a timely window to keep kids engaged with reading without making it feel like schoolwork. That framing is the smartest part. Anyone who has watched a child's face fall at the word "assignment" knows the trick is to make reading feel like something other than homework.

## The Angle: Boring Habits Beat Clever Hacks

Here's my take: the most interesting thing about this advice is how little of it is novel. It's the parenting equivalent of "drink water and sleep more." It's easy to nod along and then do nothing. The real story is the consistency, and the coaches' emphasis on routines suggests that's the point.

### Make It a Routine

The coaches recommend daily reading as a routine rather than an occasional event. A fixed slot, like after dinner or before bed, takes the negotiation out of it. It's hard not to see the logic: a habit you don't have to decide on every day is a habit that survives a chaotic Tuesday.

### Talk About What You Read

Conversation sits right alongside reading in the coaches' advice. Comprehension isn't just decoding words on a page; it's being able to say what happened and what you think about it. Pausing to chat about a story builds the oral language the coaches say underpins reading success.

A few low-pressure ways to do that (these are my suggestions, not the coaches' quotes):

- Ask what a child thinks will happen next.
- Ask why a character made a particular choice.
- Ask what they'd have done instead.

None of these have a wrong answer, which is exactly why kids tend to enjoy them.

### Use the Library

Library visits made the coaches' list, too. A library card is, if you think about it, a free pass to unlimited new plot lines. Letting kids pick their own books is a cheap way to give them a stake in what they read.

## Literacy Starts Before School

One of the report's quieter points is that reading skills begin well before kindergarten. Talking, listening, and sharing stories with young children all feed the vocabulary and oral language that later support reading. For parents of toddlers who feel they're "not doing anything yet," that's reassuring: chatting through bath time counts.

## Keep It Light Over the Break

With holidays approaching, the pressure to turn time off into a catch-up campaign is real. The coaches' framing points the other way. Keep the reading, drop the school-ish vibe. Read a favorite book for the fifth time. Read a recipe together. Read the cereal box if that's what gets a reluctant kid engaged. (Those examples are mine, but the spirit, simple habits over structured lessons, comes straight from the advice reported.)

## The Takeaway

No single bedtime story will move a national statistic. But the Green Bay coaches are betting on something that scales one living room at a time: a parent, a kid, a book, and a conversation afterward. It's not flashy, and that may be the best argument for it.
