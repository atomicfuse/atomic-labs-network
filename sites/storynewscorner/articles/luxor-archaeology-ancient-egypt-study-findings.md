Picture a mummy. Odds are you pictured a royal, arms crossed, resting in a gleaming decorated sarcophagus, probably with a curse attached. A new study suggests the real picture was messier, more varied, and a lot more thoughtful.

Per Popular Science, a team from Spain's University of La Laguna has published a study in *Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology* on chamber three of tomb TT 209 in the Theban Necropolis, not far from Luxor. The chamber holds at least 20 ancient Egyptians and one dog, with burials spanning more than 300 years. The necropolis, according to the report, contains over 400 burial sites.

The headline finding is not just the headcount. It's the researchers' argument that this crowded space was not a free-for-all.

## The "Messy Tomb" Theory Gets a Rewrite

A packed tomb with bodies stacked on bodies looks, at first glance, like a place where nobody was minding the store. Earlier theories, per the report, suggested some Egyptian burial sites grew more chaotic as they filled up.

The La Laguna team disagrees. Study co-author Jared Carballo-Pérez said in a statement: "The detailed study of the bodies indicates neither chaotic accumulation nor a loss of the original ritual over time." He described instead "a spatial logic in which each new deposition was adapted to the presence of previous bodies—a kind of funerary memory in the processes of reuse."

He also said that "deposits that were previously considered 'disordered' can be reinterpreted." It's the archaeological version of realizing the "messy" desk actually has a filing system.

## How They Read the Chamber

TT 209 was first constructed during the 25th Dynasty (754–656 BCE), also known as the Nubian or Kushite Empire. The team excavated the tomb, then photographed and sketched its occupants, describing each mummy's orientation and position within the structure. In total, they documented nine separate funerary deposits containing single and group burials, along with personal objects and religious items.

The timeline, per the report, shows space inside the chamber becoming a premium over the generations. Anyone who has ever fought for a parking spot near a popular venue may feel a flicker of kinship.

## From Coffins and Amulets to Standing Room

The earliest burials look like the pop-culture version. Egyptologist and co-author Miguel Ángel Molinero Polo explained: "The earliest individuals buried here appear to have been of high social standing, as they were interred in coffins, with funerary nets and amulets, and with an assemblage containing costly items."

Then things change. By what the report describes as the Persian period, mummies were placed wherever space was available, which sometimes meant different limb positions. Instead of individual interments, almost two-thirds of TT 209's deceased were arranged vertically.

That could read as a decline in standards. The researchers say no. Many later mummies still had arms folded over their chests in what's known as the Osirian position. Molinero Polo noted that the vertically stacked bodies "did not have coffins but were carefully mummified," and can be associated with upright jars "that point to a specific ritual not found in the earlier phases of the tomb's use."

It's hard not to see this as a story about adaptation rather than decay: fewer coffins, new rituals, same care. The format changed. The intent, according to the team, did not.

## About That Dog

Every good archaeology story needs a detail that makes people stop scrolling, and this one has it. In one instance, a mummified dog was placed directly on top of a man and a woman. The study's figure caption notes the canid (M9) rests over the lower limbs of two closely superimposed individuals.

What that means is an open question, and the researchers are careful. Carballo-Pérez said: "I find it quite beautiful. This could indicate a close relationship between these human individuals and the animal, an idea that I think is quite easy to empathize with."

Note the "could." The study doesn't claim to know the dog's story. But anyone who has ever had a pet claim the best spot on the bed will find the interpretation hard to argue with, even if it remains an interpretation.

## Why the Mummy Image Needs an Update

The Egyptian civilization, as the report points out, lasted over 3,000 years, so the royal-in-a-sarcophagus image captures just one slice of it. Movies and museum gift shops have a way of freezing that slice into the definition of "mummy." TT 209 offers a corrective in a single chamber: high-status coffin burials, vertical group arrangements, ritual jars, and a loyal-looking dog, all within roughly 300 years.

The practical upside is that archaeologists don't have to hop between sites to see how funerary rites shifted over generations. One tomb near Luxor does the job.

The larger takeaway belongs to Carballo-Pérez's phrase, "funerary memory." Each new burial in TT 209 was fitted around the people already there, as if the living kept checking in with the dead before adding to the household. A crowded tomb, it turns out, may be less a sign of forgetting than of remembering very carefully.
