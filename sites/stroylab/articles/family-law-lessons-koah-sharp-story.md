## Red flags in co-parenting that deserve to be taken seriously

The reported mix of alleged abuse, custody tension, and threatening texts is a pattern safety advocates commonly urge people to treat as a package, not as separate incidents. A few practical rules:

- **Treat ominous messages as evidence, not drama.** Screenshot them, back them up somewhere the other parent cannot access, and note dates.
- **Look at the trend, not the single event.** One angry argument is different from a steady rise in control, threats, or fixation on the child.
- **Notice when custody becomes leverage.** If access to the child is tied to your behavior, your relationship decisions, or your silence, that is a concern.
- **Don't let past abuse be reframed as past tense.** Alleged abuse before a split is relevant context when custody is being discussed.

## Why separation can be a high-risk period

Domestic violence advocates have long warned that leaving or limiting contact can be a moment when control tactics intensify, because the other person may feel they are losing power. The takeaway is practical: the period around a breakup, a custody filing, or a change in visitation deserves extra planning, not less.

## Safety planning you can start today

If you share a child with someone you fear, these steps are common advice from advocacy organizations:

1. **Document everything.** Keep threats, texts, call logs, and any incident reports in one secure place.
2. **Tell people who can act.** Family, friends, employers, and childcare providers should know who is and is not authorized to pick up your child.
3. **Bring the history to the court.** Alleged abuse and threats should be shared with your attorney or a court advocate so custody decisions are made with the full picture.
4. **Plan exchanges carefully.** Use public places, a third party, or supervised exchange options where available.
5. **Call a hotline.** In the U.S., the National Domestic Violence Hotline is at 1-800-799-7233, and 911 is the number for immediate danger.

## The role of family and friends

People around a custody dispute often hold real influence. If someone you love is describing threats, fixation on the child, or pressure to reconcile, believe them and ask what help looks like to them. Don't push them to confront the other parent. Offer to keep records, hold a spare set of documents, or sit in on a lawyer's meeting.

It's also worth being skeptical of the phrase "I just want us to be a family." It can be sincere. It can also be a pressure tactic when it arrives alongside threats or control. The context decides which it is.
