An essay republished by Thetruthseeker, written by Mike Adams, argues that global elites are running a deliberate "depopulation race" against the clock: kill off large numbers of people before an inevitable financial collapse sparks a revolt. By the end of this piece, you will know exactly what the essay claims, why that kind of story grabs people, and a practical checklist for testing any theory that looks like it.

This is a look at the argument, not an endorsement. The essay makes grave claims about mass killing, and nothing in the material we reviewed offers evidence that meets that weight.

## What the essay actually claims

Per the piece, Adams argues the following:

- Global elites, labeled "globalists," are coordinating a deliberate depopulation effort.
- The tools, he says, include mRNA vaccines, engineered famines, and bioweapons.
- He frames mRNA injections as biological weapons.
- He predicts economic collapse and asset seizure.
- The elites' plan, in his telling, is to depopulate, reset, enslave, and automate, but a growing number of people are "awakening," so revolution may erupt before the plan rolls out.
- His recommended response centers on precious metals, natural health, and decentralized communities.

Those are his assertions. The summary of his piece we worked from does not include documents, data, or named evidence supporting the central claim of a coordinated plan to kill people. That absence matters, and it is the first thing to look for in any article like this.

## Why the story feels convincing

The essay stitches several separate fears into one narrative: vaccines, food prices, economic fragility, and automation. Each fear has some real-world anchor. Prices rise. Wealth is unevenly distributed. Many people worry about AI replacing jobs. Institutions have lost trust with a lot of people.

A single villain who explains all of it is emotionally easier to hold than a messy reality of unrelated causes. That is our analysis, not Adams's claim, but it is a pattern worth recognizing. When a theory explains everything, including future events that haven't happened, it is often because it can't be proven wrong.

Timing helps too. Theories like this gain traction when economic uncertainty and automation worries rise, which is the climate the essay speaks to.

## Where the argument breaks down

### The bioweapon claim needs evidence, not framing

Calling mRNA injections biological weapons is a label, not a finding. A real case would require clinical trial data, mortality statistics compared against baselines, and independent replication. The essay as summarized leans on assertion. If you want to test the claim, look up the actual trial publications and population-level mortality data from health agencies and judge whether the numbers support a deliberate killing program. Don't take the essay's word, or ours.

### Unfalsifiable by design

The essay says the plan may fail because people are waking up. That structure is convenient: if the predicted collapse comes, the theory looks right; if it doesn't, the plan was thwarted. A claim that survives every outcome tells you nothing about the world.

### Coordination is the hardest part

A secret plan spanning governments, drug companies, and food systems would require enormous numbers of people to keep quiet and act in sync. The essay frames this as a perfect plan that works "flawlessly" on paper. Real institutions rarely coordinate that well on anything, and that is a fair question to put to the author.

## Follow the money in the solution

The essay's prescription is worth a look on its own. Precious metals, natural health products, and decentralized community preparation are things that can be sold. We can't say from this material what Adams sells or earns, so check that yourself on his own site. But as a general test, ask whether the person describing the catastrophe also profits from your fear of it. That doesn't make every claim false. It does mean you should hold the claim to a higher standard.

## The legitimate worries underneath

You don't need depopulation theory to take real concerns seriously:

- **Wealth inequality** is measurable and debated through public data.
- **Automation and AI** are changing work, and economists argue about the pace and the policy response.
- **Economic fragility** is a fair topic for anyone who follows debt, inflation, and banking.
- **Institutional trust** is a genuine problem when agencies communicate poorly.

Each can be examined on its merits without a hidden extermination plot. Bundling them into one conspiracy tends to make each worry harder to discuss sensibly.

## A five-question checklist for any theory like this

1. **What is the specific, checkable claim?** "They are killing us" is vague. Demand numbers, dates, and documents.
2. **Who else would have to be in on it?** Count the people required to keep the secret.
3. **What would prove it wrong?** If nothing could, be wary.
4. **Does the author profit from the fear?** Look for courses, supplements, or products attached.
5. **Do independent sources outside that ecosystem confirm it?** Echoes within the same circle are not confirmation.

## The takeaway

When an essay tells you a hidden group is racing to kill you before you can fight back, treat the urgency as a reason to slow down. Run the five questions, read the primary data yourself, and put your energy toward the real economic and health issues you can verify.
