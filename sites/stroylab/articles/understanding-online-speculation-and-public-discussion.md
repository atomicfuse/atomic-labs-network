Boston.com recently asked a pointed question about the Lindsay Clancy case: she doesn't deny she killed her children, so why are online conspiracy theories spreading like wildfire? By the end of this piece, you'll have a practical way to recognize how speculation takes hold around painful cases, and a few habits for keeping yourself out of it.

This is a case involving the deaths of children, so it deserves a sober approach. We won't rehash the crime. The more useful story, and the one Boston.com's piece points toward, is the media ecosystem around it.

## The Gap That Theories Rush Into

Per Boston.com, the central puzzle is a gap between three things: the legal facts, the public's emotional reaction, and what circulates on social media. Where those three don't line up, speculation moves in.

That's the pattern worth learning. When a case is settled on the question of what happened but unsettled on the deeper question of why, people look for an explanation big enough to match the horror. A theory can feel more proportionate to the tragedy than the plain facts do. That's our analysis, not a finding from the reporting, but it explains a lot about how these narratives form.

## Why Simple Explanations Feel Unsatisfying

True crime audiences are practiced at suspicion. They've watched cases where the first story was wrong, and that experience can harden into a reflex: if an account seems too tidy, assume something is hidden.

That instinct is sometimes healthy. Applied to every case, it turns into a machine that generates doubt regardless of evidence. Emotionally difficult cases are especially vulnerable, because accepting a hard, ordinary-seeming explanation means sitting with something painful that has no villain behind the curtain.

## Narratives Form Faster Than Verification

High-profile true crime cases increasingly become internet battlegrounds where stories take shape faster than verified information arrives. A confirmed fact takes time: filings, hearings, documented records. A post takes seconds.

By the time a court record surfaces, a theory may already have thousands of shares and a community invested in it. Correcting it then feels, to its believers, like part of the cover-up.

## What This Case Touches

The story sits on several nerves at once: mental health, motherhood, and online mob behavior. Each of those draws strong opinions and personal experience, which makes calm discussion harder. People bring their own fears and histories to the case, and speculation becomes a way of processing them. That's understandable, but it's a poor basis for claims about real people.

## A Quick Test Before You Share

You don't need to be an investigator to avoid feeding misinformation. Try these checks:

1. **Find the primary source.** Is the claim tied to a court document, an official statement, or a named outlet? Or only to "people are saying"?
2. **Separate what's established from what's inferred.** Someone's possible motive or hidden involvement is a guess unless it's documented.
3. **Notice the emotional payoff.** If a post makes you feel certain and outraged within seconds, slow down.
4. **Ask who gains from the share.** Engagement rewards confident claims, not accurate ones.
5. **Wait for the process.** Social media shapes public perception well before any trial. Don't let it settle your view in advance.

## The Ethics of Covering Family Tragedies Online

There are real people on every side of this story, including family members and others who knew the children. Speculation doesn't stay in an abstract comment thread; it can land on those people. Content built on rumor around a child-homicide case can compound harm for those already grieving.

If you cover or discuss cases like this, a reasonable standard is to attribute what's reported, label what's opinion, and skip theories that can't be supported.

## The Takeaway

When a case has a known outcome but a painful, unanswered "why," treat your urge to fill that gap with a theory as a signal to pause. Stick to what's documented, and let the legal process do the work that social media can't.
