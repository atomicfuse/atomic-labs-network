You can fill a pantry and still miss the vitamins and minerals your body needs. That gap, between having enough food and having a nutritionally adequate diet, is the focus of a qualitative study led by EUFIC, reported by Medicalxpress. By the end of this piece, you'll know the specific barriers the researchers identified in Italy and Poland, and how to spot them in your own community or kitchen.

## The core finding: food security is not nutrition security

Per the report, vulnerable consumers in Italy and Poland face overlapping obstacles to diets rich in micronutrients. The study's central takeaway is simple: food access alone does not guarantee nutritional adequacy.

This matters because "hidden hunger," the shortfall in vitamins and minerals, can exist alongside a full stomach. As food prices, migration pressures and inequality strain European households, it is becoming a public health issue that goes beyond counting calories, according to the study's framing.

## The barriers, one by one

The study points to six areas that stack on top of each other. Each one alone is manageable. Together, they make "just eat more fruit and vegetables" unrealistic.

### Cost

Price is the obvious barrier, and the study's title says it plainly: this goes beyond food prices. Cost is real, but it is only one item on the list.

### Transport

Fresh, varied food often means getting to a shop that sells it. If you can't reach one easily, the nearest option may be a convenience store with a narrow range.

### Cooking access

Raw ingredients only help if you can prepare them. The study identifies access to cooking as a barrier, which is easy to overlook if you've always had a working kitchen.

### Limits of food assistance

Food aid keeps people fed, but the researchers flag its limits when it comes to nutritional quality and variety. What gets donated or distributed may not cover the micronutrients people need.

### Cultural fit

Food that doesn't match what someone knows, trusts or traditionally eats is less likely to be eaten, even when it is nutritious. This is especially relevant for refugees and migrants, who are among the vulnerable groups in the discussion.

### Confusing nutrition information

Labels, guidelines and campaigns can be hard to interpret. The study lists confusing nutrition information as a barrier in its own right, not just a side issue.

## Why awareness campaigns fall short

The study argues that awareness campaigns are not enough without practical, culturally sensitive support systems. Our read: telling someone what to eat is the cheapest part of public health. It only works when the person can afford it, reach it, cook it and recognize it as food they want to eat.

## What this means for food aid and community programs

The study's conclusions point toward support that deals with the whole chain, not only the shopping basket. If you volunteer with, donate to or help run a food program, these questions follow directly from the findings:

- **Is the food culturally familiar** to the people receiving it?
- **Can recipients cook it?** If not, are there ready-to-eat or minimal-prep options?
- **Is there variety**, not just volume?
- **Is the nutrition guidance clear** and usable, rather than another set of confusing labels?

These are our practical applications of the research, not recommendations the study itself lists.

## What you can do at home

If you're on a tight budget yourself, or cooking for someone who is, the study's logic suggests starting with the barrier that's actually in your way instead of a generic goal like "eat healthier." Is it transport? Look for delivery or a shop on your regular route. Is it kitchen access? Prioritize foods that need little or no cooking. Is it familiarity? Build around dishes you already know and add nutritious ingredients to them.

The smallest useful step: name your one biggest barrier, then solve that before anything else.

## The takeaway

Next time you hear "just eat more fruit and vegetables," ask what's stopping the person from doing it. The answer is rarely willpower, and the fix is rarely a poster.
