Canned food has a reputation problem, and it's mostly undeserved. By the end of this guide, you'll know which canned staples are worth a permanent spot on your shelf, what to check on the label, and how to turn them into a quick, flavorful dinner with a nod to some classic regional dishes.

Per Health, canned foods can be nutritious, affordable and convenient, and the outlet highlights lentils, beans, sardines and fruit among its picks. Its full list names six foods, but the details we have cover four, so those are the four we'll focus on here.

## Why the can deserves a second look

Health points to three things that make canned foods useful: they're shelf-stable, cost-effective and easy. That combination matters if you want to cook more at home without a daily shopping trip, or if you're trying to waste less food. A can in the cupboard doesn't wilt in the back of the fridge.

Grocery prices remain a concern for many households, which is part of why pantry-based cooking keeps gaining attention. A few well-chosen cans give you a starting point for a meal on nights when fresh ingredients have run out.

## Canned beans: the all-purpose base

Beans are the easiest place to start. They're already cooked, so you can go from can to plate in minutes. Think of the dishes built around them in kitchens around the world: Tuscan-style beans with olive oil and herbs, chili, or a simple bean salad.

### How to use them

- Warm them with garlic, olive oil and a pinch of chili, then spoon over toast.
- Toss them cold with chopped onion, lemon juice and parsley.
- Stir them into soup near the end of cooking so they hold their shape.

A quick rinse and drain is a common habit with canned beans, and it helps you control the salty liquid they sit in.

## Canned lentils: faster than the dried kind

Dried lentils are already quick, but canned ones skip cooking entirely. That makes them handy for weeknight versions of dishes with deep regional roots, like lentil soups and warm lentil salads.

### A simple lentil salad

1. Drain and rinse one can of lentils.
2. Mix with diced cucumber, tomato and red onion.
3. Dress with olive oil, lemon juice, salt and pepper.
4. Finish with fresh herbs if you have them.

Serve it as a side, or pile it onto flatbread for lunch.

## Canned sardines: small fish, big flavor

Sardines are the item most people hesitate over, and they're the one most worth trying. Tinned fish has a long place in food traditions, especially around the Mediterranean, where it shows up on bread, in pasta and alongside simple salads.

### Easy ways in

- Mash them with lemon juice and a little mustard for a toast topping.
- Toss them through hot pasta with garlic, olive oil, chili flakes and parsley.
- Serve them straight from the tin with crackers, pickles and sliced tomato.

If you're new to sardines, start with the pasta. Heat and lemon soften the flavor and make it more approachable.

## Canned fruit: useful, if you read the label

Canned fruit is handy for breakfasts, snacks and quick desserts. The thing to watch is what it's packed in. Syrup adds sugar, so check the label and look for fruit packed without added sugar. Health's guidance centers on fruit as a convenient option, and the label is where you make it a good one.

### Ways to use it

- Spoon over plain yogurt or oatmeal.
- Chop and stir into a quick fruit salsa for grilled fish or chicken.
- Blend into a smoothie when fresh fruit isn't around.

## How to read a can before you buy

The same label check works across all four foods.

- **Sodium:** Compare brands and look for lower-sodium versions of beans and lentils when you can. Draining and rinsing also helps.
- **Added sugar:** Look for fruit without added sugar, and skim the ingredient list for anything you didn't expect.
- **Ingredients:** Short is usually better. Beans, lentils and sardines need little more than the food itself, water or oil, and salt.

## A five-minute pantry supper

Here's a simple recipe that uses two of the cans above, inspired by Mediterranean tinned-fish traditions.

### Sardine and white bean toast

1. Drain and rinse half a can of white beans.
2. Warm them in a pan with a spoonful of olive oil, one minced garlic clove and a pinch of salt.
3. Lightly mash about half the beans so the mix turns creamy.
4. Toast two thick slices of bread.
5. Spoon the beans over the toast, top with sardines, and finish with lemon juice and black pepper.

It takes about as long as the toast does, and it covers protein, fiber-rich beans and a bit of acidity for balance.

## Build your shelf slowly

You don't need to buy all four at once. Pick one can you haven't cooked with before, check the label for sodium or added sugar, and make it your dinner this week. Once you've found a version you like, restock it before it runs out.
