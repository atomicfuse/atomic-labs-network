A tomato that never reaches the market never reaches anyone's plate. By the end of this article, you'll understand where much of the world's food disappears between field and kitchen, which fixes are reported to help farmers most, and what you can do as an eater to back a less wasteful food system.

## The Headline Number: One-Third of Food Is Lost or Wasted

An explainer originally reported by Phys opens with a striking figure: roughly one-third of food is lost or wasted. The piece focuses on postharvest losses in the Global South, the food that spoils or gets damaged after it is picked but before anyone eats it.

According to the article, these losses hit cereals, vegetables, fruits, roots and tubers at high rates when nothing is done to prevent them. That list covers a lot of what fills a typical market stall: grains, leafy greens, mangoes, cassava, potatoes and yams.

The core argument is simple. Food security is not only about growing more. It also depends on keeping what farmers already grow in good condition long enough to be sold and eaten.

## Why Growing More Isn't the Whole Answer

It is natural to think hunger is solved by bigger harvests. Phys's summary of the issue pushes back: if harvests keep spoiling, extra production simply creates extra waste. A farmer who grows more but loses a large share on the way to market gains little.

This matters more now, as climate change, high food prices and resource constraints put pressure on agriculture. Cutting losses is presented as one of the fastest ways to strengthen food systems, because the food, land, water and labor have already been spent. Governments and funders are also rethinking food security beyond yield increases alone, per the report.

## Where Food Gets Lost: The Weak Links After Harvest

The article points to several stages where improvement is needed. Think of them as a chain, and any weak link costs food.

### Storage

Grain, produce and tubers all spoil differently, but all need protection from the conditions that damage them. Better storage is one of the main areas the article says needs investment.

### Transport and roads

Poor roads and gaps in cold storage are named as drivers of food waste. A fragile crop that spends too long on a rough journey without cooling arrives in worse shape, or not at all. This is a useful lens when you picture a market: the freshness you see on a stall depends on infrastructure you never see.

### Processing

Turning surplus crops into products that last longer, such as dried or milled foods, is part of the toolkit the article describes. Processing gives farmers another outlet when fresh sales are slow.

### Measurement

You can't fix what you don't measure. The piece lists measurement alongside storage and transport as an area for improvement, since reliable data on where and how much food is lost helps target solutions.

### Finance and farmer support

Even good tools are out of reach without money and training. The article says finance and support for farmers are part of the answer, not an afterthought.

## Low-Tech, Affordable Fixes Count

One encouraging theme is that simple infrastructure and affordable technologies can save food, raise farmer income and improve climate resilience. The solution is not always a high-end facility. Often it is a matter of practical tools and steady access to them.

The report for this topic also stresses that women farmers should be included in postharvest innovation. That means designing tools, training and financing with them in mind, so the people doing much of the work can use and benefit from improvements.

## Why This Belongs on a Food Lover's Radar

Regional dishes depend on ingredients that arrive in good condition. The cassava in a stew, the dried grain in a porridge, the fruit at a street stall: each relies on someone getting the harvest from field to cook intact. Understanding the postharvest chain gives you a fuller picture of why local food markets look and taste the way they do.

## What You Can Do as an Eater

The article is aimed at policy and development, so it doesn't hand consumers a checklist. These steps are my own practical takeaways, not claims from the report:

- **Waste less at home.** Plan meals, store produce properly and use leftovers. The one-third figure includes waste as well as loss.
- **Buy imperfect produce when you can.** Bruised or oddly shaped fruit is still food.
- **Preserve surplus.** Freezing, drying, pickling and cooking in batches all extend the life of ingredients.
- **Be curious about supply chains.** Ask vendors and producers how food gets to market.
- **Support groups working on farmer storage, transport and finance** if that issue matters to you.

## A Simple Way to Rescue Produce: Quick Vegetable Stew

In the spirit of saving food, here is a flexible stew that uses produce nearing the end of its life. It is a general recipe, not tied to a specific region.

**You'll need:** 1 onion, 2 cloves garlic, 2 tablespoons oil, 3 to 4 cups chopped vegetables (tomatoes, roots, tubers, greens), 1 teaspoon spice of your choice, 3 cups water or stock, salt.

1. Soften the chopped onion in oil for about 5 minutes.
2. Add garlic and spice, stir for 1 minute.
3. Add firm vegetables such as roots or tubers first, then the water or stock. Simmer about 15 minutes.
4. Add tomatoes and any greens. Cook 5 to 10 minutes more until everything is tender.
5. Season with salt and serve with grain or bread.

## The Takeaway

Before you buy more, make full use of what you already have, and pay attention to the unseen systems that carry food from field to plate. Saving a harvest can matter as much as growing one.
