Rice is one of the most widely eaten staples on earth, and plenty of home cooks still end up with a pot that's gummy, clumpy, or somehow both soggy and undercooked. According to Fox News, chefs say the fix is usually small: a skipped rinse, one method used for every kind of rice, too much stirring, or fluffing too hard. By the end of this guide, you'll know which steps to apply to which rice, and which to skip.

One caveat up front: the details below stay at the level the chefs' advice supports. Exact water ratios and timings vary by variety and brand, so treat the package directions as your starting point and adjust from there.

## Start with the step most people skip: the rinse

The headline advice in the Fox News report is that rinsing basmati and jasmine rice before cooking is the key to fluffy, distinct grains that don't clump together in the pot. Chefs point to rinsing as the step many home cooks leave out.

A simple way to do it:

1. Put the rice in a bowl or fine-mesh strainer.
2. Run cold water over it, swishing the grains with your hand.
3. Drain and repeat until the water looks noticeably clearer.
4. Drain well before it goes in the pot, so you aren't throwing off your water measurement.

The "until clearer" cue is my practical shorthand, not a chef-specified number of rinses. Let the water, not a count, tell you when you're done.

## Match the method to the rice

The broader lesson from the chefs is that rice isn't one thing. Rinsing, soaking, water ratios, resting, and fluffing all depend on the variety and on the texture you want.

### Basmati and jasmine: rinse for separate grains

These are the two varieties the chefs single out. If you want light, individual grains, rinsing is where to begin. Soaking is another step that can matter, depending on the variety and the result you're after.

### Rice meant to be sticky or creamy: think twice

The chefs' point is that you shouldn't apply one routine to everything. If the goal is a softer, clingier texture, a step designed to keep grains separate may work against you. Decide on the texture first, then choose your steps. Check guidance for the specific variety you're cooking, since the report frames rinsing and soaking as variety-dependent rather than universal.

## Measure your water on purpose

Water ratio is on the chefs' list of things that change by rice type. That means the "2 to 1" rule some of us grew up with isn't a law. A few habits help:

- Use the ratio printed on your rice's packaging as a baseline.
- Measure with the same cup every time, so changes are easy to trace.
- Remember that well-drained rinsed rice carries a little extra moisture. Adjust slightly only if your results keep coming out wet.
- Change one thing per batch. If you alter the rinse, the water, and the heat at once, you won't know what worked.

## Leave the lid and the spoon alone

Stirring during cooking is one of the mistakes the chefs flag as a way to ruin texture. The practical takeaway: once the rice is simmering, resist the urge to poke at it. Each time you stir, you're working the grains, and that's the opposite of what you want if you're after distinct, fluffy rice.

## Let it rest

Resting is also part of the chefs' advice. When the cooking time is up, don't rush to serve. Take the pot off the heat, keep the lid on, and give the rice a few minutes to settle. The report doesn't give a specific rest time here, so check your rice's directions, but build the pause into your dinner timing so it doesn't feel optional.

## Fluff gently

The last mistake on the list is fluffing too aggressively. Use a fork or a rice paddle and lift the grains rather than mashing or stirring them. Think of it as separating, not mixing. Rough handling can break grains and leave you with the clumps you worked to avoid.

## Troubleshooting: what your pot is telling you

Here's a quick diagnostic that connects each common problem to the mistakes the chefs name. These are my own readings of that advice, not additional claims from the report.

- **Gummy or clumpy:** Did you skip the rinse? Did you stir while it cooked or fluff roughly afterward?
- **Undercooked or hard in the center:** Revisit the water ratio for that specific variety, and consider whether soaking would suit it.
- **Wet at the bottom, dry on top:** Skipping the rest may be the culprit.
- **Great one week, poor the next:** Check whether you used a method meant for a different type of rice.

## A weekend rice test worth running

If you want to see the difference for yourself, cook two small batches of basmati or jasmine side by side: one unrinsed, one rinsed until the water runs clearer. Keep everything else identical, including the pot, the water ratio, and the heat. Rest both the same way, fluff gently, and compare. It's a cheap experiment, and it tells you more than any rule can.

## The takeaway

Next time you make basmati or jasmine, rinse it first, leave it unstirred while it cooks, let it rest, and fluff with a light hand. Then change one variable at a time until the pot matches the texture you want.
