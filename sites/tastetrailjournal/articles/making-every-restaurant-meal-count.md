Fewer Americans are eating out every week than at any point in Gallup's polling. A new Gallup poll, as reported by Fox News, found that just 39% of U.S. adults dined at a restaurant at least once a week in 2026, a record low and a sharp drop from pre-pandemic levels. Middle- and upper-income adults reportedly cut back the most.

That doesn't mean people have stopped caring about restaurants. Fox News frames it as a shift toward being more selective, driven by value, cost, safety concerns and changing habits. If you're part of that shift, this guide is for you: a practical way to decide when a meal out is worth it, and how to make the ones you choose count.

## What the Numbers Say (and Don't)

Here is what the reporting supports:

- 39% of adults now eat at a restaurant weekly, a record low in Gallup's polling.
- That figure is well below pre-pandemic levels.
- Middle- and upper-income adults showed the biggest pullback.
- Reasons cited include value, costs, safety concerns and changing habits.

The takeaway is that dining out is moving from routine to occasion. A weekly default is becoming a "treat yourself" meal. That's our reading of the trend, but it's a useful lens: if you eat out less often, each meal should earn its place.

## Treat Dining Out as an Occasion, Not a Default

When restaurant meals are rarer, choose them on purpose. A simple filter helps:

1. **Is it something you can't easily make at home?** Wood-fired flatbreads, regional specialties with long preparation, or dishes built on hard-to-find ingredients justify the trip.
2. **Is the setting part of the point?** A market stall, a family-run counter or a place tied to a local tradition gives you more than the plate alone.
3. **Would you be happy with this price for this food?** If the answer is a shrug, cook instead.

## Where Local Food Still Delivers Value

Because the trend is about value, the most satisfying places are often the ones doing one thing well. Look for:

- **Food markets and food halls**, where you can sample several small portions rather than commit to one large entree.
- **Street food and counter-service spots**, which tend to focus on a short menu of regional specialties.
- **Lunch menus**, which at many restaurants offer a smaller version of dinner favorites. Check the menus near you, since pricing varies by place.
- **Neighborhood restaurants rooted in a specific culinary tradition**, where a single signature dish tells you what the kitchen cares about.

When you travel, the same logic applies. Build a day around one sit-down meal you're excited about and fill the rest with markets and street stalls.

## Cook the Restaurant Dish at Home

Part of eating out less is bringing the flavor home. A good way to do it is to take a restaurant favorite and make a simple version. Here's an approachable one inspired by classic Italian trattoria cooking.

### Weeknight Garlic and Chili Spaghetti (Aglio e Olio)

**Serves 2. About 20 minutes.**

- 200 g (7 oz) spaghetti
- 4 cloves garlic, thinly sliced
- 1/3 cup olive oil
- 1/2 teaspoon red pepper flakes
- Salt
- Chopped fresh parsley
- Grated hard cheese, optional

**Steps:**

1. Boil the spaghetti in well-salted water until just shy of tender. Save a cup of the pasta water before draining.
2. While it cooks, warm the olive oil in a wide pan over low heat. Add the garlic and cook gently until pale gold, not brown.
3. Stir in the pepper flakes for 20 seconds.
4. Add the pasta and a splash of pasta water. Toss over medium heat for a minute or two until the sauce turns glossy and clings.
5. Finish with parsley and cheese if you like.

The garlic is the key. Golden is sweet; brown is bitter. Keep the heat low and you'll get a dish that tastes like a restaurant plate from pantry staples.

## Is Cooking at Home Always Cheaper?

Often, but not automatically. Gallup's findings point to cost as a driver, and the cost of ingredients matters too. Home cooking saves the most when you use what you already have, build meals around a few versatile staples and avoid buying a long list of one-off ingredients for a single recipe. A restaurant meal can be the better deal when it replaces a complicated dish that would need a dozen specialty items.

## What This Means for Restaurants

Fox News reports that restaurants are already facing higher food and supply costs, so a drop in weekly visits adds pressure. The places likely to hold on to customers are the ones that make each visit feel worth it: clear prices, honest portions and food with a real point of view. That's analysis, not a finding from the poll, but it matches what diners seem to be asking for.

## Make the Next Meal Out Count

Before you book or walk in, name the one dish or experience you're going for. If you can't, cook the aglio e olio and save the restaurant night for something you'll remember.
