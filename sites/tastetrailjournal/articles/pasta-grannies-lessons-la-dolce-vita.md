La dolce vita often gets sold as a look: a spritz on a terrace, a linen shirt, a golden-hour piazza. The women behind Pasta Grannies offer a more practical version. By the end of this piece, you'll have four everyday habits drawn from their approach, plus a sense of what to try first if you want to cook like them.

## Who the Pasta Grannies Are

Per Vogue, Vicky Bennison is the author and creator of Pasta Grannies, a project that documents Italian nonne who keep handmade pasta traditions alive. The older women at the center of it share their techniques and, along the way, a good deal of life wisdom.

Bennison has a new cookbook, *Eat Everything*, and Vogue's profile includes a recipe from a grandmother named Rosa: cicatelli con ciambutella. More on that below.

The appeal, as Vogue frames it, is a counterweight to hyper-optimized wellness culture. Interest in slow living, heritage cooking, longevity and low-waste food is growing, and these cooks show what that looks like without a program or a product attached.

## Habit 1: Cook With What You Have

The title of Bennison's new book, *Eat Everything*, points to a no-waste mindset. You can read it as a simple kitchen rule: start from what's already in the house rather than from a shopping list built around a recipe.

Try this in practice:

- Before shopping, look at what needs using up first.
- Let that decide the meal, not the other way around.
- Treat odds and ends (the heel of a vegetable, the last of a bunch of greens) as ingredients, not leftovers.

That's my interpretation of the idea, not a rule quoted from the book, but it fits the spirit of the title.

## Habit 2: Learn One Handmade Shape

Handmade pasta traditions are the heart of the project, and Bennison's work exists in part because those traditions are held by older cooks and can fade if they aren't passed on. You don't need to master dozens of shapes. Pick one.

Rosa's cicatelli is a good candidate, since it's a regional shape made by hand rather than with specialist equipment. Start with the recipe in Vogue's feature or in *Eat Everything*, and make it more than once. Repetition is how these skills have always moved between generations.

## Habit 3: Keep Cooking a Social Act

The Pasta Grannies project is built on women sharing knowledge: with Bennison, with viewers, and with the people they cook for. Vogue describes the appeal as a hunger for intergenerational knowledge.

You can borrow that without traveling anywhere:

- Ask a relative or older neighbor for a dish they actually make, and watch them make it.
- Write down what you see, including the details a printed recipe would skip.
- Cook it together once, then cook it alone.

A preserved family recipe is a small act of heritage work, and it costs nothing.

## Habit 4: Treat Pleasure as Part of the Plan

If la dolce vita is more than an aesthetic, it's a way of valuing ordinary pleasure: a meal made with your hands, eaten with other people. Compared with wellness regimens that optimize every bite, the grannies' example is gentler. Cook something real, share it, and keep doing it for years.

## Try It: Rosa's Cicatelli con Ciambutella

Vogue's piece includes Rosa's full recipe for cicatelli con ciambutella. Since the ingredient list and method aren't something I can reproduce accurately here, head to the Vogue feature or *Eat Everything* for the measurements and steps.

What you can do before you start:

1. Clear a clean, large work surface.
2. Read the recipe through once, start to finish.
3. Set aside unhurried time. Handmade pasta rewards patience over speed.
4. Plan to eat it with someone.

## The Takeaway

You don't need a trip to Italy to practice la dolce vita. This week, choose one handmade pasta recipe, cook it with what's already in your kitchen, and invite someone to the table.
