Remote island destinations are easy to fall for, and easy to plan badly. A story reported by the BBC shows why the route home deserves as much attention as the route there. By the end of this piece, you'll have a short checklist for confirming your exit before you commit to a far-flung trip.

## What happened

Ruchira Ranasingha, 50, has been stranded in the Cook Islands since 2023 and wants to return to Sri Lanka, according to the BBC. She reportedly followed bad immigration advice that left her unable to leave or go home.

Her problem is a bureaucratic catch-22. Per the BBC's reporting, New Zealand and Australia won't grant her transit visas to fly through their countries to Sri Lanka. Other routes, via Hawaii or Tahiti, would require biometric processing, and that can only be done in New Zealand.

So the doors that would let her leave are locked behind each other. The BBC also reports pressure from home: her son's wedding is approaching and her mother's health is declining.

Hers case involves immigration status and advice, which is a different situation from an ordinary holiday. Even so, the structure of the trap is useful for any traveler to understand.

## Why remote destinations raise the stakes

Small, far-off islands often have few flight options. When there are only a handful of ways out, each one tends to pass through a larger country with its own entry and transit rules. If those countries say no, you may have no easy workaround.

That is the pattern in this story. The routes mentioned run through New Zealand, Australia, Hawaii and Tahiti, and each depends on a rule that someone else controls.

## A pre-booking checklist

These are general planning habits, not rules drawn from this case. Use them as prompts for your own research.

### 1. Map every realistic route home

Before booking, list at least two ways to get from your destination to your home country. Note every country where you would change planes, even for a short layover.

### 2. Check transit rules for each stopover

Entry rules and transit rules are not always the same thing. Look up whether your passport needs a transit visa for each connecting country, using the official government site, not a forum or a third party.

### 3. Find out where any biometrics are collected

The BBC reports that Ranasingha's alternative routes hinge on biometric processing that can only happen in New Zealand. If any visa you might need requires fingerprints or a photo in person, find out where that happens and whether you can reach it from where you will be.

### 4. Treat consultants with caution

The BBC says Ranasingha followed bad immigration advice. If you are paying someone to guide your paperwork, confirm they are properly qualified in the country concerned and verify what they tell you with the official immigration authority. Get answers in writing.

### 5. Keep your status clear

Know exactly what your visa permits and when it ends. If anything about your status is ambiguous, resolve it with the official authority early, while you still have options and time.

### 6. Build in a buffer

Avoid tying your travel to fixed, can't-miss events without slack. A wedding or a family emergency back home is the worst moment to discover a missing document.

## The wider lesson: policies that don't talk to each other

It is hard not to notice how this works. No single rule here is unreasonable on its own. But when several countries' policies line up, they can produce a situation where no legal exit exists. That is analysis, not a claim from the reporting, but it is a good reason to check routes as a whole instead of one leg at a time.

## If you're already in a bind

Don't improvise. Contact your country's embassy or consulate, and an immigration lawyer or accredited adviser who is qualified in the relevant jurisdiction. Keep copies of everything you have been told and every document you have submitted.

## Where food fits in

This site usually helps you decide what to eat in a place. Markets, street stalls and regional dishes are the reason many people choose remote islands in the first place. But the best meal on a trip is a better memory when you know you can get home afterward. Before you book your next far-off food destination, trace the route back to your own front door and confirm every leg of it.
