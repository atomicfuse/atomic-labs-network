Billy Bob Thornton has picked a side in the great Thanksgiving debate. The host of "The Mayflower" told Fox News Digital that he's a Thanksgiving "purist" who prefers a traditional holiday meal at home rather than at a restaurant, per Fox News. The framing of the piece is simple: Thanksgiving is a comfort-driven, nostalgic ritual, and home cooking and familiar traditions are what make it feel right.

You don't have to agree with him. But his stance is a useful prompt for a real decision many households face every November: cook it all, order some of it, or book a table. Here's how to think it through, with a few practical ways to protect what matters most to you.

## What the purist argument is really about

Thornton's take, as reported, isn't a complaint about restaurant food. It's about what the day is for. A holiday built around nostalgia and routine depends on the things a dining room can't easily copy: the smells in the house, the dishes that show up every year, the slow build toward the meal.

That's worth taking seriously, because it gives you a better question than "which is easier?" Ask instead: **what part of Thanksgiving would I miss most if it vanished?** If the answer is the cooking, the leftovers, or the lingering at the table afterward, home probably wins. If it's simply being together, a restaurant can work.

## When cooking at home makes the most sense

Home is the stronger choice if any of these sound like you:

- **You care about the ritual.** The prep, the family recipes, and the kitchen chaos are part of the holiday for you.
- **You want leftovers.** Turkey sandwiches the next day are a tradition in themselves, and a restaurant meal rarely leaves you with a fridge full.
- **You want an unhurried afternoon.** At home, nobody is waiting for your table.

## When a restaurant is a reasonable call

To be fair to the pragmatists, there are good reasons families consider dining out or ordering in, and the convenience-versus-tradition debate is exactly what's being talked about this season. A restaurant can make sense if you're hosting a small group with no kitchen capacity, if cooking would leave the host too exhausted to enjoy the day, or if the point of the gathering is simply to be in one room.

The tradeoff is the one Thornton points to: convenience can quietly cost you the feeling that made the holiday feel "real."

## A middle path: keep the ritual, cut the workload

You don't have to choose between a from-scratch marathon and a reservation. If your goal is to keep the home-cooked spirit without burning out, try this:

1. **Pick your non-negotiables.** Choose two or three dishes that carry the memories, such as the stuffing or a family pie, and make those yourself.
2. **Outsource the rest.** Let guests bring sides, or buy one item ready-made so you aren't juggling everything.
3. **Prep ahead.** Work backward from the meal, chopping vegetables and setting the table the day before so Thursday is for cooking and company.
4. **Keep the table at home.** Even a simplified menu eaten in your own dining room preserves the setting that purists value.

## A simple way to settle the debate at your table

If your household is split, skip the argument and run a quick check. Have everyone name the one thing that makes Thanksgiving feel like Thanksgiving. Write the answers down. Whatever shows up most is what your plan has to protect, whether that's a home-cooked bird or just everybody sitting down together.

## The takeaway

Thornton's preference for a traditional meal at home is a personal one, and plenty of families will land elsewhere. But his instinct holds a useful lesson: decide what your holiday is for before you decide where to eat it. Name your must-have tradition this week, protect it in your plan, and let everything else be as easy as you like.
