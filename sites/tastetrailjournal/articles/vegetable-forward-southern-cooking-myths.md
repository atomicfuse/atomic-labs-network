Think Southern food and you probably picture fried everything and gravy over the top. Texas-based chef and restaurateur Tiffany Derry wants you to look again. Per Fox News, she is challenging common assumptions about what is traditionally served across the South, pointing to wholesome ingredients, colorful vegetables, and simple preparation.

According to Fox News, Derry reframes traditional Southern cooking as fresh, colorful, and vegetable-forward, rooted in simple ingredients rather than heavy stereotypes. It's also part of a wider conversation. As people rethink comfort food through health, heritage, and authenticity, Southern cuisine is being revisited beyond outdated assumptions.

## Why the Stereotype Sticks (and Where It Falls Short)

Stereotypes usually start from something real. Rich, celebratory dishes are memorable, so they travel farthest in the popular imagination. That's our take, not Derry's, but it helps explain the gap. A stereotype built on the showstoppers leaves out the vegetables, the simple sides, and the plain cooking that fill out the rest of the table.

The practical lesson: when you cook Southern-inspired food, don't treat vegetables as the supporting act. Treat them as the point.

## How to Cook Vegetable-Forward Southern Style at Home

These are general, approachable steps in the spirit of Derry's message of wholesome ingredients and simple preparation. They are our suggestions, not her recipes.

### 1. Start with what looks best

Buy what's fresh and colorful. A plate with several colors is a good sign you're on the right track. Tomatoes, okra, greens, corn, peppers, and peas are all classic Southern-table vegetables you can find in many markets.

### 2. Keep the preparation simple

Let the ingredient lead. Season well, cook it until it's tender or just crisp as you prefer, and taste before adding more. Fewer steps means the vegetable's own flavor comes through.

### 3. Build a plate, not a single dish

Pick two or three vegetable sides and give them equal billing with whatever protein you serve. A table of varied sides is a very Southern way to eat, and it's naturally lighter than a single heavy centerpiece.

### 4. Use richness as an accent

A little smoky seasoning, a spoonful of something tangy, or a finishing squeeze of acid can make vegetables taste deeply satisfying without burying them.

## A Simple Weeknight Template

Try this loose framework rather than a strict recipe:

- **One green:** sautéed or simmered with onion and garlic until tender.
- **One summer-style vegetable:** sliced tomatoes with salt and pepper, or quickly cooked okra or corn.
- **One hearty side:** beans, peas, or rice.
- **One simple main:** whatever protein fits your household.

Put it all on the table at once and let everyone build their own plate. You get color, variety, and a meal that reflects the fresh, simple approach Derry describes.

## Honoring Tradition While Changing the Story

There's a useful distinction in Derry's perspective. She isn't asking anyone to abandon tradition. She's asking people to see more of it. Per Fox News, the aim is to honor heritage while challenging clichés. That's a good rule for any regional cuisine you explore: look past the famous dishes and ask what people actually eat day to day.

## If You're Traveling

When you eat your way through the South, or any region, scan menus and markets for the sides and seasonal produce, not just the headline dishes. Ask what's in season. Those plates often tell you the most about how a place really cooks.

## The Takeaway

Next time you cook Southern-inspired food, start at the produce stand, not the fryer: pick the most colorful vegetables you can find, keep the cooking simple, and let them lead the plate.
