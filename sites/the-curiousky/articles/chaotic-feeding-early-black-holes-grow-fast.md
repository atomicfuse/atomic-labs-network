One black hole in the early universe has been bothering astronomers for years. When the cosmos was less than 700 million years old, the supermassive black hole powering a quasar called J0313-1806 had already reached about 3 billion times the mass of the sun, per Phys. A new study suggests the answer to how it got so big may be surprisingly untidy. By the end of this article, you'll understand the "speed limit" problem, why chaotic feeding might sidestep it, and which telescopes could test the idea.

The study, reported by Phys and published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics*, argues that early supermassive black holes may have grown fast enough through "chaotic accretion." In this scenario, ordinary stellar-mass seeds could explain the earliest known giant black holes, without needing rare heavy seeds or long stretches of super-Eddington feeding.

That is a lot of jargon. Here is what it means in plain terms.

## The speed limit problem

Black holes grow by pulling in gas. As that gas spirals inward, it heats up and shines, which is why quasars are among the brightest objects known. That light pushes outward on the incoming gas, so a black hole can only feed so fast before its own glow starts holding back the meal.

That ceiling is called the Eddington limit. A black hole with a stellar-mass starting point, feeding at a steady, sensible pace, struggles to reach billions of solar masses in under 700 million years. J0313-1806 is one of the earliest known examples of a giant that apparently did it anyway.

That leaves astronomers with a few options, and this is the mystery in a nutshell:

- **Heavy seeds:** the black hole started out far bigger than a typical collapsed star, giving it a head start. These would be rare.
- **Speed-limit-breaking growth:** the black hole fed beyond the Eddington limit (super-Eddington) for a long time.
- **Productive chaos:** the new proposal, in which ordinary seeds grow quickly because of how the gas arrives.

## What "chaotic" feeding means

The gas around a black hole doesn't always arrive neatly. In the chaotic accretion picture described by Phys, gas disks around the black hole are randomly oriented. One feeding episode may swirl one way, and the next may tilt or reverse relative to the last.

This matters because of spin. A black hole fed steadily from one direction gets spun up, like a wheel being pushed again and again the same way. Feeding from randomly oriented disks tends to cancel that out, which keeps the black hole's spin low.

## Why a slow spin means faster growth

Here is the counterintuitive part. Per the study's model, a slowly spinning black hole is less efficient at turning infalling matter into light. That sounds like a drawback, but it works in the black hole's favor.

If less of the matter's energy escapes as radiation, there is less outward push resisting the next helping of gas. More of the mass ends up swallowed rather than lost to glare. Messy, inefficient feeding becomes a way to put on weight quickly while staying within the normal feeding rules.

That is why the study says stellar-mass seeds may be enough. No exotic head start and no prolonged breaking of the speed limit is required, according to the model.

## Why this debate is heating up

The timing reflects what telescopes are finding. According to the Phys report, JWST and upcoming surveys are revealing surprisingly massive black holes and "little red dot" galaxies in the early universe. These discoveries have intensified the argument over how cosmic giants formed so quickly. The summary of the research doesn't say how little red dots connect to this particular model, so treat that link as an open question rather than a settled one.

## How the idea could be tested

A good model makes predictions that can fail. The key prediction here is that these early black holes should be slow spinners, products of chaotic feeding. Phys reports that future missions, including Euclid, Roman and NewAthena, could help test that.

If the earliest giants turn out to be slow-spinning, that would favor productive chaos. If they don't, heavy seeds or super-Eddington growth look better by comparison. Either way, the observations will narrow the field.

## What this means when you look up

You can't see J0313-1806 with a backyard telescope, but you can carry away the main lesson: a faster path to a giant doesn't always mean a cleaner or more efficient one. Next time you read about a record-breaking early black hole, ask which of the three explanations it supports. That question is exactly what astronomers are trying to answer.
