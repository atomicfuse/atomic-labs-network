Look up at a quiet Moon and it seems perfectly still. It isn't. By the end of this article you'll know why a moonquake can keep shaking the ground for close to an hour, and why that matters for anyone hoping to build on the Moon.

Researchers have developed the first ground motion model made specifically for shallow moonquakes, according to a report from Phys. Their finding: seismic shaking on the Moon can stay strong over distances far beyond what Earth-based models predict. The work combines seismic data from the Apollo missions with lunar physics simulations.

## Why an Earth model doesn't fit the Moon

Civil engineers on Earth rely on ground motion models. These estimate how hard the ground will shake at a given distance from an earthquake, and they guide how buildings are designed. The obvious shortcut for lunar construction would be to borrow those models.

The new research asks whether that shortcut works. Per Phys, the answer is that Earth-based models underestimate how far strong shaking carries on the Moon. A structure far from a moonquake's source could still feel serious shaking.

## Shaking that lasts nearly an hour

The numbers are striking. Seismic shaking on the Moon can last nearly an hour and travel more than 1,000 kilometers, according to the report. For comparison, most people think of an earthquake as something that shakes for seconds to a minute or two. That is a general point about Earth experience, not a figure from the study.

A 1,000-kilometer reach is roughly the distance from Paris to Rome. Shaking from a single shallow moonquake could, in principle, matter across a very large region.

## Why the Moon rings like a bell

The coverage points to two features of lunar geology that let seismic energy travel farther and linger longer: the Moon's cold interior and its fractured surface.

The report version of the physics, as I read it: on Earth, seismic energy fades relatively quickly as it moves through the ground. On the Moon, conditions let that energy persist. The details of the mechanism are in the researchers' work, and I won't guess beyond what Phys reported. But the practical result is clear. The shaking doesn't die out the way Earth-trained intuition expects.

## What Apollo data still does

The model leans on seismic records gathered during the Apollo era, then pairs them with lunar physics simulations. That is a useful reminder that those instruments, left on the surface decades ago, are still shaping how we plan for the Moon. The new work turns that older data into practical guidance for the next phase of lunar development.

## Why this matters for lunar bases

Governments and private companies are planning lunar bases, habitats and infrastructure. Engineers designing them need realistic seismic hazard assessments, and until now they lacked a model built for the Moon.

That suggests "moonquake-proof" design may need different rules than earthquake-resistant design on Earth. This is my analysis, not a finding from the study, but the logic follows: if shaking lingers for nearly an hour and carries over great distances, buildings may need to handle long-duration shaking, and being far from a known fault may offer less protection than it does at home.

## What you can take from this

The Moon isn't a silent, dead rock. Its cold, fractured crust carries vibrations a surprisingly long way. Next time you see the Moon overhead, remember that people are now working out how to build on it, and that old Apollo recordings are helping them do it safely.
