Holiday travel is crowded, pricey and prone to disruption, and a road trip doesn't get you out of that. The interstate before Thanksgiving can look like a parking lot. Time Out's editors recently published 11 holiday travel tips aimed at cutting stress and cost, mostly with airports in mind. Some translate cleanly to the car. A few need adapting. One tells you to stay home.

Here's the full list as Time Out published it, followed by which tips we'd use on a drive and how.

## The 11 tips, per Time Out

Time Out's editors list these, in this order:

1. Plan a buffer day (or two) around travel
2. Moisturize
3. Leave at least two hours between connections
4. Don't pack what you can get delivered instead
5. Travel on the actual holiday day if you can
6. BYO…W.
7. Two words: Airport charcuterie
8. Schedule your ride
9. Bring your own airport food
10. Celebrate the holiday on another day
11. Just don't travel

We're working from those headlines. Anything below beyond them is our own take, not Time Out's, so read the original for the editors' reasoning.

## The tip that matters most on a road trip: build in a buffer

Time Out leads with planning a buffer day, or two, around your travel. For a driver, that's the single most useful idea on the list.

A drive with no slack turns every slow patch of highway into a small crisis. A buffer changes that. If you leave a day early, a closed lane or a snowstorm is an inconvenience, not a missed dinner. It also gives you room for the kind of stop this site exists for: a diner two minutes off the exit, a small-town main street, a short overlook.

Our suggestion: pick the one stop you'd normally skip, and put it on the schedule instead of treating it as an extra. A planned 45-minute break often beats three rushed ones.

## Travel on the holiday itself, if your family can swing it

Time Out's fifth tip is to travel on the actual holiday day if you can. Our read is that the logic works for drivers too: the days right before a holiday tend to be the busiest on the road, and the holiday itself may be quieter. We haven't verified traffic patterns, so check live conditions before you commit.

The tradeoff is real. You arrive later and may skip a big meal's prep, so make sure your hosts know your ETA.

## Food: the tips that apply to the car

Two of Time Out's tips are about eating on the go: bring your own airport food, and the cheekily phrased "Airport charcuterie." The airport details are theirs. The road-trip version is easy to build on your own.

- **Pack a cooler or bag** with things that don't need a fork: cheese, crackers, fruit, nuts, cured meats. A snack board that travels is the car cousin of airport charcuterie.
- **Save restaurant money for the stop that earns it.** Eating your own lunch in the car frees you to sit down at a real diner or pie counter later, where the meal is the point.
- **Keep water in reach.** Time Out's "BYO…W." is cryptic as a headline, and we won't guess what the editors meant. On a drive, a refillable bottle is a safe bet either way.

## Moisturize, and other small comforts

Time Out's second tip is simply "Moisturize." The editors presumably have dry airplane air in mind. Car heaters are dry too, so a small tube of hand cream and lip balm in the cupholder is cheap and takes no effort.

## Tips that are really about airports

Some of the list doesn't map to a drive:

- **Leave at least two hours between connections.** This is flight advice. The driving equivalent is not scheduling a tight arrival window with someone waiting on you.
- **Schedule your ride.** Time Out's headline points to arranging transportation ahead. If you're driving, the parallel is knowing your parking situation at the destination, especially if you're staying with family in a crowded neighborhood. If you're flying and renting, book ahead.
- **Don't pack what you can get delivered instead.** Our interpretation: ship bulky gifts or buy basics at your destination. A trunk packed to the roof limits visibility and makes stops a hassle.

## The two tips about not going at all

The last two are the boldest. Time Out suggests celebrating the holiday on another day, and, at the very end, "Just don't travel."

It's the funniest entry, and it's also reasonable. If the forecast is rough, the budget is tight or everyone is exhausted, shifting the gathering a weekend or staying put is a legitimate plan. A calmer trip a few days later could be better for everyone, and it may suit a road trip especially well, since you can pick a scenic route instead of the fastest one.

## A simple holiday drive plan

Here's how we'd combine the tips, again as our suggestion rather than Time Out's:

1. **Add a buffer day** if you can, or at least padding in your schedule.
2. **Choose one planned stop** within a few minutes of your route, and look up its hours before you go. Holiday hours often differ from regular ones, and we haven't verified any here.
3. **Pack food and water** so you're not at the mercy of a crowded rest area.
4. **Check road conditions** with your state's official traffic or transportation site before leaving.
5. **Have a fallback**: a later departure date, or a gathering moved to another day.

## The takeaway

The best holiday travel tip for a driver is the first one on Time Out's list: leave yourself room. Block in the buffer, pick one good stop, and let the drive be part of the trip instead of the thing to survive.
