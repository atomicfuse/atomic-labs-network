Banff National Park has a celebrity, and he weighs a lot more than any hiker. Bear 122, a grizzly known as "the Boss," has survived a train, cars and rival bears, according to a New York Times profile. The same report raises an uncomfortable question: some people fear the biggest threat to his life now is his own fame.

If you're planning a trip to Banff, that tension is worth understanding before you go. This guide covers how wildlife fame creates risk, and what you can do to see animals well without making things worse.

## Why a famous bear is a vulnerable bear

Per the Times, the Boss has already beaten long odds. He has been hit by a train and by cars, and he has held his ground against other bears. Those are the dangers of living in a park that is also crossed by transportation corridors and visited by crowds.

Fame adds another layer. When a particular animal becomes a social media favorite, more people go looking for him. More people, in turn, means more chances for the kind of close encounters that are bad for both sides. That is my read of the situation, and it is the central worry the Times reports: admiration can turn into risk.

The lesson holds for any park. The more famous the animal, the more careful you need to be.

## How "bear jams" cause trouble

A bear jam is what happens when drivers stop on a road to watch a bear. It sounds harmless and often looks festive. It isn't.

- Stopped cars on a road where animals and vehicles already collide add to the danger. Vehicles are one of the hazards the Boss himself has survived.
- Crowds can push a bear to behave differently around people, or keep it from crossing where it needs to.
- People on foot near a wild grizzly put themselves in real danger.

If you see a bear from the road, keep moving unless Parks Canada staff or posted signs direct otherwise. If there is a legal, designated pullout, use it, and stay in your vehicle.

## What not to do around famous wildlife

These are standard wildlife-viewing rules, and they apply doubly when an animal is a star.

### Don't chase the sighting
Tracking a specific bear through apps, posts or tips from other visitors turns a hike into a stakeout. Go for the landscape, and treat any wildlife you see as a bonus.

### Don't get closer for the photo
Use a zoom lens or binoculars. If an animal changes its behavior because of you, you're too close.

### Don't stop in traffic
Pulling over wherever you spot an animal creates the exact jam described above.

### Don't post exact locations
As a matter of judgment, not a rule from the Times: sharing precise spots where a famous animal was seen can draw crowds to it. Describe the trip without pinpointing the bear.

## Better ways to enjoy a Banff day

You can have a full day in the park without hunting for a headline animal.

1. **Pick a trail for the scenery.** Choose a hike for its lake, waterfall or viewpoint and let wildlife be incidental.
2. **Hike with the right gear.** Carry bear spray where recommended, keep it accessible rather than buried in your pack, and know how to use it before you need it.
3. **Make your presence known.** Talk or call out on blind corners so you don't surprise an animal.
4. **Hike in a group.** More people means more noise and more safety.
5. **Give animals the right of way.** If a bear is on or near the trail, turn around.

## The takeaway

The Boss has outlasted a train, cars and rival bears. The best thing you can do for him and for any wild animal you meet is to keep your distance, keep moving, and let the bear get on with being a bear.
