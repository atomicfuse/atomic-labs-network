Rising seas threaten migratory shorebirds because the beaches, salt marshes, and tidal flats they feed and rest on can't always move inland as the water creeps up. When a wall, road, or row of buildings sits behind the habitat, the habitat gets pinched out. Scientists call that "coastal squeeze," and by the end of this article you'll be able to spot it on your own shoreline.

The finding comes from a review of 37 studies, reported by Phys. According to that report, the research shows widespread risks to shorebird habitats, to bird survival, and to the migration "bottleneck" sites these birds depend on.

## What "coastal squeeze" actually means

Picture a beach as a strip of sand between the ocean and whatever is built behind it. As sea level rises, the waterline moves inland. In a natural setting, the beach, marsh, or mudflat can shift inland along with it and keep functioning.

Now put a seawall, a road, or houses behind that strip. The water keeps advancing, but the habitat has nowhere to go. It gets squeezed between the rising sea on one side and infrastructure on the other, and it shrinks or disappears.

A familiar comparison: think of a rug pushed against a wall. Shove it from the other side and it can't slide, so it bunches up and loses usable space.

Per Phys, coastal development and rising seas are converging to create this squeeze, and it is happening while many shorebird populations are already declining.

## Why shorebirds are especially exposed

Shorebirds rely on three kinds of coastal habitat that sea-level rise is shrinking and degrading, according to the report:

- **Beaches**, used for resting and, for some species, nesting
- **Salt marshes**, the grassy wetlands flooded by salty tides
- **Tidal flats**, the muddy or sandy areas exposed when the tide goes out

These places sit right at the edge of the water, so they are the first to feel a change in sea level. A tidal flat that is uncovered for hours at low tide is a feeding ground. If the sea sits higher, there is less exposed ground to feed on.

## Bottleneck sites: where a small loss does big damage

Migratory birds don't spread out evenly along their routes. The review highlights migration bottleneck sites, places where large numbers of birds funnel through or stop over on the journey. (The report details here are limited to the existence of these sites and the risk to them; the report as summarized doesn't name specific locations.)

The logic is simple. If many birds depend on a few stopover spots, losing one of those spots affects far more birds than losing a random stretch of coast. That is why habitat loss at these sites matters out of proportion to their size.

## Seawalls versus nature-based protection

This is where the story reaches people. Hard structures like seawalls protect property, but they can fix the shoreline in place, which is exactly what sets up a squeeze on the habitat in front of them.

Nature-based approaches work differently. The idea is to keep or restore marshes, beaches, and flats so they can act as a buffer. The Phys report notes that protecting these habitats matters in part because they also defend human communities, store carbon, and support wider biodiversity.

That is the useful takeaway: what's good for a sandpiper's feeding ground is often good for the town behind it. A marsh that soaks up water is doing a job that people would otherwise pay to engineer.

## How to read your own coastline

You don't need a research grant to see the pattern. When you visit a coast, look for these:

1. **What's directly behind the beach or marsh.** A wall, road, or buildings means the habitat has little room to retreat.
2. **How much open space is left at low tide.** Narrow flats mean less feeding area.
3. **Whether there's any undeveloped land inland.** Open ground gives habitat a place to move.

These are my own rules of thumb, not findings from the review, but they follow directly from how coastal squeeze works.

## What this means for planning

The angle that ties it together is shoreline planning. Decisions about where to build, and where to leave room, decide whether habitat can shift as seas rise. Shorebird declines are already underway, per the report, so the margin for error is thin.

Sea-level rise can feel like a faraway problem measured in ice caps. For a shorebird on a tidal flat, it is a local one, and the same is true for the community behind that flat.

The action that follows: the next time your area debates a shoreline project, ask one question: where does the beach or marsh go when the water rises?
