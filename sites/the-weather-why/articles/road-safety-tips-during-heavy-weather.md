If you come to water covering a road during a storm, the safest move is to turn around and take another route. You can't tell how deep the water is or whether the pavement beneath it is still intact. That's the short answer, and the rest of this piece explains why it holds up and what to do in each situation.

The reminder came this Thanksgiving. Per HuffPost, storms battered much of the U.S. with heavy rain, high winds, coastal flooding, snow, power outages and major travel disruption, from the East Coast to California. HuffPost reported that the weather forced the postponement of Macy's 100th Thanksgiving Day Parade, and that it brought flight delays and infrastructure damage during one of the busiest travel periods of the year.

Your own forecast may not look like that, but flooded roads can happen anywhere heavy rain falls. Here is what to know.

## Why a flooded road is more dangerous than it looks

Standing water hides things. The road surface may have washed out, a storm drain may be uncovered, or debris may be sitting just below the surface. Water that looks shallow at the edge can be much deeper in the middle, especially where the road dips.

Moving water adds another problem. Flowing floodwater pushes on a vehicle with real force, and a car doesn't need to be submerged to be affected. That's why the standard advice from weather agencies is "Turn Around, Don't Drown," and it applies even to roads you drive every day.

## Before you leave: check the route, not just the weather

Holiday travel makes this harder, because you may be driving on unfamiliar roads with a deadline. A few habits help:

- **Check conditions for your whole route.** Look at the forecast and road alerts for where you're going and what's in between, not only where you start.
- **Use official sources.** The National Weather Service and your state transportation department are the places to verify warnings, timing and closures.
- **Know the terms.** A flood *watch* means conditions could produce flooding. A flood *warning* means flooding is happening or is expected soon. Watch means prepare; warning means act.
- **Build in slack.** If rain is heavy, leave earlier or wait. A delayed arrival beats a stranded car.

This kind of planning matters more when storms stack up. HuffPost's account of Thanksgiving included wind, snow and coastal flooding in different places at once, so one clear stretch of road didn't mean the whole trip was fine.

## When you meet water on the road

Step through it in this order:

1. **Slow down early.** Heavy rain cuts visibility and traction before you ever reach standing water.
2. **Stop before the water, not in it.** If a road is covered, don't try to judge it by watching other cars cross.
3. **Turn around.** Pick a different route, even if it adds time. Barricades and closure signs mean the road is closed. Don't drive around them.
4. **Go to higher ground if you can do so safely.** Parking lots and side streets can flood quickly too.
5. **Wait it out if needed.** Some flooding recedes within hours, but check an official source before you set out again.

## If your car stalls or water is rising around it

This is the situation you want to avoid, but it helps to know the priority: you matter more than the car. If water is rising around a stalled vehicle and you can get out safely, get out and move to higher ground. Call emergency services if you need help. Don't wait until the water is at the doors to decide.

Don't walk into moving water to reach your car or to cross a road. Floodwater can knock a person over faster than most people expect.

## Why coastal flooding is a different kind of problem

HuffPost's summary pointed to coastal flooding and king tides as part of the holiday disruption. A king tide is an especially high tide, which happens when the sun, moon and Earth line up in a way that boosts the normal rise and fall of the ocean. On a calm day, that can mean water creeping into low-lying streets.

Add a storm, and the effect can stack. Strong winds can push extra water toward shore, and heavy rain has nowhere to drain if the tide is already high. The result can be flooded roads even in places that don't get much rain. If you live or travel near the coast, check the tide schedule along with the forecast.

HuffPost also noted that coastal flooding, king tides and extreme storms are increasingly disrupting major public events, transportation and local infrastructure. For your planning, the takeaway is practical: coastal roads you rely on may be vulnerable during storms that look manageable inland.

## Power outages and wind: the other half of a storm trip

The same storms that flood roads also knock out power. HuffPost reported widespread outages during the holiday. If you're on the road or hosting at home, a few steps cut the stress:

- Keep your phone charged and a charger in the car.
- Treat any downed power line as live and stay well away from it, including water around it.
- If you're driving and traffic lights are out, treat the intersection as a four-way stop.
- Keep the fridge and freezer doors closed during an outage to hold the cold in longer.

High winds can also bring down branches and make driving tricky for tall vehicles and anyone towing something. Slow down and give yourself more room.

## The decision to postpone is a safety call

When Macy's postponed its 100th Thanksgiving Day Parade, per HuffPost, it was a large, visible example of a choice many travelers face on a smaller scale. When conditions are unsafe, delaying the plan is a legitimate answer. Dinner can be reheated, and flights and gatherings can be rescheduled. A flooded road or a downed tree can't be undone.

The next time heavy rain is in the forecast and you're deciding whether to push on, make the call early: if the road is covered with water, turn around.
