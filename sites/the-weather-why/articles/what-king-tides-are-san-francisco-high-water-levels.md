A king tide is simply an extra-high tide, and when one lines up with a storm, water can reach places that normally stay dry. That is the situation San Francisco is preparing for ahead of Thanksgiving. By the end of this article, you will know what a king tide is, why rain makes it riskier, and what to check before you travel.

## What's happening in San Francisco

Per HuffPost, Northern California is bracing for an unprecedented deluge accompanied by high tides ahead of the holiday. The outlet reports that record-level king tides and approaching storm systems are raising the risk of coastal flooding in San Francisco, and that the city's mayor is urging residents to reconsider travel. Officials are also asking people to prepare and to watch for flood impacts that could last a while.

The same report notes that central Florida is dealing with historic flooding, with scattered rain making things worse, along with dangerous marine conditions.

Because conditions can change quickly, check the National Weather Service forecast office for your area for exact timing, locations and warnings. Treat that as the final word over anything you read in a summary like this one.

## What a king tide is

Tides rise and fall every day because of the pull of the moon and sun on the oceans. A king tide is the informal name for the highest tides of the year, which happen when that pull is strongest. It is not a storm. On a calm, dry day, a king tide may just mean the water creeps higher on the shoreline than you are used to.

Think of a bathtub filled almost to the rim. It looks fine until someone adds a little more water. A king tide fills the tub nearly to the top, and anything else, like rain or wind, can push it over.

## Why a storm on top of a king tide is the real problem

That is the combination officials are worried about. Heavy rain falls on streets and runs toward storm drains. Those drains usually empty into the bay or ocean. If the water there is already unusually high, the drains cannot empty as freely, and rainwater can back up onto streets.

So two things happen at once: water arrives from above, and the place it normally drains to is already full. Low-lying streets near the waterfront are typically the first to show it.

This is also why flooding can seem to come from nowhere on a day that is not especially rainy. Timing matters as much as rainfall totals. The same storm at low tide might cause a nuisance. At high tide, it can close roads.

## What El Niño has to do with it

HuffPost's coverage ties the disruption on both coasts to El Niño-driven weather patterns. El Niño is a periodic warming of the tropical Pacific Ocean that can shift where storms track across North America. Its effects vary from place to place, which is why it can be connected to wet weather in both California and Florida. This article does not go beyond that link, and local forecasters are the best source for how it is shaping your specific region.

## How to plan around coastal flooding

The mayor's advice, as reported, is to reconsider travel. If you are in or headed to the Bay Area, here are practical steps that make sense for any flood-risk week:

- **Check the tide timing.** Local tide tables and the National Weather Service will show when high tide peaks. Plan any essential trip away from those hours.
- **Look at your route, not just your destination.** Roads near the water are the likeliest to flood. Have an alternate path in mind.
- **Never drive through standing water.** You cannot tell how deep it is or whether the road beneath it is intact.
- **Move things up.** If you live or park in a low-lying spot, move vehicles to higher ground and lift valuables off the floor.
- **Keep essentials handy.** Phone chargers, medications, water, a flashlight and important documents should be easy to grab.
- **Confirm before you go.** Check flight, train and road status the day you travel, since holiday schedules have little slack.

## Why this lands harder at Thanksgiving

HuffPost points out that the threat arrives during one of the busiest travel weeks of the year. More people on the roads and at airports means less room for delays. Residents, visitors and small businesses all feel it. Flooding can interrupt a holiday week of customers for shops near the waterfront.

## The takeaway

When you hear "king tide" and "storm" in the same forecast, look at the clock as well as the rain total. Find out when high tide peaks, stay off low-lying routes around then, and check official National Weather Service updates before you head out.
