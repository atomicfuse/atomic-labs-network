Glass skyscrapers struggle with heat because glass lets sunlight in, and sunlight turns into heat once it's inside. That heat has to be removed by air conditioning, which takes energy and pushes warmth back out into the city. You've felt a small version of this if you've ever climbed into a parked car on a sunny afternoon.

The New York Times recently examined the rise of glass towers and asked whether their reflective, glittering design makes sense as global temperatures climb. The piece frames these skylines as symbols of modernity that may carry serious climate and energy costs. Here's the weather science behind that question, and what it means for the air you feel on a hot city street.

## The parked-car effect, scaled up

Sunlight arrives as visible light and some invisible radiation. Glass lets much of it pass through. Once inside, that energy is absorbed by floors, desks, and people, and it is re-released as heat. Heat doesn't pass back out through glass as easily as light came in, so it builds up.

A parked car is the familiar example: windows closed, sun overhead, and the interior gets far hotter than the air outside. A glass tower works on the same principle, only with many floors of occupied space that need to stay comfortable.

The practical result is more cooling demand. Buildings that gain heat quickly rely heavily on air conditioning, and that is the core tradeoff behind the glittering look. Per the Times, building design is increasingly seen as a climate resilience issue rather than just an aesthetic choice.

## Why cooling a building warms the street

Air conditioning doesn't make heat disappear. It moves heat from inside a building to outside. Units on rooftops and along buildings release warm air into the surrounding streets.

So a tower that needs a lot of cooling can add to the heat people feel around it. This is one piece of what meteorologists call the urban heat island effect: cities running warmer than nearby less-built areas, because pavement, buildings, and human activity all hold and release heat.

If you've noticed that a downtown stays muggy long after the sun sets while the suburbs cool off, you've felt this effect. Materials and building choices are part of why.

## Reflective doesn't mean cool

It's tempting to think a shiny surface just bounces the sun away. Sometimes reflection does help, but a mirror-like facade sends light somewhere, and that somewhere can be the sidewalk, the street, or the building next door. Anyone standing in that path can feel the extra glare and warmth.

The Times piece questions whether the sleek, reflective design is practical as heat intensifies. The key point for you as a reader: the look of a surface tells you little about how a whole building handles heat. What matters is how much sun gets in, how much heat gets trapped, and how much energy it takes to remove it.

## Why this matters more as heat rises

A building designed around constant air conditioning works only as long as the cooling keeps running and the energy to power it is available. On hotter days, demand for cooling goes up, which means more strain at exactly the moment heat is most dangerous.

This is why the conversation has shifted. Per the Times, developers, architects, and city leaders face growing pressure to rethink materials that intensify cooling demand and urban heat. The question is no longer only whether a tower looks impressive, but whether it stays livable when summers get harsher.

## What a heat-friendlier building looks like

The Times article focuses on the problem rather than a full list of fixes, so treat the following as general weather logic rather than reported findings. The basic ways to keep heat from building up are well understood:

- **Shade the windows.** Blocking sunlight before it enters the glass is more effective than cooling the air afterward. Think of how a porch roof or awning keeps a room cooler.
- **Use less glass.** Solid walls and smaller windows admit less sun.
- **Let air move.** Openings that allow cross-breezes can reduce reliance on mechanical cooling.
- **Think about the street.** Trees and shaded sidewalks around a building help the surrounding area stay cooler.

None of these requires giving up tall buildings. They require treating the sun as a force to be managed, not a feature to be displayed.

## How to spot the tradeoffs yourself

You don't need an engineering degree to read a skyline. Next time you're downtown on a sunny day, look for:

1. **Shade.** Do windows have overhangs, fins, or tinted coverings, or is the glass fully exposed?
2. **Glare.** Are you squinting at light bouncing off a nearby facade?
3. **Warm air at street level.** Do you feel exhaust from vents or a heat difference when you step from a shaded block into a sunny one?
4. **Greenery.** Are there trees nearby doing some of the cooling work?

These small observations reveal a lot about how well a place copes with heat.

## Luxury versus livable

Glass towers have become shorthand for success and modernity. The Times asks whether that image holds up in a hotter world. The honest answer from the weather side is that a design that traps sun and demands heavy cooling is working against the climate it has to live in.

The next time a forecast promises a scorching week, look up at the shiniest building on the block and ask where all that sunlight is going. Then look for the shade: that's where a city is quietly doing it right.
