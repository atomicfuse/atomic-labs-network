Moonquake shaking can last nearly an hour and travel more than 1,000 kilometers, far beyond what Earth-based models would predict. The short reason: the moon is cold, dry, and fractured near the surface, and those conditions let vibrations keep going instead of fading out.

That's the headline from new research, reported by Phys, which describes the first ground motion model built specifically for shallow moonquakes. A ground motion model is a tool that estimates how strongly the ground will shake at a given distance from a quake. Civil engineers use them on Earth to decide how sturdy a building has to be. The question for the moon is whether Earth's versions would work there. Based on this research, the answer looks like no.

## What a ground motion model actually does

Think of dropping a stone in a pond. Close to the splash, the waves are tall. Farther out, they shrink and die away. A ground motion model is a rulebook for that shrinking: how much shaking you should expect at 10 kilometers, at 100, at 1,000.

Earth-based models were built from decades of recorded earthquakes. Per Phys, the researchers wanted to know if those rules transfer to the moon, where engineers hope to eventually build bases, habitats, and other infrastructure. Using Earth's rules there could mean misjudging the risk.

## Why moon shaking lingers

On Earth, you may know the feel of a quake: a sharp jolt, a rolling shake, then it's over, often within a minute or so. Moonquakes behave differently, and the reporting points to two main reasons.

### The surface is fractured

The moon's outer layer is broken up. Seismic energy bounces and scatters through that cracked material rather than traveling in a clean, quick pulse. Picture clapping inside a small tiled bathroom versus outdoors: the sound lingers because it keeps reflecting. The shaking works in a similar way, and that helps explain durations approaching an hour.

### The interior is cold

Per Phys, the moon's cold interior is part of why seismic energy travels farther. On Earth, a warm, wet crust soaks up vibrations and weakens them as they spread. A cold, dry interior absorbs less, so more energy makes it across greater distances. That is how shaking stays strong well past 1,000 kilometers.

## Where the data came from: Apollo

The model combines two ingredients: seismic data recorded during the Apollo era and physics simulations of how waves move through lunar materials. The Apollo seismometers left on the surface gave researchers real recordings. Simulations filled in the physics needed to turn those recordings into a usable model for shallow moonquakes.

That's a notable second life for decades-old measurements. Those instruments were never designed with construction codes in mind, yet their records are now helping shape guidance for the next phase of lunar development.

## What this means for building on the moon

I'll flag this part as my own analysis rather than something the researchers stated: if shaking can stay strong over long distances and last a long time, then "moonquake-proof" design probably can't simply copy earthquake-resistant design.

Here's why that reasoning holds up:

- **Distance matters differently.** On Earth, a site far from a fault is often considered relatively safe. If moon shaking stays strong across 1,000 kilometers, distance buys less protection.
- **Duration matters.** Structures can be stressed by repeated cycles of shaking. A quake that rattles on for close to an hour asks something different of a building than one that ends in a minute.
- **Assumptions need testing.** A model built for Earth's crust could understate the hazard on the moon, which is exactly the gap this new work aims to fill.

As governments and private companies plan lunar bases, hazard assessments like this give engineers a starting point grounded in lunar data instead of Earth assumptions.

## Why a weather site cares about moonquakes

At its core, this is the same kind of question we ask about weather: why does the same type of event behave so differently depending on the environment? A storm over warm ocean water acts differently than one over cold land. Waves of energy traveling through different materials do the same. The moon has no atmosphere to speak of for this story, but the lesson carries over: conditions set the outcome, so you can't just borrow rules from somewhere else.

## The takeaway

When you hear about plans for moon bases, remember that the ground itself is part of the forecast. Shaking there can last longer and reach farther than Earth experience suggests, which is why researchers are building lunar-specific rules instead of reusing Earth's.
