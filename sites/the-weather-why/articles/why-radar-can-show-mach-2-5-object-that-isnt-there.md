Radar can make an object appear to fly at Mach 2.5 without anything moving that fast. Radar doesn't see an object. It sees echoes, and it calculates speed from how those echoes shift between sweeps. When the echoes are unreliable, the math can still look precise while the conclusion is wrong.

That's the lesson in a recent New York Post report on Pentagon UFO files. Per the Post's headline, the files describe mysterious radar detections suggesting flight at around Mach 2.5, "but there's a catch." The details I can verify here are limited to that framing: the detections appeared to indicate very high speed, and there may be a limitation or alternative explanation behind the data. I can't tell you from that report what the object was, where it was, or exactly what the catch is. What I can do is explain how radar works, which is the same technology your weather app uses, and why its readings deserve caution.

## What Mach 2.5 actually means

Mach 1 is the speed of sound, which is roughly 760 mph at sea level but lower at high altitude where the air is colder. Mach 2.5 is two and a half times that speed. That's the territory of high-performance military jets, not something you'd mistake for a passing airliner.

So a Mach 2.5 reading is eye-catching. But a number on a screen is only as good as the measurement behind it.

## How radar gets speed, and where it goes wrong

Radar sends out a pulse of radio energy and listens for what bounces back. The delay tells it distance. Track that distance over several sweeps and you get speed. Weather radar works the same way, except the targets are raindrops, hail, and snow rather than aircraft.

That means every speed reading depends on the radar linking the right echoes together from one sweep to the next. If it links the wrong ones, the speed is wrong. Here are common ways that happens:

- **Mismatched echoes.** If a radar picks up two separate returns and treats them as one moving object, the apparent jump between them can look like extreme speed.
- **Bending radio beams.** Air layers with sharply different temperature or moisture can bend a radar beam. Meteorologists call this anomalous propagation. It can make the radar "see" ground clutter or distant objects that aren't where they appear.
- **Non-aircraft targets.** Birds, insects, clouds of particles, and even clear-air features can return echoes. On weather radar, these show up regularly as unexplained blobs.
- **Processing artifacts.** Software that smooths or filters data can create tracks that never existed physically.

The weather connection is real. If you've ever seen a radar loop show a ring of rain that appears at sunrise and spreads out, then vanishes, you may have watched birds taking off or a temperature inversion at work. It looked like weather. It wasn't.

## Why one sensor isn't enough

A single radar track is a clue, not a conclusion. Analysts generally want a second, independent source before trusting a surprising result: another radar, an infrared camera, a pilot's visual, or a transponder signal. When only one sensor reports something extraordinary, the simplest explanation is often a sensor quirk.

That's why the "catch" framing in the Post headline matters. It signals that the data may have a limitation. I'd treat that as the most important part of the story, since I can't confirm from the report what the limitation is.

## Why "UFO file" headlines travel fast

Public interest in UFOs and UAP (unidentified anomalous phenomena, the government's preferred term) stays high as disclosures and military sighting debates continue. A phrase like "Mach 2.5" gives a vague mystery a number, and numbers feel like evidence.

But "unidentified" means exactly that: not yet identified. It doesn't mean "not explainable." Many reports in this category end up classified as data problems or ordinary objects once someone checks the sensors.

## How to read a radar claim yourself

Next time you see a dramatic radar headline, ask four things:

1. **How many sensors saw it?** One is a lead. Two or more independent ones is stronger.
2. **Did the track stay continuous?** Real objects move smoothly. Jumps between echoes are a red flag.
3. **What was the air doing?** Temperature inversions and moisture layers can distort beams.
4. **Who is saying what?** Separate what officials documented from what commentators speculated.

By that checklist, the Pentagon file reported by the New York Post sits where most UAP stories do: intriguing data, an unresolved explanation, and a caveat worth taking seriously.

## The takeaway

When a radar reading looks impossible, check the measurement before you doubt physics. Ask what else could produce those echoes, and wait for a second sensor to agree.
