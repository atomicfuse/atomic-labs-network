Why are rising seas a problem for shorebirds? Because the water is climbing into the places they feed and rest, and in many spots there's nowhere for those places to move. That trap has a name, coastal squeeze, and once you understand it, you'll see your own shoreline a little differently.

The topic comes up again with a review of 37 studies, reported by Phys, that points to widespread risks to shorebird habitats, survival, and migration bottleneck sites. Here's what that means, and why it matters well beyond the birds.

## The short answer: the coast can't back up

Migratory shorebirds depend on beaches, salt marshes, and tidal flats. Per Phys, climate-driven sea-level rise is shrinking and degrading those habitats.

In a natural setting, a rising sea isn't necessarily the end of a marsh or a beach. These habitats can shift inland as the water moves up. Think of a beach as a line that slides uphill over time.

## What "coastal squeeze" actually means

Now picture that same beach with a road, a parking lot, or a row of buildings right behind it. The water pushes in from the front. The infrastructure blocks the back. The habitat in between gets thinner and thinner until it is squeezed out.

That is coastal squeeze: ecosystems trapped between rising water and human development. According to the Phys report, coastal development and rising seas are converging to create exactly this situation.

A simple way to picture it is a sandwich being pressed flat. The filling, which is the beach, marsh, or mudflat, has nowhere to go.

## Why this hits shorebirds especially hard

The timing is poor. Many shorebird populations are already declining, per the report, so losing habitat adds pressure to species that are already struggling.

The review of 37 studies found widespread risks in three areas:

- **Habitat:** the beaches, marshes, and flats birds use are shrinking or degrading.
- **Survival:** the loss of feeding and resting places threatens the birds themselves.
- **Migration bottleneck sites:** these are the key stops where large numbers of migrating birds depend on a limited stretch of coast.

### Why bottleneck sites matter

A bottleneck is a narrow point that lots of traffic has to pass through. For migratory birds, it's a stop they rely on during a long journey. If a bottleneck site shrinks or degrades, the effect isn't local. Birds that travel between distant regions can be affected at once, because they all depend on the same few places along the way.

## Why you should care even if you never see a sandpiper

The same habitats that serve birds also serve people. Per the report, protecting them is increasingly urgent because they defend human communities, store carbon, and support broader biodiversity.

In practical terms:

- **Defense:** coastal ecosystems stand between the open water and the communities behind them.
- **Carbon:** these habitats store carbon, so losing them works against climate goals.
- **Biodiversity:** shorebirds are one part of a larger web of coastal life.

So saving a tidal flat isn't a trade-off between birds and people. A healthy shoreline helps both.

## What smarter shoreline planning looks like

The coastal squeeze problem points toward a straightforward idea: leave room for the coast to move. When planners keep space behind beaches and marshes free of development, those habitats have a chance to shift inland as seas rise, rather than being pinned against a barrier.

A useful comparison is hard barriers such as seawalls versus nature-based protection, which relies on living coastal habitats. A seawall holds a line in place. That can protect what's behind it, but it can also contribute to the squeeze in front of it. Habitats that can migrate inland keep working as both wildlife habitat and a buffer.

The Phys summary doesn't lay out a detailed design for any particular place, so local conditions will shape what works where. The broad principle is clear, though: planning with the coast's movement in mind beats fighting it.

## How to spot coastal squeeze near you

You don't need to be a scientist to notice the pattern. On your next coastal walk, look at what lies directly behind the beach or marsh.

1. **Find the back edge.** Is there a wall, road, or building right behind the habitat?
2. **Look for open space.** Is there undeveloped land where the beach or marsh could shift upward over time?
3. **Ask your local officials.** Shoreline planning decisions are often public, and questions about leaving room for coastal habitats are fair ones.

If the back edge is hard and close, that stretch is a candidate for squeeze as sea levels rise. That's my read of the concept, not a measurement of any specific shore.

## The takeaway

Rising seas aren't only an ice-cap story. They reach the beach, marsh, or mudflat near you, where migratory shorebirds feed and rest. Next time you're at the coast, check what's behind the shoreline, and speak up for leaving room for it to move.
