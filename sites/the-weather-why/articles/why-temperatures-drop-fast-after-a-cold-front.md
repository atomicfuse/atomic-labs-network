Temperatures "nosedive" after a cold front because the front is the leading edge of a colder, denser air mass pushing out the warmer air that was sitting over you. The change can feel abrupt, sometimes within a few hours, rather than a gentle slide downward. That is the story behind this week's Thanksgiving forecasts, and knowing how it works helps you plan what to wear, what to cover, and when to travel.

## What's happening this week

Per FOX Weather, a cold snap is looming, with temperatures set to "take a nosedive" for more than 200 million Americans ahead of Thanksgiving. Local stations are describing the same pattern. KCBD reported an overnight cold front bringing a chilly forecast for the Sunday of Thanksgiving week, following "one more beautiful warm day." WAFB was tracking widespread showers before a chilly Thanksgiving.

Exact timing and temperatures depend on where you live, so check your local National Weather Service forecast for your hour-by-hour details.

## What a cold front actually is

A front is the boundary between two air masses with different temperature and moisture. At a cold front, the colder air is the one advancing. Cold air is denser, so it wedges under the warmer air and shoves it upward.

Think of opening the door of a warm house on a freezing day. The cold doesn't politely drift in; it spills across the floor first. A cold front works on a much bigger scale, but the idea is similar.

## Why the drop feels sudden

The temperature doesn't fall gradually because the boundary between the air masses is fairly sharp. Before the front passes, you are in the warm air. After it passes, you are in the cold air. The transition can take just a few hours.

That is why a forecast can read "beautiful and warm" one day and "much chillier" the next. You may notice other signs as it passes:

- A wind shift, often turning more northerly or westerly
- Falling temperatures even in the middle of the day
- A change in humidity, usually drier air behind the front

## Why showers and fog come first

When the warm air is forced upward, it cools, and the moisture in it condenses into clouds and rain. That is why showers so often travel along or just ahead of the front. It fits WAFB's headline pairing of widespread showers with a chilly Thanksgiving.

Fog is a related piece. WAFB's story also referenced dense fog ahead of the cold front. Moist air can sit near the ground when conditions are calm, and fog can form when that air cools to its dew point, the temperature at which the air can't hold its moisture as vapor. Fog is a reason to leave extra time on morning drives.

## Why the cold feels worse than the number

The first day after a front often feels harsher than the thermometer suggests, for three reasons:

1. **Your body adjusts slowly.** Going from a warm day to a cold one is a bigger shock than a gradual cooling.
2. **Wind picks up.** Moving air strips heat from your skin faster, so it feels colder than the actual temperature.
3. **Damp air chills.** If showers just passed, wet clothes and wet ground make you lose warmth faster.

## A quick prep checklist before it arrives

You have a little time. Here is a practical order of operations:

### Today or tomorrow
- **Pull out layers.** A base layer, a warm middle layer and a wind-resistant outer layer beat one bulky coat, because you can adjust as the day changes.
- **Move the coats to the front of the closet.** Gloves and hats are easy to forget until you need them.
- **Check your car.** Look at tire pressure, since cold air lowers it, and keep an ice scraper and blanket handy.

### Before the front passes
- **Bring in or cover tender plants.** Cover them with a sheet or cloth, not plastic touching the leaves.
- **Disconnect hoses and protect outdoor faucets** if temperatures near freezing are in your local forecast.
- **Plan for pets.** If it's too cold for you to stand outside comfortably, shorten outdoor time for pets and give them a warm, dry place.

### For Thanksgiving travel
- **Check the forecast along your route, not just your destination.** A front moves, and conditions at each end of your drive can differ.
- **Build in time for rain and fog.** Showers and low visibility are the more likely early-week trouble in the reports above.
- **Pack a warm layer in the car even for a short trip.**

## Why forecasts can shift near a front

Because fronts are boundaries, a small change in speed can move the temperature change by hours or by a town. That is one reason a forecast may be adjusted overnight. If your plans hinge on the exact timing, check again the morning you head out.

## The takeaway

A cold front is a warm-to-cold handoff that can happen in a few hours, so dress for the air that's coming, not the air you woke up in. Check your local National Weather Service forecast, set out your layers tonight, and leave extra time for showers and fog.
