A Mumbai apartment has a lesson for anyone who wants a home that feels expensive without feeling fussy: pick one thing to treasure, and let almost everything else relax around it. Below, you'll find how that idea played out in a real project, plus ways to borrow it at three budget levels.

## The project behind the idea

Per Architectural Digest, Cape Town studio ARRCC worked with Avas Design Studio on its first project in Mumbai: a Malabar Hill apartment in South Mumbai. The building was built by the late architect and celebrated artist Jehangir Vazifdar. The team kept one cherished art deco ceiling and let go of almost everything formal.

The guiding idea is what the coverage calls "barefoot luxury," a philosophy that puts comfort, restraint and timelessness ahead of ostentation. The restored art deco ceiling is the home's anchor, and Japanese sliding screens help blur the boundaries between rooms.

I haven't seen the floor plan or exact measurements, so I won't invent any. What the project does give us is a clear method, and it translates well to ordinary homes. The suggestions below are my own conceptual takeaways, not details from the apartment.

## Step 1: Choose one anchor and protect it

The apartment's anchor is a ceiling. Yours might be a window with good proportions, a tiled fireplace, an old wooden door, or a single piece of art. The principle: decide what's special, then make everything else quieter so it can be seen.

- **Low budget:** Paint the walls and trim in one soft, matte color so your anchor stands out. A gallon of quality paint typically covers about 350 to 400 square feet, so a standard room needs one to two gallons.
- **Mid budget:** Add a picture light or a small adjustable spotlight aimed at the feature. Warm bulbs around 2700K keep it inviting.
- **Higher budget:** Hire a restorer for the feature itself, such as plaster repair, refinishing or re-tiling. That is the closest match to what the Malabar Hill team did with its ceiling.

## Step 2: Edit the formal out

The coverage describes letting go of almost everything formal. At home, that means asking of each item: do I use it, or does it just perform? Formal dining sets nobody sits at, matching furniture suites and stiff, symmetrical styling are common culprits.

Try this quick edit:

1. Clear one surface completely, such as a console or coffee table.
2. Put back only three things: something useful, something personal, something beautiful.
3. Live with it for a week before adding anything else.

The goal is a room that invites you to kick your shoes off, which is the spirit of the "barefoot" idea.

## Step 3: Keep the palette quiet

Restraint works best with a limited color range. A reliable approach is three tones: a light neutral for walls, a mid-tone for larger furniture and textiles, and one darker accent for small touches like frames or a lamp base. Vary texture rather than color. Linen, wool, wood, ceramic and cotton make a neutral room feel layered instead of flat.

- **Low budget:** Swap cushion covers and a throw in natural-fiber fabrics. Covers are often far cheaper than new inserts.
- **Mid budget:** Replace one synthetic rug with wool or jute, sized so at least the front legs of your sofa sit on it.
- **Higher budget:** Commission or buy one handmade piece, such as a woven rug or a ceramic lamp, that carries the room's texture.

## Step 4: Make rooms flexible with screens

The Japanese sliding screens in the apartment let spaces open up or close off as needed. That is a smart small-space idea, because a room can serve more than one purpose without permanent walls.

- **Low budget:** A freestanding folding screen, usually around 150 to 180 cm (5 to 6 ft) tall, can hide a desk or sleeping area in a studio. Pick a light, matte finish to keep it calm.
- **Mid budget:** A ceiling-mounted curtain track with floor-length linen gives a similar soft divide and costs far less than building anything.
- **Higher budget:** Custom sliding panels on a track, in timber and paper or a translucent material, let light pass through while still separating zones.

Whichever you choose, leave enough clear floor space for the panels to travel, and keep furniture out of their path.

## Step 5: Curate rather than collect

A home with heritage details can look cluttered quickly if every shelf is full. Display fewer pieces, and give each room to breathe. Group objects in odd numbers, leave empty space around them, and rotate items seasonally instead of showing everything at once. Store the rest.

This is also a budget-friendly approach: you are using what you already own, and the editing is free.

## Step 6: Let objects change jobs

One of the ideas associated with this kind of home is that objects can evolve beyond their original purpose. In practice, that is a bowl that becomes a key catcher, a vintage trunk that becomes a side table, or a bench that doubles as extra seating at the dining table. These repurposed pieces give a room an unforced, collected feel that purchased sets rarely achieve.

## A quick weekend plan

- **Saturday morning:** Pick your anchor and clear everything competing with it.
- **Saturday afternoon:** Edit one surface using the three-item rule.
- **Sunday:** Swap in natural textiles, adjust lighting around the anchor, and add a screen or curtain if the room needs to do double duty.

Luxury of this kind isn't about adding more. Start by finding the one thing in your home worth protecting, then remove whatever is shouting over it.
