BuzzFeed recently rounded up 56 kitchen products it calls worth both the space they take and the money they cost. The full product list isn't something I can pass along here, so instead of repeating picks I can't vouch for, this article gives you the thinking behind a list like that: a simple way to test any kitchen purchase before it earns a spot on your counter or in your cabinet.

The pitch behind that roundup is a familiar frustration. Gadgets that look useful in a photo often turn into clutter, and the ones that do work tend to cost more. Here's how to tell which is which.

## Start With Cost Per Use

A higher price isn't a problem if you use the thing constantly. The math is simple: price divided by the number of times you'll realistically use it.

- A $20 gadget used twice costs $10 per use.
- A $100 pan used three times a week for a year (about 150 uses) costs well under a dollar per use, and drops further every year after.

Be honest about the number. Count what you actually cooked last month, not the meals you imagine making once the new tool arrives.

## Run the One Tool, Three Jobs Test

In a small kitchen, single-purpose items are the first to go. Before buying, name three different things the product will do for you. If you can't, it needs to be very good at its one job to justify a drawer.

Some quick examples of how to apply it:

- A large, sturdy pot that handles soup, pasta, and braising passes easily.
- A tool that only peels one kind of fruit probably doesn't.
- A good knife replaces several specialty cutters.

## Measure Before You Buy

Measuring is the least glamorous step and the one that saves the most returns. Do it for every item bigger than a hand.

1. **Measure the home it will live in.** Tape out the footprint on the counter, or measure the cabinet's depth, width, and height. Standard upper cabinets are often around 12 inches deep, so a tall appliance may not fit under them.
2. **Check the door swing and clearance.** Lids, handles, and cords need room.
3. **Decide what leaves.** If something new comes in, something similar goes out. This one rule keeps clutter from creeping back.

## Match the Material to the Job

Durability is where a pricier product often earns its cost. Look at what the item is made of and how it will wear:

- **Cookware:** Heavy bases hold heat more evenly and resist warping.
- **Utensils:** Solid one-piece construction has fewer seams where food collects and parts break.
- **Storage containers:** Glass resists stains and odors, while plastic is lighter and cheaper.
- **Knives and blades:** Stay sharp longer with decent steel, and can be resharpened rather than replaced.

A product that lasts years instead of months is the cheaper product, even when the tag says otherwise.

## Splurge and Save by Budget

You don't need premium everything. Spend where your hands and heat meet the product, and save where it matters less.

### Under $25
Start with tools you touch daily: a good spatula, a sturdy peeler, a reliable set of measuring cups. Cheap versions of these often fail first.

### $25 to $100
This range suits one workhorse piece, such as a skillet or a chef's knife. Choose one and use it for years.

### $100 and up
Reserve this tier for things you use several times a week and can't easily replace with something simpler. If you'd pull it out less than weekly, think twice.

## Use Vertical and Hidden Space

If a product passes every test but you're short on room, make space instead of skipping it. These are conceptual ideas, not a specific makeover:

- Hang frequently used pots and tools on a wall rail, which can free up a drawer.
- Add a shelf riser inside a cabinet to double usable surface.
- Stash seldom-used items on top of the fridge or cabinets, and keep daily items within arm's reach.

Storage should follow frequency. What you use every day sits at counter or eye level; what you use monthly goes up high or down low.

## Watch for These Red Flags

- It needs its own accessories, charger, or special refills.
- The main selling point is novelty.
- You'd have to clear a counter permanently for it.
- A tool you already own does 80 percent of the same job.
- You're buying it because of a photo, not a problem you have.

## Try a 30-Day Wait

For anything over about $50, put it on a list and wait a month. If you still want it, and you can name the job it solves, buy it. Impulse wants fade quickly, while real needs get louder every time you cook.

## The Takeaway

Before your next kitchen purchase, write down three things: the price per use, the three jobs it will do, and the exact spot where it will live. If you can fill in all three, it's worth the space and the money.
