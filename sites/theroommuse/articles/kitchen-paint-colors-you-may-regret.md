Before you commit to a gallon of paint, it helps to know which kitchen colors tend to cause second thoughts. House Beautiful recently rounded up six shades that designers say homeowners may regret painting their kitchens: cool gray, black, bright yellow, stark white, red, and lime green. The common thread, per the report, is that overly cold, stark, or saturated shades can leave a kitchen feeling unwelcoming, stressful, or hard to maintain.

Below, you'll find each color, the likely problem, and a practical swap. The swaps are my own suggestions, not quotes from the designers, so treat them as starting points and test before you buy.

## Why kitchen color deserves extra caution

Your kitchen does double duty. It's a workhorse where you cook and clean, and it's a social hub where people gather. A color has to look good, feel comfortable for hours at a time, and put up with daily wear. Interiors have also been moving toward warmer, softer looks, which makes harsh or high-maintenance shades feel especially risky right now.

## 1. Cool gray

Gray has been a go-to neutral for years, but cool versions can feel chilly in a room meant to be welcoming. In a kitchen with little natural light, a blue-leaning gray can read flat and a bit institutional.

**Try instead:** a warmer neutral such as greige, soft taupe, or a creamy putty. If you love gray, pick one with a hint of beige or green in it.

## 2. Black

Black is dramatic, and designers flag it as a color some people regret in the kitchen. My take: it's a lot of commitment on walls, and it tends to show every smudge, fingerprint, and splatter on cabinet doors.

**Try instead:** keep black as an accent. Matte black cabinet pulls, a black faucet, or a single painted island give you the drama without the upkeep across the whole room. For a deeper full-room look, consider charcoal with a warm undertone or a dark green.

## 3. Bright yellow

Yellow can feel cheerful in a sample swatch, but a bright, saturated version covering all your walls can become overwhelming, especially in a room where you start your mornings.

**Try instead:** a buttery, softened yellow or a warm cream with a golden undertone. You get the sunny feel without the glare. A small budget option: keep walls neutral and bring in yellow through a bowl of lemons, a tea towel, or a painted stool.

## 4. Stark white

White sounds safe, but a stark, high-contrast version can feel cold and clinical. It can also be unforgiving, since it shows splashes and scuffs quickly in a room that gets heavy use.

**Try instead:** a softer white with a little warmth. Look at off-whites with cream or a touch of gray in them, and check them beside your counters and flooring, since the wrong undertone will clash.

## 5. Red

Red is energetic, and designers say it can feel stressful in a space where you're already juggling cooking, schedules, and conversation. Large doses of it can make a kitchen feel intense rather than inviting.

**Try instead:** a muted terracotta, clay, or warm rust. These keep the earthy warmth of red with less intensity. Or limit red to small items like a mixer, a rug, or enamel cookware.

## 6. Lime green

Lime green is a very saturated shade, and it's the sort of color that can date quickly and tire the eye. Because it's so loud, it's hard to pair with other finishes.

**Try instead:** a softer sage or olive. These greens feel natural and calm and work with wood tones and brass or black hardware.

## How to test a color before you commit

These steps work at any budget, and they catch most regrets before they happen.

1. **Buy a sample pot** (usually a few dollars) instead of relying on a tiny paper chip.
2. **Paint a large swatch**, roughly 2 feet by 2 feet, on at least two walls. Or paint a sheet of poster board and move it around.
3. **Check it at different times of day.** Morning light, afternoon light, and your evening bulbs can each change how a color looks.
4. **Hold it next to fixed items** like countertops, backsplash, flooring, and appliances. Those won't change with the paint.
5. **Live with it for a few days** before you decide.

## Bold color without the regret

If you love a strong color, you don't have to give it up. Use it in a smaller, easier-to-change way:

- **Low cost:** paint the inside of an open shelf or a single wall, or swap in colorful accessories.
- **Mid-range:** paint just the island or lower cabinets in a deeper shade and keep the rest light.
- **Higher commitment:** repaint all cabinets only after testing a sample door or a hidden cabinet side first.

These are general ideas rather than a specific makeover, so adjust them to your layout, lighting, and finishes.

## The takeaway

Before you pick up a roller, paint a big sample and look at it in your kitchen at breakfast and at dinner. If it feels cold, loud, or fussy in either light, keep looking.
