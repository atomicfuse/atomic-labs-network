A plain, characterless home does not need a wrecking ball. It needs layers. That's the lesson in a Melbourne townhouse recently featured by Homestolove, where interior designer Alexandra Brownlow turned a bland 1990s semi into a richly layered, art-filled retreat that blends English country-house references with colourful maximalism.

Below, you'll find the practical takeaways from that approach, plus ways to scale each one to your budget. Where I go beyond what Homestolove reported, the ideas are conceptual suggestions, not details from Brownlow's home.

## Start With What the House Is, Not What You Wish It Were

Per Homestolove, the starting point was an ordinary 1990s semi. The transformation came through colour, pattern, antiques, contemporary art and personal objects, rather than a reinvention of the structure. That is a useful mindset if you rent, are saving for a renovation, or simply live in a neutral box.

A good first step is to decorate before you demolish. Live with the rooms for a while, add character in reversible ways, and see which problems remain. You may find the layout was fine and the walls were the issue.

## Use Bold Colour to Set the Atmosphere

The feature highlights chartreuse as one of the colours that helps transform the rooms. A strong, acid-leaning green behaves differently from a safe beige: it adds warmth and energy and makes a plain room feel deliberate.

How to try it at different budgets:

- **Low cost:** Paint one small room, such as a powder room, hallway or study. A small space lets you be brave, and a repaint is cheap if you change your mind.
- **Mid cost:** Paint a whole living room, including the woodwork, so the colour feels enveloping rather than patchy.
- **Higher cost:** Hire a painter for ceilings and detailed trim, where prep work matters most.

Before committing, paint a sample of roughly 50 x 50 cm (about 20 x 20 inches) on two different walls and look at it morning and evening. Strong colours shift a lot with light.

## Let Wallpaper Do the Heavy Lifting

Statement wallpaper is another tool the feature credits for lifting bland rooms. English country-house style leans on pattern, and wallpaper is the quickest way to get it.

- **Renter-friendly:** Peel-and-stick panels on a single wall or inside a bookcase back.
- **Budget-friendly:** Wallpaper just one feature wall, a hallway or a small bathroom, where you need only a few rolls.
- **Splurge:** A designer print across a whole room, with a plain painted ceiling to keep it from feeling heavy.

Measure height and width, then ask the supplier about pattern repeat. Matching large patterns can mean buying extra rolls, so it's worth checking before you order.

## Build an Art Wall That Feels Collected

Contemporary art is central to this home's personality. You don't need gallery-level pieces to get the effect. What matters is that the art reflects you.

Try this approach:

1. **Cut paper templates** the size of each frame and tape them to the wall. Move them around until the arrangement feels balanced.
2. **Keep about 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 inches) between frames** so the group reads as one composition.
3. **Hang the centre of the group at roughly 145 cm (57 inches) from the floor**, a common eye-level guide.
4. **Mix sizes and frame finishes.** A large piece anchors the wall; smaller ones fill around it.

On a budget, thrifted frames, prints from artists you like, children's drawings and postcards all work. If you can spend more, buy one original piece from a local artist and build outwards.

## Mix Antiques With Contemporary Pieces

The English country-house influence in Brownlow's home shows up in the mix of antiques and modern art. The combination keeps the rooms from feeling like a period set. The old pieces add a sense of history, while the new work keeps things fresh.

A few ways to do this in a city home:

- Pair one antique piece, like a wooden chest or side table, with a contemporary lamp or artwork above it.
- Shop secondhand markets, estate sales and online listings for wood furniture, which is often cheaper than new and sturdier.
- Repeat a colour from your art in an old object, such as a ceramic or a cushion, so unrelated pieces feel connected.

The goal is not matching. It's a conversation between things.

## Add Personal Objects Last

Homestolove describes the result as a lived-in city retreat, and that comes from personal objects as much as from big decisions. Books, ceramics, travel finds and family pieces make a room feel like yours.

Place them in groups rather than scattering them. Odd numbers, varied heights and a tray or stack of books to anchor a cluster usually look more intentional than single items spread across a surface.

## Keep Lighting Warm and Layered

The feature doesn't detail Brownlow's lighting, so treat this as a general tip rather than something she did. Rich colour and pattern look best with warm, low light. Use table and floor lamps at different heights instead of relying on one overhead fitting, and choose bulbs around 2700K for a cosy glow.

## A Quick Plan for This Weekend

If maximalism feels like a lot, take it in order:

1. Pick one small room.
2. Choose a single bold colour or wallpaper.
3. Hang a first group of art.
4. Bring in one antique or secondhand piece.
5. Add personal objects and warm lamps.

Maximalism, as this Melbourne home shows, is built up over time. Start with one room, one strong choice, and let the rest accumulate.
