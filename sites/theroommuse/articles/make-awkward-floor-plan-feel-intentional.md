Curved hallways, odd wings and rooms that don't line up are the layouts people most often try to hide. A Mumbai apartment profiled by Architectural Digest suggests a better move: lean into the oddity. Below, you'll find the strategies that project illustrates, plus ways to scale them to a rental, a modest budget or a full renovation.

## The Home That Started It

Per Architectural Digest, Studio PKA designed a roughly 4,000-square-foot apartment in Mumbai for a couple and their daughter, who regularly flies in from the US. The home began as a pandemic purchase and combines two apartments into one. The result is a floor plan shaped like a horseshoe.

According to the report, the architects embraced the curve rather than fighting it. They planned privacy for two generations and curated the objects, artwork and finishes from scratch, working in a calm, honey-toned palette. The horseshoe shape also carries symbolic weight as a good-luck motif, which the piece treats as part of the home's story.

That's a large, high-end project, and I'm not suggesting your hallway needs a architect. But the thinking behind it translates. The rest of this article is my own takeaway from it, not a reproduction of the designers' methods.

## Treat the Quirk as the Feature

The first move is a mindset shift. When a layout has a bend, an alcove or an uneven wing, ask what it does well before asking how to fix it.

- **A curve or bend** creates a natural reveal. You can't see the whole space at once, which makes it feel larger and more layered.
- **A long, narrow stretch** works as a gallery, a reading run or a built-in storage wall.
- **A dead-end nook** becomes a desk, a bench or a plant corner.

**Budget option:** Name each odd zone with a single purpose and style it that way. A $30 picture ledge and a lamp turn a pass-through into a destination.

## Use One Design Language Throughout

A home built from two apartments, or any layout with distinct zones, risks feeling like separate pieces. Architectural Digest describes the Mumbai home as cohesive and honey-toned. The takeaway is that repetition glues zones together.

Pick a short list and repeat it:

1. **One warm color family.** Think honey, caramel, cream and soft brown rather than five unrelated hues.
2. **Two or three materials.** For example, warm wood, a stone or plaster texture and one metal finish.
3. **One lighting temperature.** Choose bulbs of the same warmth, typically 2700K for a cozy glow, across every room.

**Budget option:** Buy a single quart sample of your main color and check it in each zone at morning and evening light before committing.

## Plan Privacy When Several Generations Share a Home

The Mumbai home was planned so two generations could live comfortably, including a daughter who visits from abroad. You don't need 4,000 square feet to give people their own space. You need separation and clear signals.

- **Distance:** Put bedrooms at opposite ends of a layout when you can, with shared living areas in the middle.
- **Buffers:** Closets, bathrooms or a hallway between sleeping areas soften noise.
- **Soft sound control:** Rugs, lined curtains and upholstered headboards absorb sound without construction.
- **Visitor zones:** Give a frequent guest a drawer, a closet section and a bedside lamp that are always theirs.

**Budget option:** A solid door sweep ($10 to $20) and a thick rug cut sound more than most people expect.

## Curate Instead of Filling

The project's objects, art and finishes were selected from scratch, per the report. That's a luxury of starting fresh, but you can borrow the discipline: choose each piece for a reason.

- Set a rule that every large item must work with your repeated palette.
- Buy fewer, better pieces first (a sofa, a dining table), and fill gaps slowly.
- Mix one meaningful or handmade piece into each room so the space feels personal, not showroom-perfect.

**Budget option:** Thrift frames, paint them one color and group them. A matched finish makes mixed art look curated.

## Give the Space a Story

The horseshoe is a traditional symbol of good luck, and Architectural Digest frames it as part of what makes this home meaningful. You can do a small version of this. Choose one motif, memory or object that matters to your household and let it recur quietly: a ceramic piece from a trip, a family textile framed on a wall, a shape repeated in mirrors or lamps.

A story gives your choices a thread, and a thread is what makes a tricky layout feel designed.

## A Conceptual Makeover to Try

This is a hypothetical exercise, not a project from the Mumbai home. Imagine a long, bent hallway connecting a living room to two bedrooms.

1. Paint the hallway walls a warm cream so the bend reads as soft rather than awkward.
2. Hang a narrow runner, roughly 2.5 feet wide, to guide the eye around the curve.
3. Add one wall sconce or plug-in lamp at the turn so the corner glows.
4. Style the widest stretch with a slim console (10 to 12 inches deep) and a mirror.

Total cost can stay in the low hundreds of dollars, and the hallway stops being a problem.

## Start Here

Walk through your home tonight and find the one spot you've been apologizing for. Give it a single purpose, a warm light and one repeated color from the rest of the house. That's the whole principle, and it works at any size.
