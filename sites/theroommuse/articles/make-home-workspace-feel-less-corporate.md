If your home workspace feels like a corner of a beige office that wandered into your living room, you can fix that without moving a wall. Architectural Digest India recently profiled five Indian homes that have become offices, and the shared lesson is simple: the best workspaces keep the warmth of a home instead of scrubbing it away.

The piece, titled "5 Indian homes that have been transformed into intimate workspaces," ranges from a minimalist retreat in Mumbai to a nostalgic, cosy studio in Chennai. Its argument, per the outlet, is that post-pandemic workspaces are moving away from sterile corporate environments toward spaces that feel personal, adaptive, and rooted in place.

The article's five entries are titled "Extending Office Hours," "Home Sweet Office," "The Charm Of A Bandra Bungalow," "Rising From The Ruins," and "Reviving The Haveli." I only have the outlet's framing and those headings, not room-by-room dimensions or materials. So below, I take the themes it highlights (light, texture, courtyards, terraces, old rooms) and translate them into steps you can try. Anything specific, like a measurement, is my suggestion, not a detail from those projects.

## Start with the room you already have

The common thread in these projects is reuse: homes gaining what the outlet calls a second life as workplaces. Before you buy anything, treat your existing room the same way.

- **Keep what gives the room character.** An old window seat, a deep sill, a built-in shelf: leave them visible instead of hiding them behind office furniture.
- **Work with the room's original purpose.** A former dining room suits a big shared table. A spare bedroom suits two or three focused desks.
- **Skip the cubicle logic.** Rows of identical desks are what makes an office feel corporate. Mix a table, a couch, and a counter-height perch.

**Budget options:** Free: rearrange what you own. Under $100: a secondhand table or a lamp. Larger spend: have a carpenter build a simple shelf into an unused alcove.

## Use light like a home, not a facility

The homes in the piece are praised for details like courtyards and terraces, which bring daylight and air into the working day. Most of us don't have a courtyard, but the principle travels.

1. **Face the desk toward or beside the window.** Sitting at a right angle to the window cuts screen glare while keeping daylight on your work.
2. **Layer three light sources.** Daylight, a desk lamp for tasks, and one warm lamp at a distance to soften the room.
3. **Choose warm bulbs.** Around 2700K to 3000K feels homey, and many people find around 4000K better for detailed tasks. Use the cooler one only on the desk.

**Budget options:** A plug-in lamp and a pair of bulbs cost little. If you can spend more, add a dimmer switch.

## Add texture to soften the screens

Hard surfaces and screens make a space feel institutional. Texture is the cheapest antidote, and it's one of the domestic details the outlet's examples lean on.

- **Underfoot:** A flat-weave or jute rug, roughly 5 by 8 feet for a single desk zone, anchors the area and dampens echo.
- **On the walls:** Fabric pinboards, framed textiles, or a wall of family photos beat blank white.
- **On the furniture:** Wood, cane, or linen-look seating warms up a laminate desk.

**Budget options:** A throw over a chair is a few dollars' worth of change. A good rug is the splurge worth saving for.

## Create a palette from the house, not a catalog

The Chennai studio is described as nostalgic and the Mumbai one as minimalist, so the five homes clearly take different roads. The takeaway is to let the building choose the palette.

- **For a minimalist feel:** Pick two or three quiet tones, such as off-white, pale wood, and one muted accent, and repeat them.
- **For a nostalgic feel:** Pull colors from things you already love, like an old rug, a family painting, or tiles.
- **Either way:** Limit yourself to three main colors plus a neutral, and the room will feel intentional.

**Budget option:** Test a sample pot on one wall for a weekend before committing to a whole room.

## Build in flexibility

The outlet describes these spaces as adaptive, and that matters at home, where the room often has to switch roles. A conceptual layout for a small room, about 10 by 10 feet, shows how:

- **Desk on the longest wall,** so the chair has room to pull out about 3 feet.
- **A shelf or console behind you** that holds supplies and can double as a display ledge after hours.
- **A rolling cart** instead of a filing cabinet, so you can tuck it away.
- **One comfortable chair** for calls, reading, or a break away from the screen.

This is a conceptual makeover for illustration, not a plan taken from any of the featured homes.

## Give the space a way to end the day

A workspace inside a home needs an off switch. Offices in houses work best when you can visually close them. Try a curtain or screen across the desk, a basket that swallows laptop and cables, or a lamp you turn off as a ritual. Small steps like these keep the comfort of home from turning into a place you can never leave.

## Why this approach is catching on

According to the piece, as hybrid work reshapes office culture, businesses are rethinking what a workplace should feel like, and many are choosing comfort, heritage, and human scale over glass-tower uniformity. In dense Indian cities, the outlet adds, adaptive reuse of homes also offers a design response to sustainability, preservation, and changing work habits. You don't need to restore a haveli to benefit from the same thinking: reusing and reworking what's already there is kinder to your budget and your space.

## Your first move

This week, move your desk next to the best light in the room, add one warm lamp and one textured thing, and see how your workday feels. If it works, build from there.
