A newer or freshly renovated home can look right on paper and still feel a little cold. If that sounds familiar, a recent project from designer Emily Ruff offers a useful playbook. Per Homes & Gardens, Ruff took a stripped-back 1926 Seattle home and turned it into a warm, characterful sanctuary built for hosting. Below, you'll find the repeatable moves behind that approach, plus ways to scale each one to your budget.

A quick note on scope: the details we have from the original report are about the overall approach, not exact dimensions, paint names or prices. So the budget tiers and measurements below are my own suggestions, clearly labeled, not specifics from Ruff's project.

## What Ruff Did, in Short

According to Homes & Gardens, the home's charm was restored through a handful of ingredients: architectural details, English-inspired decorating, floral wallpapers, warm woods, brass, stone and soft yellow tones. The goal was continuity, so the house feels like one cohesive place rather than a series of unrelated rooms.

That last point is the real lesson. None of these elements is exotic. The warmth comes from repeating them.

## Start With Architecture, Even Small Touches

The report highlights architectural details as a core part of the restoration. If your home lacks them, you can suggest them.

- **Low budget:** Add picture-frame molding or simple trim around a doorway. Paint the trim and wall in closely related tones so it looks original rather than applied.
- **Mid budget:** Swap flat, builder-grade door casings for deeper, more substantial ones. (A casing in the 3 to 5 inch range is a common starting point; confirm what suits your doors.)
- **Bigger budget:** Have a contractor add an arched opening between rooms. Arches are a classic marker of older homes and soften straight-edged layouts.

If you rent or can't alter walls, an arched mirror or arched bookcase gives a nod to the same shape.

## Use Floral Wallpaper Where It Will Be Noticed

Floral wallpaper is a signature of English-inspired rooms, and Ruff's project leans on it. Pattern is what makes a space feel collected rather than newly finished.

Place it where people pause: a powder room, a dining nook, the inside of a bookcase or a entry hall. In a small space, one patterned room feels deliberate, while patterning every wall can overwhelm.

- **Low budget:** Peel-and-stick paper on one wall or inside shelving. Measure the width and height, then add roughly 10 to 15 percent for pattern matching and mistakes.
- **Higher budget:** Traditional pasted paper, ideally hung by a pro, for a more durable and authentic finish.

For a calmer look, choose a floral with a muted background instead of high contrast.

## Warm Materials Do the Heavy Lifting

The project pairs warm woods, brass and stone. Together they create the layered, lived-in feel that stark renovations often lack.

Try this simple formula, my own suggestion rather than Ruff's: one wood, one metal, one stone, repeated across rooms.

- **Wood:** a dining table, open shelving or a butcher-block counter.
- **Brass:** cabinet pulls, a picture light or a lamp base. Unlacquered or aged finishes look especially at home in older-style rooms.
- **Stone:** a marble-topped side table, a stone vanity top or even a small tray.

On a budget, thrift stores and secondhand marketplaces are rich in wooden furniture and brass lamps. Vintage pieces also bring the sense of age that new items can't fake.

## Use Soft Yellow Without Overdoing It

Soft yellow is part of the palette Homes & Gardens describes. It reads sunny without shouting, and it pairs naturally with wood and brass.

To keep it gentle:

1. Pick a buttery or creamy yellow rather than a bright lemon. Test large swatches (at least 12 by 12 inches) and check them morning and evening.
2. Use it as one layer, such as walls, or curtains, or a single upholstered piece, not all three.
3. Balance it with warm neutrals like cream, oatmeal and wood tones.

If you're nervous, start with textiles: a yellow lampshade, pillows or a runner cost little and are easy to retire.

## Repeat Elements for Continuity

This is the move that ties everything together. Ruff's home works because its details echo from room to room. You can do the same:

- Carry the same brass finish through every room.
- Repeat one wood tone in furniture, frames and shelving.
- Let a color from the wallpaper reappear in a pillow, a rug or a painted ceiling elsewhere.

This matters most in a small home, where rooms are visible from each other. Repetition makes the space feel intentional and larger.

## Light It Like an Older Home

The report doesn't detail Ruff's lighting, so treat this as general advice. Older-feeling rooms tend to use several low sources instead of one bright overhead: a table lamp, a wall sconce, a picture light over art. Aim for warm bulbs, typically around 2700K, and use dimmers where you can. Hosting, which this home was designed for, is far more inviting under soft light.

## A Conceptual Starter Plan

Here's a labeled, hypothetical makeover for a plain dining room, not Ruff's project:

- **Weekend, under a modest budget:** Paint walls a soft yellow, add a thrifted wood table and a brass lamp.
- **Next step:** Wallpaper the inside of a built-in or one wall with a muted floral.
- **Later:** Add trim or an arched opening to make the room feel original to the house.

## The Takeaway

Pick one pattern, one warm wood, one metal and one soft color, then repeat them in every room you touch. Character comes from that repetition far more than from any single expensive change.
