Designer Ken Fulk has reimagined a historic Healdsburg vineyard estate once tied to actor Fred MacMurray, according to Veranda. He turned a group of mismatched buildings into one cohesive multigenerational family retreat, blending Old Hollywood glamour, Old California ranch character, and layered natural materials.

You probably don't have a wine country compound. But the thinking behind it translates to a spare bedroom, a living room, or a rental. Below are the lessons you can use, with options at different budgets. The examples are conceptual suggestions inspired by the project, not details from the estate itself.

## Start with the story of the place

Per Veranda, Fulk's approach turns history into atmosphere. The estate's past, as a ranch with a Hollywood connection, shaped the mood rather than being treated as trivia.

You can do the same at home. Before buying anything, write down three facts about your space or your life: the house's era, the region you live in, a place your family loves. Those become your filter. If a lamp or a rug doesn't fit any of the three, skip it.

- **Free:** Choose your three words and tape them inside a closet door.
- **Low budget:** Frame a local map, an old photo of your street, or a family snapshot.
- **Bigger budget:** Commission a piece from a local maker that references your area.

## Unify mismatched spaces with a limited material list

The estate's buildings didn't match, and the design made them feel like one place through layered natural materials. That's a trick for any home with rooms that feel unrelated.

Pick three to four materials and repeat them in every room. A concept for a typical home:

- Weathered wood (a shelf, a frame, a bench)
- Linen or cotton in warm neutral tones
- Aged metal such as brass or iron
- Stone, clay, or ceramic

Repeat each one at least once per room, even in small doses. A 12-inch ceramic bowl in the hallway and a larger one in the living room do more to connect the spaces than matching paint.

## Borrow Old Hollywood glamour in small doses

Old Hollywood doesn't have to mean sequins and mirrors everywhere. In this project, glamour sits alongside ranch character and understated luxury, per the coverage. The lesson is restraint: pick one glamorous element per room and let everything else stay relaxed.

Some ways to do that:

- **Lighting:** Swap one overhead fixture for a table or sconce pair with warm bulbs (around 2700K) and put them on a dimmer. Soft, low light is the fastest route to a cinematic mood.
- **One luxe surface:** A velvet pillow, a brass tray, or a good mirror against otherwise earthy textures.
- **A statement moment:** One bar cart, one dramatic piece of art, or one standout chair.

When glamour is the only note in a room, it feels staged. When it's one note among worn wood and soft linen, it feels lived in.

## Source vintage and antiques so rooms look collected

Veranda highlights the collected antiques at the estate, and that's the clearest difference between a room that feels decorated and one that feels gathered over time. New furniture bought in one trip tends to read as a set. Pieces from different eras read as a life.

A workable ratio for a living room, again as a concept: aim for roughly one-third vintage or secondhand, with the rest new. Start with items that carry little risk:

- **Under $50:** Frames, lamps, trays, ceramics, and books from thrift stores and estate sales
- **$50 to $300:** A side table, a bench, or a vintage rug from a flea market or online marketplace
- **Higher budgets:** An antique case piece or a good armchair worth reupholstering

Buy slowly. A room that takes a year to fill will look better than one filled in a weekend.

## Design for gathering, not just looking

The retreat was designed for several generations of one family, which changes how you plan a layout. Think about who sits where and how people move.

Practical layout checks for a living room:

- Leave about 30 to 36 inches for main walking paths.
- Keep seating within about 8 feet of each other so conversation works without raised voices.
- Include at least one flexible piece, like an ottoman or a pair of stools, that can move to where guests need it.
- Make sure every seat has a surface within reach for a drink.

These are general layout guidelines, not specifics from the project. They help any room, including a small one, host more people comfortably.

## Let the outdoors anchor the house

Landscape integration is one of the angles Veranda's coverage points to, and it matters even if your yard is a balcony. A home feels rooted when inside and outside share a palette and materials.

Pull colors from what you see through your windows: olive greens, dusty golds, bark browns. Then echo them inside. On a small scale, that could mean:

- A row of potted herbs or olive-type plants on a sill or balcony rail
- Natural-fiber rugs and baskets that match outdoor textures
- Furniture placed so the best window view is the focal point of the room

## A simple way to try this in one room

Here's a weekend plan, conceptual and scalable to your budget:

1. Choose your three story words.
2. Pick three or four repeating materials.
3. Remove anything that doesn't fit either list.
4. Add one glamorous element, such as a dimmable lamp or a good mirror.
5. Add two vintage finds and one plant that echoes the view.

The lasting lesson from Fulk's work is that atmosphere comes from decisions about meaning, not from spending. This week, pick one object in your home that has a real story and build the corner around it.
