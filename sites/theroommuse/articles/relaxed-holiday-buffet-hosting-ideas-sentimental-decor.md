Holiday hosting gets stiff when everything is styled to within an inch of its life. You can loosen it up this season with a few moves borrowed from Isabelle d'Ornano, co-founder of Sisley Paris, who talked to Elle about hosting, family traditions, and her beloved embroidered cushions. By the end of this piece, you'll have a practical plan for a warmer, more personal holiday table and living room, with options at every budget.

A note on sourcing: the details about d'Ornano below come from Elle's profile, which describes a maximalist Paris home, self-serve buffets, and mixed guest lists as part of a more intimate, rule-breaking idea of elegance. The measurements, steps, and budget suggestions are my own practical translations, not her instructions. Treat them as a conceptual starting point, not a recreation of her home.

## Choose a self-serve buffet over a formal sit-down

Elle highlights d'Ornano's self-serve buffets as part of her relaxed style of entertaining. It's the most transferable idea here, and it solves real problems: no one is stuck at the stove, and guests eat what and when they want.

### Set it up in a small space

- **Use what you have.** A console, a dresser top, or a kitchen counter works. Aim for roughly 30 to 36 inches of surface for a modest spread; a standard dining table gives you far more.
- **Pull it off the wall if you can.** Letting guests approach from two sides stops a line from forming.
- **Build height.** Stack a few sturdy books under a platter or use a cake stand so dishes don't all sit at one level.
- **Group by course.** Plates and napkins first, mains in the middle, sides after, cutlery last so no one juggles.

### Budget options

- **Low:** Borrow platters, use bread boards, and serve from the pots you cooked in on trivets.
- **Mid:** Add two or three cake stands or risers from a thrift shop.
- **Higher:** Invest in one large serving table or a sideboard that works year-round.

## Let sentimental objects do the decorating

The cushions are the star of the Elle piece: deeply personal, embroidered, and part of a home that reads as lived-in rather than curated. You don't need a Paris apartment to borrow the logic. Objects with a story do more work than anything bought for a single season.

### Start with textiles

Cushions are the easiest place to begin because they're soft, movable, and quick to swap.

- **Standard sizes:** 18 or 20 inches square suits most sofas. For a standard three-seat sofa, three to five cushions look generous without crowding.
- **Mix, don't match.** Combine an heirloom or handmade piece with plain linen or wool in one or two shared colors so the collection feels intentional.
- **Material matters.** Tightly woven cotton, linen, or wool holds up to guests leaning on it. Delicate embroidery is better on the cushion that gets less wear, or cover the back with sturdy fabric.

### Budget options

- **Low:** Pull a vintage tablecloth, a scarf, or a piece of inherited fabric and wrap it around an inexpensive cushion insert, pinned or hand-stitched at the back.
- **Mid:** Buy plain cushion covers and add simple embroidery yourself: initials, a date, or a pattern from a family memory.
- **Higher:** Commission a custom embroidered cover from a local maker.

## Think warm and layered, not perfect

Elle describes d'Ornano's home as maximalist. If your space is small, a scaled version works as long as you edit by grouping rather than scattering.

- **Cluster, don't sprinkle.** Put framed photos, candlesticks, and a few books on one surface so they read as a collection.
- **Light low.** Lamps and candles at table or shelf height feel friendlier than overhead lighting. If you have a dimmer, set it low for the evening.
- **Stick to a tight palette.** Two or three colors, such as deep green, cream, and a touch of red, let lots of objects coexist without chaos.

## Build the guest list on chemistry

The Elle profile points to mixed guest lists as part of d'Ornano's approach to hosting. The takeaway is that the people matter more than the polish. In practice, that can mean inviting a neighbor, a cousin, and a coworker who would genuinely enjoy each other, rather than sorting by obligation.

A few ways to make mixed groups work in a compact home:

- **Create two or three seating spots**, not one big circle, so conversation can split and re-form.
- **Give people a job.** Handing someone the playlist or the drinks makes a newcomer feel part of things.
- **Keep food forgiving.** Dishes that taste good warm or at room temperature let you stay at the party.

## Make one tradition that lasts

Family traditions are central to the Elle piece, and the useful lesson is that traditions stick when they're simple and repeatable. Pick one small thing and do it every year: the same cushion on the same chair, the same dish on the buffet, a handwritten date stitched or inked somewhere discreet.

If you're starting from scratch, choose something you can afford and manage even in a busy year. A ritual that needs a weekend of prep won't survive; one that takes ten minutes will.

## A simple weekend plan

1. **Friday evening:** Gather sentimental textiles and objects from around the house. Decide on your two or three colors.
2. **Saturday:** Clear a buffet surface, test the traffic flow, and place lamps or candles.
3. **Before guests arrive:** Set out plates, napkins, and cutlery in order, and pick your one tradition.

The point isn't to copy a Paris matriarch's home. It's to host with the confidence that a warm, slightly imperfect room full of things you love is already enough. This season, take one object with a story and put it where people will actually sit.
