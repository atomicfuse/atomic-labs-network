Industrial rooms look sharp in photos and feel chilly in person. If yours has metal, glass, or concrete and you want it to feel more like a place to linger over dinner, Jennifer Aniston's dining room offers a useful template.

Per Homes & Gardens, her Beverly Hills dining room, designed by Stephen Shadley, pairs exposed bulbs and sleek windows with natural materials, and the result is a cocooning, moody space. The outlet points to wood, stone, rugs, velvet, candles, and warm lighting as the ways raw industrial materials get balanced. Below, those ideas become a plan you can follow at different budgets.

A note on what follows: the materials list comes from the reporting. The measurements, prices, and step-by-step choices are my own conceptual suggestions for adapting the look, not details of Aniston's room.

## Start With the Rule: One Hard Material Needs One Soft Partner

The simplest takeaway from this room is balance. Industrial design leans on raw, cool surfaces. Warmth comes from pairing each one with something natural or soft.

- Metal frames or fixtures: pair with wood
- Large glass or windows: pair with fabric, such as velvet
- Concrete or hard floors: pair with a rug
- Bare, bright light: pair with warm bulbs and candles

Walk your dining room and check each hard surface against that list. Wherever a pairing is missing, you've found your first purchase.

## Fix the Lighting First (It's the Cheapest Change)

Exposed bulbs are part of the industrial look in this room, and they work because the light itself feels warm. The fix costs very little.

### Budget: under $30
Swap cool white bulbs for warm ones. As a general guideline, look for bulbs labeled around 2200K to 2700K, which give an amber glow. Filament-style bulbs keep the exposed look while softening the tone.

### Mid-range: $50 to $150
Put your dining light on a dimmer. Being able to drop the light low at dinner is what makes a room feel moody rather than just dark. Check that your bulbs are dimmable.

### Add candles
Candles are on the list of warming elements for a reason. Set a few at different heights down the center of the table, such as three taper holders between 8 and 12 inches tall, so flame light sits below eye level and the overhead fixture can stay dim.

## Bring In Wood and Stone

Natural materials do the heavy lifting against metal and glass.

### Wood
If you're not replacing your table, add wood in smaller ways: a cutting board or tray as a centerpiece base, wooden chairs or stools, or a wood-framed mirror or shelf. If you are shopping for a table, a visible grain with a medium to dark finish suits a moody palette.

### Stone
Stone adds weight and a cool-but-organic texture. You don't need a stone tabletop. A marble or travertine tray, a stone bowl, or a pair of heavy candleholders brings the same effect for a small spend. A sideboard with a stone top is the bigger investment.

## Soften the Floor and Windows With a Rug and Velvet

### The rug
A rug is one of the clearest ways to take the chill off a hard floor. For sizing, a common rule is that it should extend at least 24 inches past the table edge on every side, so chairs stay on the rug when pulled out. For a 36-by-72-inch table, that means roughly an 84-by-120-inch rug, or an 8-by-10 as the nearest standard size.

Choose a low-pile or flat-weave style for under dining chairs so they slide easily and crumbs are manageable. Wool or wool-blend feels warmest underfoot, while a washable synthetic costs less and handles spills.

### The velvet
Velvet is the soft counterpoint to sleek glass. You have a few scalable options:

- **Lowest cost:** velvet cushion covers or chair pads in a deep tone
- **Mid-range:** velvet curtains hung high and wide, which also frames large windows
- **Bigger project:** reupholster or replace dining chairs

Deep, saturated shades suit the moody feel better than pastels. Think forest green, burgundy, or charcoal, though that palette is my suggestion rather than a reported detail.

## Build a Moodier Palette Without Repainting

You can darken a room's feel without picking up a roller. Let the textiles, wood tones, and candlelight do the work, and keep metal and glass as accents instead of the main event. If you do want paint, a deep shade on a single wall, say the one behind the table, gives the cocooning effect without making a small room feel heavy.

## A Weekend Plan by Budget

### Under $100
- Warm dimmable bulbs
- Three taper candles and holders
- Velvet cushion or chair pad covers
- A wood tray for the table

### $100 to $500
- Everything above, plus a properly sized wool-blend or flat-weave rug
- Velvet curtains
- A stone bowl or marble tray

### $500 and up
- A dimmer installed on the dining fixture
- Wooden or velvet-upholstered chairs
- A wood or stone-topped table or sideboard

## Why This Look Fits Fall

Per the broader design conversation around this story, people are leaning away from cool, polished minimalism toward textured, lived-in rooms. As the days shorten, that shift is easy to feel at home: candles, rugs, and velvet make a room friendlier at 6 p.m. when the windows go dark.

## The One Move to Make Tonight

Change the bulbs over your dining table to warm ones, light a few candles, and sit down for dinner. You'll learn in one meal how much of your room's chill came from the light, and which soft material to add next.
