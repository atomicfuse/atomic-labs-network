Every year, twin pastors James and Chad Hampton fill their freezer with venison. It's a simple habit, but it says a lot about how they live. They recently told Fox News Digital how their passion for hunting connects to their faith, their food choices, and their commitment to physical and spiritual well-being.

This isn't a story about a trophy or a sport. It's about what happens when you put your own hands to the work of feeding your family, and what that can teach you about gratitude.

## Two Brothers, One Freezer

James and Chad Hampton are twins, and both are pastors. According to Fox News Digital, they see hunting and filling the freezer with venison as part of a lifestyle that supports both body and spirit.

I can't give you more detail than that about their particular hunts, because the report as summarized here doesn't spell out the specifics. What it does make clear is the shape of the idea: the work of the outdoors and the work of faith aren't kept in separate boxes for them.

## From Field to Freezer

There's a phrase that fits their approach well: field to freezer. The meat on the table has a visible beginning. Someone rose early, spent time outside, did the work, and brought something home.

Most of us never see that chain. Food arrives wrapped in plastic, and we rarely stop to wonder how it got there. A way of life that keeps the whole story in view naturally tends toward thankfulness.

## Why Venison Appeals

The Hamptons' choice also touches on food. Venison is often seen as an alternative to highly processed, store-bought meat, and interest in cleaner eating and self-sufficiency keeps growing. For families trying to know more about what they eat, filling their own freezer is one way to do it.

That's the appeal here for many readers, even if you never pick up a hunting license. You may not hunt, but you can ask the same question: do I know where my food comes from, and am I grateful for it?

## Stewardship, Discipline, and Gratitude

Here is my own reflection, not a claim from the report. Hunting asks for patience, early mornings, and respect for the land and the animal. Those are close cousins of the habits many people of faith try to practice: showing up, waiting, and taking care of what has been entrusted to them.

For a pastor, the outdoors can serve as a quiet sanctuary. Many believers find it easier to notice small wonders when they're away from screens and schedules, and to see provision as a gift rather than a given.

## Well-Being Beyond the Plate

The Hamptons describe their outdoor lifestyle as supporting both physical and spiritual well-being. That pairing makes sense. Walking miles, staying alert, and spending hours in stillness are good for the body. The same quiet can be good for the soul.

If you want to borrow the spirit of their story without borrowing the rifle, here are a few doable ideas:

- Spend one morning a month outdoors with no phone, just listening.
- Learn where one item on your dinner table came from, and say a real thank-you before the meal.
- Invite a family member into a shared task, like cooking a meal from scratch, so the work becomes time together.
- Ask a neighbor who hunts, fishes, or gardens to teach you something small.

## What the Freezer Really Holds

A freezer full of venison is, on the surface, just food for the months ahead. But for two brothers who spend their lives shepherding others, it seems to hold something more: proof that provision is received with thanks, and that a life of faith can be lived with muddy boots as well as open hands.

The truth underneath it is an old one. What we work for and are thankful for tastes different from what we merely consume.
