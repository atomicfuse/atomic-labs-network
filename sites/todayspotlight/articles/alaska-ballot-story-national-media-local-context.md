When a national outlet drops into a local story, what gets left out can matter as much as what gets reported. That's the argument in a recent opinion piece published by the Anchorage Daily News (ADN), which takes aim at a New York Times podcast episode about Alaska Democrat Mary Peltola. By the end of this article, you'll know what the column claims, what remains allegation, and a few habits for judging any parachuted-in political reporting.

One caution first: this is an opinion piece, and what follows is its argument, not established fact. We haven't independently verified its claims, and the allegations involving Peltola are described here only as the column describes them.

## What the Column Is Responding To

Per the ADN opinion piece, The New York Times' "The Daily" podcast ran an investigation into Peltola's alleged involvement in placing a decoy candidate on the ballot. The candidate in question is Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., whose name resembles that of incumbent Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan.

The column's author doesn't present this as a minor quibble. The piece is headlined as giving the Times the "Fox News Unfair and Unbalanced Award," and its description says the journalists "have allowed half of the story to elude them," the half the author says most Alaskans know and Lower 48 reporters should too.

The piece appears as Alaska's 2026 Senate race heats up, which is part of why national outlets are paying closer attention to state politics.

## The Core Complaint: A Story With Only Half the Context

The column's central claim is about selective framing. The author argues the podcast focused on the name-similarity angle while overlooking bigger decoy-candidate stories closer to home.

The example the column leans on is Eric Hafner. According to the piece, Hafner is imprisoned in New York, his name is largely unknown, and he represented an actual ballot threat. The author argues that story was ignored in favor of the Sullivan name-similarity angle.

If you're weighing that critique, here's the useful question it raises: when a story is about a tactic, did the reporter check whether the tactic is unusual, or just whether it's present? A trick that looks damning in isolation can look different next to other examples. Equally, other examples don't automatically excuse the first one. Both things can be true.

## Do Different Candidates Get Different Standards?

The column also asks whether "dirty tricks" coverage depends on who's accused. That's the author's framing, and it's a take, not a finding. But it's a fair test you can run yourself.

When you read a story about ballot confusion, name-matching or similar tactics, ask:

- Would this story run if the candidate's party were reversed?
- Does the piece mention comparable cases from other candidates?
- Does it separate what's documented from what's alleged?

A story that can't answer those questions isn't necessarily wrong. It's just incomplete.

## Alaska's Ballot Is Genuinely Unusual

The column argues that national reporters don't grasp Alaska's quirks. Its example: in 2022, Peltola beat Sarah Palin and Santa Claus on the ballot. It's a vivid reminder that Alaska elections can include candidates and names outsiders wouldn't expect, and that context shapes how a decoy-candidate claim should be read.

The piece also says reporters misread local behavior, with generosity and hospitality framed as suspicious. It additionally points to what it describes as basic reporting lapses, including fact-checking gas prices and missing a nearby convict candidate. We can't confirm those specifics from the column alone, so treat them as the author's charges.

## How to Read Parachute Journalism

You don't have to take a side in the Peltola dispute to take something useful from this debate. A few habits help with any outside-in story about a place you don't know:

1. **Look for local reaction.** Do residents, local reporters or officials appear, or only outsiders?
2. **Check for comparisons.** Does the story show how common or rare the behavior is?
3. **Note attribution.** Phrases like "alleged" and "according to" matter. Claims presented as settled should be backed.
4. **Read the rebuttals.** An opinion column like this one is itself one-sided by design, so read it next to the original reporting, not instead of it.

That last point applies here too. The ADN column critiques the podcast for missing half the story, while being openly an argument rather than neutral reporting. Listen to the episode, read the column, and decide where they agree and where they don't.

## The Takeaway

When a political story hinges on a tactic, ask what the reporter compared it to. Then go find the half the story may have skipped.
