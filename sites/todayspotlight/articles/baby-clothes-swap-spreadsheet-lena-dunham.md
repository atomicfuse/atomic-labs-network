Lena Dunham has a spreadsheet for her vintage baby clothes. According to The Cut, which originally reported the story, she tracks vintage and thrifted baby items organized by age, and the list is shared among a parent "trading club." The Cut frames the approach as quirky, relatable, and part of a broader upcycled parenting habit.

It's a funny detail. It's also a genuinely good idea. Babies outgrow clothes fast, and a shared list is a simple way to keep wearable things moving instead of sitting in a bin. Here's how to borrow the concept for your own friend group, using the pieces the report describes: a spreadsheet, sorting by age, and a group of parents who trade.

## Start With the One Rule From the Report: Sort by Age

The detail we know about Dunham's setup is that it's organized by age. That's the right backbone, because age is the first thing any parent checks. A cute jacket in the wrong size is just clutter.

Create one tab or one section per age range. Pick ranges that match how baby clothes are typically sized, such as newborn, 0-3 months, 3-6 months, and so on. Keep them consistent so nobody has to translate.

## Set Up the Columns

The report doesn't spell out Dunham's columns, so what follows is our suggestion, not her system. A simple layout works best:

- **Item:** "Striped romper," "corduroy overalls"
- **Size/age range:** matching the tab it lives on
- **Condition:** like new, gently used, well loved
- **Owner:** who currently has it
- **Status:** available, requested, or in use

Five columns is enough. The more fields you add, the less likely people are to fill them in.

## Recruit a Small Trading Club

Dunham's list reportedly circulates among a group of parents, so the sharing is what makes it work. Start with three to six friends whose kids are at different ages. That spread matters: your outgrown 6-month clothes are exactly what a friend with a younger baby needs.

Set expectations upfront in a short group message:

1. Anyone can add items they're ready to pass on.
2. Claim something by marking it "requested" in the Status column.
3. Pass it along when your baby outgrows it, rather than returning it to the original owner.

That last rule keeps things from becoming a lending library with a bookkeeping burden.

## Make It Easy to Keep Up

Spreadsheets only work if they stay current. A few habits help:

- **Update when you do laundry.** That's when you notice what no longer fits.
- **Pick one person to tidy the sheet** every month or so, deleting claimed items.
- **Use a shared link** that works on a phone, since most updates will happen one-handed.

This is the "spreadsheet friend" energy in action. Someone in every group enjoys organizing, and a trading club gives them a purpose that everyone else benefits from.

## Add Vintage and Thrifted Finds

The Cut's piece centers on vintage and thrifted baby clothes, which is the other half of the idea. If you shop secondhand, log those pieces too, so they get a second and third life once your child outgrows them.

A few practical tips when shopping for vintage kids' items:

- Check seams, snaps, and fabric wear before buying.
- Wash everything before the first wear.
- Note any quirks in the Condition column so the next family knows what to expect.

## Why This Idea Has Legs

Secondhand shopping and parent-to-parent sharing are increasingly mainstream as families look to cut costs and reduce waste. That's our read of the broader trend, and it's easy to see the appeal: baby clothes are worn briefly, so passing them on is practical and budget-friendly.

The celebrity angle is what turned this into a conversation. A famous person with a color-coded system for baby clothes is inherently a little funny. But the underlying habit doesn't need a famous name, a vintage budget, or fancy software. It needs a shared document and a few friends willing to use it.

## Your Next Step

Open a blank spreadsheet today, make one tab per age range, and send the link to two parents you know. Add three outgrown items yourself so the list isn't empty. A swap club starts the moment someone else adds a line.
