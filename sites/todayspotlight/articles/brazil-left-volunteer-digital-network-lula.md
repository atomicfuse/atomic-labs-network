For three weeks in October, Veridiana Cunha became a so-called "soldier" in a digital army working to reelect Brazil's leftist President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. That's the opening of a CNN report on how Brazil's left took tactics long associated with the political right and used them to help Lula win.

If you've ever wondered how ordinary people end up powering a national campaign from their phones, this is a useful case study. Below: what the report says, what it suggests about volunteer-driven politics, and how you can read online campaigns more clearly the next time one lands in your feed.

## What CNN Reported

The core claim, per CNN, is simple: Brazil's left reinvented the right's online playbook, and it helped Lula win. Rather than leaving digital mobilization to opponents, the left-wing coalition adopted similar methods, including organizing grassroots volunteers it calls "soldiers" into online campaigns.

Cunha is the human face of that approach. By CNN's account, she spent three weeks in October as one of those soldiers, part of a digital army rather than a traditional party staff.

I'm limited here to what CNN's reporting establishes. The details of exactly which platforms were used, how many volunteers took part, or how much any single tactic mattered to the result are beyond what I can verify, so I won't guess at them. For the full account, read the original CNN piece.

## Why the "Soldier" Model Is Worth Understanding

The word "soldier" is doing a lot of work, and it's worth pausing on. It frames campaigning less as a job and more as a cause you enlist in. That's our read, not CNN's, but it fits a wider pattern: volunteers who feel like members of a movement tend to be more motivated than people who feel like targets of ads.

That's the appeal of decentralized organizing. Instead of every message flowing from a central headquarters, supporters amplify, share, and defend a campaign in their own networks. A message from a neighbor or a coworker often lands differently than one from a candidate.

## Borrowing From the Other Side

The most interesting part of the story is the borrowing. Political movements rarely invent tactics from scratch. They watch what works for opponents, strip out the ideology, and rebuild the machinery for their own side.

CNN's framing suggests Brazil's left did exactly that. It's a reminder that digital strategy isn't owned by any one party or worldview. A playbook that looks like one camp's advantage can become the other camp's blueprint within an election cycle or two.

This is analysis rather than reporting, but it carries a practical point: when one side seems to dominate online, the gap often closes faster than people expect.

## How to Spot Organized Online Campaigns in Your Own Feed

You don't need to follow Brazilian politics to benefit from this story. Volunteer-driven digital campaigns now exist in almost every democracy, and a few habits help you read them.

### Look for repeated phrasing

When many different accounts share nearly identical wording, hashtags, or graphics within a short window, that's usually coordination. Coordination isn't automatically sinister. Volunteers often get shared talking points. But it tells you the enthusiasm may be organized, not purely spontaneous.

### Check whether the account is a real person

Volunteer networks are made of real people like Cunha, who by CNN's account joined openly as a supporter. Other accounts may be automated or anonymous. A visible history, a real name, and normal posting about non-political topics are good signs, though not proof.

### Separate passion from proof

A message shared a thousand times isn't more true than one shared ten times. Before you repost something, ask where the claim started and whether a credible outlet has confirmed it.

### Notice what the content asks you to do

Some posts inform. Others recruit. If a post invites you to join a group, share on a schedule, or "defend" a candidate, you're being asked to become a soldier yourself. You're free to say yes, but it helps to know that's the invitation.

## What Other Countries Might Take From It

CNN's report is about Brazil, and I won't stretch it into a prediction for any other election. Still, it's reasonable to wonder what campaigns elsewhere will learn from it. A few questions seem worth asking:

- Do volunteers get real roles, or just a list of posts to share?
- Can a campaign keep enthusiasm up for weeks, as Cunha did, without burning people out?
- Does the online energy translate into offline action, like talking to neighbors and showing up to vote?

Those are questions, not findings. The report's central lesson is the one that travels: the tools of digital persuasion are available to anyone willing to learn them.

## The Takeaway

Next time you see a wave of passionate posts about a candidate, take ten seconds to ask who organized it, who's behind each account, and what you're being asked to do. Then decide whether to join in, scroll past, or read the original reporting first.
