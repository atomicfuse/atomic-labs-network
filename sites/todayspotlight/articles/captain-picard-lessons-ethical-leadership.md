A Boston Globe piece published this week, written by two longtime Star Trek fans, looks at how Patrick Stewart's Captain Jean-Luc Picard became more than a sci-fi icon. Its title says it plainly: through Picard, Stewart "taught us the measure of a man." The idea is worth borrowing. Below, you'll find a way to think about why Picard still works as a model for principled adulthood, and what you can take from him into your own work and relationships.

I'm working only from the Globe's framing here, so this isn't a scene-by-scene breakdown. Think of it as a guide to the qualities the piece highlights and how they play out in everyday life.

## The Core Idea: Picard as a Teacher

Per the Globe, the authors argue that Stewart made Picard a teacher, a "Space Dad," and an enduring blueprint for principled adulthood. That's a different claim from "he was a great captain." Plenty of TV leaders are competent. The argument is that Picard modeled how to be a grown-up: how to hold a position, how to admit doubt, and how to treat people well while doing it.

That's the lens worth keeping. When you rewatch an episode, ask what Picard is showing you about conduct rather than what happens in the plot.

## Three Qualities the Globe Highlights

The piece describes Picard as a model of moral clarity, vulnerability, and humane leadership. Each one is something you can practice.

### Moral clarity

Picard is remembered as a character who knows what he believes and acts on it. The practical takeaway: decide your non-negotiables before the pressure hits. People with clear lines make faster, calmer decisions, and others know where they stand with them.

### Vulnerability

The Globe credits Stewart with making Picard emotionally honest, not just authoritative. That combination is the interesting part. Strength and openness are often treated as opposites, and Picard is a long-running argument that they aren't. If you manage people or raise kids, admitting what you don't know is not a loss of authority. It's often how you earn it.

### Humane leadership

A "Space Dad" doesn't win by being the loudest voice on the bridge. The nickname points to care: someone who looks out for the people under him while still expecting their best. In your own life, that looks like setting high standards and being generous with the people trying to meet them.

## Why We Still Crave Characters Like This

The Globe frames Picard's appeal as newly relevant in a polarized era hungry for trustworthy leadership and emotional honesty. That's the paper's argument, and it's a reasonable one. My own take: a lot of modern TV is built around flawed antiheroes, so a leader who is plainly admirable can feel almost radical. Comfort characters work because we trust them. You don't have to wonder whether Picard will do the right thing. The drama comes from how hard it is.

That's also why audiences keep returning to legacy characters. The Globe notes that these figures shaped many viewers' ideas of masculinity, duty, and integrity, so revisiting them is partly a way of checking in with the values you absorbed.

## How to Use Picard as a Personal Checklist

Here are a few practical ways to turn the idea into something you can use.

- **Before a hard conversation,** write down the one principle you won't compromise on. Start there.
- **When you're wrong,** say so plainly and early. Treat it as part of leading, not a failure of it.
- **When someone on your team is struggling,** ask how they're doing before you ask about the deadline.
- **When you rewatch,** pick one episode and note a single behavior you'd like to copy. Keep it small enough to actually do.

## A Note on Stewart

The Globe's central point is that this lasting impact belongs to Stewart as much as to the writing. A character can be well designed on paper and still fall flat. According to the piece, Stewart's performance is what turned Picard into a teacher for a generation of viewers. If you want to honor that, the best way is probably to watch with attention to the small moments: a pause, a look, a decision to listen first.

## The Takeaway

Pick one Picard trait, whether that's clarity, honesty about doubt, or care for your crew, and practice it this week. The measure of a man, as the Globe's headline has it, is something you show through daily choices rather than speeches.
