The Justice Department is asking a federal judge to sanction an immigrant rights group and its lawyers over a data analysis of the department's own list of alleged noncitizen voters in California. By the end of this piece, you'll know what the analysis reportedly found, what the dispute is about, and how to read the competing claims without getting lost.

The account below comes from PBS reporting. Details beyond what PBS reported are not asserted here.

## What happened

On Saturday, attorneys for the immigrant rights group CHIRLA filed court papers describing their analysis of the DOJ's list. Per PBS, they found that more than 40% of the people on the list do not appear in the state's voter file.

The Justice Department responded by urging the judge to sanction the group and its lawyers. The department claims the analysis violated a court order restricting how the data could be used, and it has suggested contempt of court charges, per the report.

The timing is notable. The dispute is unfolding days before a major election, and it sits within the Trump administration's broader push to challenge voter eligibility. Per PBS, those efforts have largely failed in court so far.

## What a 40% mismatch does and doesn't tell you

The plain reading of the finding is this: the list names people the DOJ describes as alleged noncitizen voters, and more than four in ten of those names don't show up in California's voter file at all.

That matters because a person who isn't on the voter rolls can't be casting a ballot through registration. A list meant to flag voters that includes many non-voters raises a basic question about how it was built.

Here is what we can't say from the reporting. We don't know why the names don't match. Differences in spelling, outdated records, or errors in the source data are all possible explanations, but those are my speculation, not findings from the case. The analysis also is the group's, and the DOJ disputes how it was produced and shared. Treat the 40% figure as a claim from CHIRLA's attorneys, filed in court, not as a settled number.

## The legal fight is separate from the data question

It helps to keep two questions apart.

**Question one: is the list accurate?** That's a factual matter, and an independent check against the state voter file is one way to test it.

**Question two: did the group break a court order?** The DOJ says yes, arguing the order limited how the data could be used. That is a legal question about the terms of the order, and a judge will decide it.

The DOJ's push for sanctions and its mention of contempt address the second question. They don't answer the first. A ruling that the group misused the data wouldn't by itself make the list accurate, and a finding of errors wouldn't by itself excuse a violation of a court order. Both can be true or false independently.

## Why accuracy matters for election administration

Election offices act on information. If a list of supposed ineligible voters is wrong in large part, the cost of acting on it falls on real people: eligible voters flagged in error, and staff spending time chasing names that lead nowhere.

PBS's reporting doesn't detail what California election officials are doing with the list, so that part remains an open question worth watching as the case develops.

## How to read the next headlines

When updates arrive, a few habits will help:

- **Check who is making each claim.** The 40% figure comes from CHIRLA's attorneys. The sanctions request comes from the DOJ.
- **Separate the data from the discipline.** Whether the list is flawed and whether the group violated an order are different issues.
- **Look for the judge's own words.** Court rulings carry more weight than filings from either side.
- **Watch for explanations of the mismatch.** The most useful missing piece is why the names don't appear in the voter file.

## The takeaway

When a dispute like this lands days before an election, resist the urge to pick a side from the headline. Read the filings or the original PBS report, note which claims are allegations, and wait for the court to speak on each question separately.
