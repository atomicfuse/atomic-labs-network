You can pack the perfect capsule wardrobe, decant every serum and still get stuck, because the document that matters most isn't in your suitcase. A case reported by the BBC shows how a routing and visa problem can turn a trip into years of limbo. Here is what happened, and the checks that help you avoid a version of it.

## What happened in the Cook Islands

Per the BBC, Ruchira Ranasingha, 50, has been stranded in the Cook Islands since 2023 and wants to return to Sri Lanka. The BBC reports she followed bad immigration advice that left her unable to leave or go home.

The trap, as reported, is a catch-22. New Zealand and Australia won't grant her transit visas to fly through their countries to reach Sri Lanka. Other routes, via Hawaii or Tahiti, reportedly require biometric processing that can only be done in New Zealand. So the one place that can unlock the alternatives is a place she can't get to.

The human stakes are high. Per the BBC, her son's wedding is approaching and her mother's health is declining.

Hers is an extreme case involving immigration status, and most travelers will never face anything like it. But the underlying lesson is ordinary and useful: the route you take matters as much as the destination.

## Check the transit rules, not just the entry rules

Most of us look up whether we need a visa for where we're going. Fewer check whether we need one for where we're passing through.

- Look up transit requirements for every airport on your itinerary, using each country's official government immigration site.
- Check whether a layover counts as airside (you never leave the secure area) or landside (you clear immigration). Rules can differ.
- Do this before you pay for the ticket, not after.

The Cook Islands story shows why. Remote destinations often have limited flight connections, so one refused transit country can close off most of your options.

## Know your exit before you enter

Island and remote destinations have fewer flights, fewer carriers and fewer fallback routes. Before booking, sketch out your way home and a backup.

1. Write down your outbound route and your return route as separate lists.
2. Identify at least one alternative route for the return leg.
3. For each alternative, note any transit visa, biometric appointment or advance authorization it requires.
4. Find out where any biometric processing happens. In the case the BBC describes, the location of that processing is the whole problem.

If a backup route depends on an appointment in a third country, treat it as a real requirement, not a footnote.

## Be careful who you trust for immigration advice

The BBC's account centers on advice that went wrong. The details of who gave it aren't something we can add here, so take it as a general prompt rather than a verdict on any one person or business.

Practical habits that protect you:

- Verify any adviser's credentials with the relevant government or professional regulator before paying.
- Confirm important advice against the official immigration website of the country concerned.
- Get advice in writing and keep copies.
- Be wary of anyone who promises outcomes no one can guarantee.

If an answer affects whether you can leave a country, a second opinion is cheap by comparison.

## Build a paperwork kit as carefully as a beauty kit

You already know the routine: travel sizes, a spare of the essentials, everything in one pouch. Give your documents the same treatment.

- Passport with plenty of validity left, plus a photo copy stored separately.
- Printed and digital copies of visas, permits and approval letters.
- Contact details for your embassy or consulate at your destination and at any transit stop.
- A dated record of every application and any reply you receive.

If something goes wrong, organized records speed up every conversation you'll have with an airline, an embassy or an official.

## If you hit a wall, act early

The Cook Islands case is a reminder that problems compound with time. If you learn mid-trip that a route or status isn't working, don't wait for it to resolve itself.

- Contact your embassy or consulate as soon as you know there's an issue.
- Ask specifically which routes and documents would let you travel, and in what order.
- Keep every refusal in writing, because it may matter for later applications.

We don't have details of what further steps Ranasingha has taken, and we won't guess at them. What the reporting makes plain is how hard it is to untangle a situation once each country's rules depend on another's.

## The one habit to take with you

Before you book anything, trace your trip from door to destination and back again, and confirm in official sources that every stop along the way will let you through. Do it before you buy the ticket, while changing your plans is still free.
