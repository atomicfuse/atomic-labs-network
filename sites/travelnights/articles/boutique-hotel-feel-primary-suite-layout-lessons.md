If the best hotel room you've ever stayed in made you want to move in, you already know what a good primary suite feels like. The trick to recreating it at home, according to one award-winning Southern California project, isn't a new bedspread. It's rethinking the room itself.

That's the takeaway from Modern Luxury's Design 9 Awards, which named designer Elspeth Keller Scott's redo of a dated Dana Point primary suite as Southern California's best in the category. Modern Luxury describes the project as giving the room "a dose of drama" and proving that sometimes the best way to make room is to rethink it entirely.

The published details we have are limited, so this isn't a room tour. Instead, think of it as a lesson in approach, one you can borrow whether you're planning a renovation or just daydreaming after a great hotel stay.

## What Happened in Dana Point

Per Modern Luxury, Keller Scott took a primary suite that felt dated and reworked it, with the layout at the center of the story. The result reads as both more dramatic and more functional. That pairing is the point: the room didn't just get prettier, it got better to live in.

We won't speculate on specifics like where walls moved or what finishes were used, since those details aren't in the coverage we're working from. What the outlet does make clear is the philosophy: rethink first, decorate second.

## Why Layout Beats Decor

Hotels understand something many homes miss. A room feels luxurious because of how you move through it and where your eye lands, not only because of the linens.

A dated primary suite often has a layout problem disguised as a style problem. New paint and furniture can freshen it, but if the bed, closet, and bath relate to each other awkwardly, the room will still feel off. Rethinking the plan is what lets drama and function show up together.

### Questions to ask before you shop

These are our suggestions, not details from the project:

- Where does your eye land when you walk in? If it's a dresser or a door, there's room for a stronger focal point.
- Does the path from bed to bath to closet feel natural, or do you zigzag around furniture?
- Is any part of the room doing a job it's bad at, like a corner that only collects laundry?
- What would you remove if you could, rather than add?

## Treat the Room Like a Retreat

Modern Luxury frames primary suites as increasingly personal retreats, with homeowners prioritizing spaces that feel spacious and emotionally impactful. If you travel often, you've likely felt this: a good hotel suite resets you. A primary suite designed with the same intent does it daily.

### Borrow the hotel mindset

When you're on the road, notice what makes a room work, then write it down. Was it the bed placement? The way the bath opened to the bedroom? The sense that nothing was crowded? Those observations are a ready-made brief for a designer, and they're more useful than a Pinterest board of pretty finishes.

## Planning Your Own Dated-Room Rescue

You don't need a luxury budget to apply the thinking. Here's a doing-order approach:

1. **Live in it critically for a week.** Note every friction point: where you bump into things, what you avoid, what feels cramped.
2. **Sketch the room to scale.** Even a rough floor plan shows whether furniture is fighting the space.
3. **Try rearranging before renovating.** Moving the bed to a different wall costs nothing and tells you a lot.
4. **Decide on one dramatic move.** A single bold gesture usually lands better than many timid ones.
5. **Bring in a professional for structural questions.** If the fix involves walls, plumbing, or closets, a designer like Keller Scott's caliber of practitioner can see options you won't.
6. **Decorate last.** Once the plan works, finishes and textiles are the fun part rather than a patch.

## The Takeaway

Before you buy a single pillow, stand in your primary suite and ask what the room is getting wrong structurally. Fix that question first, and the hotel feeling you keep chasing on vacation has a much better chance of following you home.
