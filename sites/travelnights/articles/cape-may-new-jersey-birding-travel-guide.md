Every September, something remarkable happens at the southern tip of New Jersey. Thousands of birds — raptors, songbirds, shorebirds, waterfowl — funnel down the Atlantic Flyway and pile up at Cape May, temporarily landlocked by the Delaware Bay. For birders, it's one of the great spectacles in North America. For everyone else, it's a very good excuse to visit a genuinely charming Victorian seaside town with excellent food, historic hotels, and beaches that don't require a crowd to enjoy.

If you're planning a trip — whether you're arriving with a field guide or just a vague appreciation for nature — this guide covers where to bird, where to eat, and where to sleep, with enough non-birding material to keep travel companions happily occupied.

## Why Fall Migration Makes Cape May Extraordinary

Cape May's prime migration windows run September through November, with a secondary surge in May. During the fall peak, the geography does the work: birds traveling south hit the bay and, rather than crossing open water, concentrate along the peninsula. That density is what makes Cape May special. You don't need to trek far or get lucky — the birds come to you.

The town has organized its birding infrastructure accordingly. Multiple nature preserves sit within easy reach of each other, most with flat or well-maintained trails that don't require serious hiking gear.

## The Best Birding Spots in Cape May

### Sunset Boulevard Nature Preserve
Locally regarded as the top birding spot in Cape May, Sunset Boulevard nature preserve is where serious birders head first. During migration, expect warblers, hawks, and — depending on timing — species that rarely show up this far north or south of their usual range.

### Higbees Beach
A 1,100-acre nature preserve with trail access, Higbees Beach draws songbirds and raptors. It's also a legitimate beach, so a walk here covers both the ornithological and the scenic bases.

### 801 Wilson Street Nature Preserve
At 180 acres, this preserve is particularly good for songbirds and raptors moving through on their southward routes. It's quieter than some of the more well-known spots, which is a feature.

### Forsythe National Wildlife Refuge
A short drive from the Cape May peninsula, Forsythe spans 48,000 acres and functions primarily as a waterfowl haven. If you want volume — both species variety and sheer numbers of birds — this is worth the trip.

### Captain Bob's Waterway Tour
For a different perspective, Captain Bob runs four-hour tours on The Osprey, covering the waterways around Cape May. Seeing the coastline and marshes from the water changes what you're able to observe, and it's a good option for a morning when land-based birding feels like a change of pace.

## Where to Stay

Cape May's accommodation options lean historic, and that's genuinely part of the appeal — this isn't a chain-hotel beach town.

**Congress Hall** is the anchor. Opened in 1816, it claims the distinction of being the oldest seaside resort in America, and it still functions as a full resort with restaurants and a jazz bar (The Brown Room) on-site. If you want to be central to everything without thinking too hard about logistics, this is the default choice.

**140 Stevens Street** takes a different approach: a hotel set on a regenerative farm with individual cottages. If the appeal of Cape May is partly about slowing down and connecting with the landscape, this property delivers on that idea more literally than most.

**805 Stockton Avenue** is a historic 10-room bed and breakfast — the kind of place where the rooms have character and breakfast is an event rather than an afterthought.

**623 Columbia Avenue** is a Victorian bed and breakfast that suits travelers who want to lean fully into Cape May's architectural identity. The town's Victorian streetscapes are a genuine attraction, and staying in one of the period properties makes that more immersive.

## Where to Eat

The food scene punches above what you'd expect from a small coastal resort town.

**Blue Pig Tavern at Congress Hall** is the reliable all-rounder — convenient if you're staying there, worth visiting regardless.

**The Olive Branch** (1891 Bayshore Road) is a Greek BYOB, which is a combination that works. Bring wine, order thoughtfully, and plan for a relaxed evening.

**Beach Plum Cafe** at 484 West Perry Street handles breakfast well, which matters when you're trying to get to Sunset Boulevard before the morning migration activity peaks.

**The farm-to-table cafe at 104 Jackson Street** is the midday option for travelers who want local sourcing and a lighter meal after a morning in the field.

**The Ugly Mug** at 426 Washington Street is the unpretentious bar-food haunt — exactly what you want after a long day on the trails. No agenda, just food and a drink.

For something sweet, the fudge and salt water taffy shop at 513 Washington Street Mall is the kind of place that's been operating since before anyone called it artisanal.

## Beyond the Binoculars

Cape May earns repeat visits partly because it doesn't require birding to justify the trip. The Victorian architecture makes the town itself worth wandering. West End Garage at 484 West Perry Street stocks vintage finds and locally made goods — the kind of shop that takes longer than expected.

The **Harriet Tubman Museum** at 632 Lafayette Street connects the region to a broader and important history. It's worth time, particularly if you're in town for more than a quick overnight.

**Beach Plum Farm** at 400 Stevens Street operates as both a working farm and an event space. It's connected to the Stevens Street hotel and reflects the town's broader lean toward agricultural and environmental consciousness.

## Planning Your Trip

September through November is the prime window, with October typically offering the most concentrated migration activity. May provides a second peak for northbound migrants. If you're visiting specifically for birding, aim for early mornings — activity drops off significantly by midday.

Most of the nature preserves are accessible without specialized equipment. Comfortable walking shoes, a decent pair of binoculars, and a bird identification app will get a beginner through a productive morning. The trail infrastructure at Higbees and 801 Wilson Street is approachable for most fitness levels.

Cape May is small enough that you can cover the main birding spots and still have afternoons free for the town itself. That balance — serious natural spectacle plus a place that's genuinely pleasant to exist in — is what keeps it on the short list for travelers who might otherwise skip New Jersey entirely.
