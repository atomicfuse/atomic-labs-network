Billy Bob Thornton has a firm Thanksgiving rule: no restaurants. The host of "The Mayflower" told Fox News Digital that eating out on the holiday just doesn't hit the same, and he casts himself as a Thanksgiving "purist" who prefers a traditional meal at home, per Fox News.

That's one actor's stance, but it lands in the middle of a real debate: as more families weigh dining out or ordering in, is convenience worth what you might lose? If you're traveling this week, or hosting people who are, this is a practical question. Here's how to answer it for your own table.

## What Thornton's stance actually says

The framing in Fox News' piece is simple. Thanksgiving is a comfort-driven, nostalgic ritual, and home cooking and familiar traditions are what matter most. Thornton isn't arguing that restaurant food is bad. He's arguing that the setting is part of the meal.

That's a useful lens, because it shifts the question from "which meal tastes better?" to "what do I want this day to feel like?"

## Ask what your Thanksgiving is actually for

Before you book anything, name the one thing you'd miss most if it vanished. Be honest. Common answers:

- **The cooking itself.** If the kitchen chaos, the smells and the leftover plans are the point, a restaurant can't replace them.
- **The people.** If the goal is simply getting everyone in one room, a restaurant table does that with far less work.
- **The ritual.** If it's specific dishes, a specific seat, or a specific playlist, you're in purist territory, like Thornton.

If your answer is the first or third, stay home or recreate the ritual wherever you land. If it's the second, a restaurant is a perfectly good choice, not a failure.

## When a restaurant makes sense anyway

Purism has a cost, and it's fair to weigh it. Dining out can be the smarter call when:

- You're traveling and don't have a kitchen, or you're staying in a hotel.
- Your group is small and nobody wants to cook for two.
- The host is burned out and the alternative is resentment over the dishes.

If you go this route, book as early as you can and confirm exactly what the menu includes. Holiday menus are often fixed, so check dietary options when you reserve rather than at the table.

## If you're traveling, bring the ritual with you

This is where the purist instinct is most useful. You can't always be home, but you can carry a piece of home's Thanksgiving with you.

1. **Pick one non-negotiable dish.** Cook it in a rental kitchen, or ask relatives to make it.
2. **Keep one tradition intact.** A toast, a walk before dinner, a specific game or show afterward.
3. **Choose the setting on purpose.** A rental with a kitchen, a long table at a relative's place, or a restaurant with a family-style menu all preserve more of the feel than a quick grab-and-go meal.

The goal isn't to copy your usual Thanksgiving exactly. It's to keep the one or two things that make the day feel like itself.

## The convenience trade-off

Ordering in or eating out saves hours. What it can cost is the part of the holiday that happens around the food: the shared prep, the slow afternoon, the leftovers. That's an editorial take, not a finding from Thornton's comments, but it's worth sitting with. If you choose convenience, decide in advance what you'll do to replace that slow-build feeling, even something small like cooking one side together.

## A quick way to settle the argument

If your household is split between purists and pragmatists, try this:

- Each person names their single must-have.
- Find the plan that covers the most of them.
- Accept that no plan covers everyone's.

A hybrid often wins: a home-cooked main course with store-bought sides, or a restaurant meal followed by pie and a movie at home.

## The takeaway

Thornton's rule works for him because he knows what Thanksgiving means to him. Do the same: decide what one thing the day can't lose, then pick the home, restaurant or hybrid plan that protects it.
