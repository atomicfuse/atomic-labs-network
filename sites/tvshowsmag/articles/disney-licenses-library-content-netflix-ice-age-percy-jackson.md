Disney and Netflix are back in business together — and the terms are very, very deliberate.

As reported by Vulture, Disney is licensing a curated selection of its library to Netflix, with the *Ice Age* franchise and *Percy Jackson and the Olympians* arriving on the service this weekend. Legacy network shows including *Felicity* and *Revenge* are part of the broader wave, with more titles to come. What is conspicuously *not* in the package: Marvel, Star Wars, and the Pixar vault. That omission isn't an oversight. It's the entire strategy.

## The Deliberate Art of the B-List Deal

Cast your mind back to 2017, when Disney made its theatrical exit from Netflix to build what would become Disney+. It was a landmark flex — a signal that the Mouse House believed its IP was valuable enough to anchor a streaming service entirely on its own. That bet paid off, for a while. But Disney+ is now facing real subscriber pressure in a brutally competitive market, and it turns out there's money sitting idle in the library that isn't named Iron Man.

Enter the two-tier strategy. Disney appears to be drawing a clean line between its crown jewels — the Marvel Cinematic Universe, the *Star Wars* galaxy, and Pixar's theatrical catalog — and what we might generously call the supporting cast. The *Ice Age* movies are beloved, *Percy Jackson* has a genuinely passionate fanbase, and *Felicity* is peak late-'90s television. But none of them are *Avengers: Endgame*. By licensing this tier to Netflix, Disney generates licensing revenue without cannibalizing the content that gives Disney+ its competitive reason to exist.

It's the streaming equivalent of selling the paperback rights while keeping the hardcover exclusive. Smart, if a little cold-blooded.

## The Netflix Bump Is Real, and Disney Knows It

Here's where the chess match gets interesting. Disney isn't just collecting a licensing check — it's using Netflix's algorithmic reach as a marketing engine.

Consider the timing. *Ice Age: Boiling Point* is heading to theaters in February, and *Percy Jackson* season three is due in November. Dropping the existing *Ice Age* films and the first two seasons of *Percy Jackson* on Netflix right now is a move that would make any studio marketing department nod approvingly. Netflix's recommendation system has a well-documented track record of turning catalog titles into cultural conversations — the *Suits* phenomenon being the most cited example, a show that became a genuine ratings phenomenon years after its original run ended, after landing on Netflix.

If even a fraction of that energy attaches itself to *Percy Jackson* on Netflix, it builds anticipation for a new season arriving on Disney+ in November. The two platforms, technically rivals, end up functioning like a promotional relay race. Disney gets cross-platform buzz. Netflix gets content. Subscribers get more to watch. It is, genuinely, one of the cleaner win-win structures the streamer wars have produced.

## The Nostalgia Factor Nobody Should Underestimate

And then there are the legacy shows — *Felicity*, *Revenge*, *This Is Us* — which represent a different kind of value proposition entirely.

Netflix has become the de facto home for a very specific streaming behavior: comfort rewatching. Audiences who were in high school when *Revenge* premiered are now in their thirties, looking for something familiar to put on while they're doing other things (or not doing other things — no judgment). The shows don't need to be prestige television. They need to be *known*. There's an enormous appetite for TV that already lives in people's emotional memory, and Netflix's catalog approach has repeatedly demonstrated that 2000s network drama is catnip for that audience segment.

For Disney, which owns these titles through its various acquisitions and historical deals, monetizing them on Netflix is essentially found money. These shows aren't driving meaningful new subscriptions to Disney+, so licensing them out costs nothing in competitive terms while adding a real revenue line.

## What This Tells Us About the State of the Streamer Wars

Zoom out and this deal sketches a portrait of two companies adapting to a streaming economy that has matured past its original assumptions.

Netflix built its first decade on originals and the premise that licensed content was a crutch. Then it watched its own subscriber numbers respond very enthusiastically every time a licensed catalog title went viral, and quietly reassessed. The company is now aggressively acquiring licensed content to diversify beyond originals — a strategic pivot that this Disney deal underlines.

Disney, meanwhile, is contending with the reality that building and maintaining a streaming service is expensive, subscriber growth has limits, and not every piece of IP in the vault justifies the infrastructure cost of exclusive hosting. Licensing the mid-tier generates cash that can be reinvested in the content that *does* justify exclusivity.

Neither company is retreating. Both are getting more precise about what their platforms are actually *for*.

## The One Question Worth Watching

The deal raises a genuinely interesting question for the months ahead: which of these titles gets the *Suits* treatment? Which one lands on Netflix, gets surfaced to an audience that either missed it or wants to revisit it, and suddenly becomes the thing everyone is talking about again?

*Percy Jackson* has the built-in fandom and the incoming season three as a narrative hook. *Revenge* has the melodrama and the devoted rewatcher demographic. *Felicity* has nostalgia so potent it practically hums.

Disney calculated that these titles were worth more on Netflix than sitting quietly in the Disney+ library. If even one of them catches fire in the way licensed content occasionally does, it won't just have been right — it will have gotten Netflix to pay for its own marketing campaign. That's not a bad trick for a company that spent nearly a decade insisting it didn't need to share.
