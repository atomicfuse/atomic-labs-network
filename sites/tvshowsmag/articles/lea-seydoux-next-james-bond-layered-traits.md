Léa Seydoux has a job description for the next James Bond, and it doesn't start with a tuxedo or a Walther. In a new interview with Elle, the actor who played Dr. Madeleine Swann says the next 007 needs to be "layered": emotional depth and complexity first, swagger second.

She also stressed keeping Bond's "British spirit" intact as the franchise enters a new era under Amazon MGM, per Elle.

## Why her opinion carries weight

Seydoux isn't a casual observer. Madeleine Swann is, per Elle's framing, Bond's most influential love interest, and she's the person who spent time across from the last 007 in a version of the character that cared about feelings. That gives her a vantage point most commentators lack.

The timing matters too. Bond is at a turning point: new creative leadership at Amazon MGM, Denis Villeneuve attached to the project, and casting speculation fueling fan debate. Everyone has a pick for the next 007. Seydoux is weighing in on the more interesting question, which is what kind of person that actor should be playing.

## What "layered" could actually mean

Here's where we step into analysis, because Seydoux's quoted comments are brief and the rest is us reading between the lines.

For decades, Bond has been defined by surface: the quip, the car, the martini order. A layered Bond suggests an inversion, where the surface is a performance and something messier sits underneath. It's hard not to see that as a continuation of what the Daniel Craig era did, with a Bond who could be hurt, who fell for someone, and who was allowed to be affected by the job.

Madeleine Swann was a big part of that approach. She was written as someone with her own history and her own guardedness, rather than a woman who arrives in the second act to be rescued. If the next Bond is meant to be "layered," it follows that the people around him will need to be, too.

## The tricky part: evolving without losing the plot

The other half of Seydoux's message is the "British spirit" point, and it's a fair tension to flag. Bond is now owned by an American tech giant's studio arm, and fans have been asking whether the character will keep his identity. Seydoux's comment, as reported by Elle, reads as a reminder that depth and heritage aren't opposites. A psychologically complex Bond can still be unmistakably British.

Neither the Elle piece nor anything else we have here says what the casting will be or what Villeneuve's version will look like, so treat everything beyond Seydoux's remarks as speculation. But her two requirements work as a handy checklist:

- **Emotional complexity:** a lead who can play vulnerability without losing menace.
- **Cultural specificity:** a Bond who feels rooted somewhere, not a generic global action hero.

## The Craig-sized hurdle

Whoever steps in inherits a hard comparison. Craig's run made emotional stakes part of Bond's DNA, so a return to the untouchable, unbothered version would feel like a step backward rather than a fresh start. That is our read, not Seydoux's, but her emphasis on layers suggests she'd agree that the bar has moved.

The smart move for the next actor isn't to out-brood Craig. It's to find a different set of layers entirely: a different wound, a different kind of charm, a different reason the mask exists.

## The bottom line

Bond has survived by reinventing itself every time the world changed, and the one constant is that the man underneath has to be interesting. Seydoux's advice is less a casting tip than a design principle: build the person first, and the tuxedo will take care of itself. Casting directors, take notes.
