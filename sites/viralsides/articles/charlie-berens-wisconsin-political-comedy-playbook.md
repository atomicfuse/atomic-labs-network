If you've ever wanted to make a funny video about politics without it turning into a lecture or a shouting match, there's a useful model to study. NPR's Sacha Pfeiffer recently interviewed comedian and former journalist Charlie Berens about how he turns Wisconsin politics into comedy. Per NPR, his style blends political satire with a distinctly Wisconsin sense of humor.

Below is the creator playbook that profile points to, plus ways you can borrow it for your own jokes, sketches, and memes. I'm working only from NPR's summary of the conversation, so the tactics here are my read of the approach, not Berens' own words.

## The Setup: A Journalist Who Became a Comedian

NPR describes Berens as a comedian and former journalist. That combination matters. Reporters spend their days around the actual machinery of government: who's running, what's on the ballot, what people argue about at the local level. Comedy built on that background has something solid to push against.

The takeaway for you: jokes land harder when you know the topic. You don't need a press badge, but you do need to understand the issue well enough to find what's actually absurd about it.

## Lead With Place, Not Party

The core move in NPR's framing is that Berens' satire is wrapped in regional identity. The humor comes from being from Wisconsin, not from picking a team.

That's a smart structural choice. Place-based comedy gives your audience a shared starting point before any politics arrives. If viewers already feel "that's us," they're more willing to laugh at the topic you bring in.

### How to try it

- List five things only people in your state or region would instantly recognize: accents, foods, weather complaints, local rivalries, road names.
- Pick one civic topic in the news, like a ballot question, a budget fight, or a local race.
- Write one joke where the regional detail does the heavy lifting and the politics rides along.

## Make Politics Less Exhausting

One of the angles worth drawing out: as political content gets more polarized, audiences get tired. Humor rooted in local culture offers a different door into the same subject. Instead of asking viewers to brace for a fight, it invites them to recognize their own neighbors, habits, and quirks.

This is the real appeal of the "Midwest nice" flavor of satire. Politeness itself becomes the joke engine: the awkward way people avoid conflict, the understatement, the cheerful deflection. You can poke at serious topics through tone without needing to shout.

## The Creator Playbook, Condensed

Here's a practical version of what Berens' approach suggests for anyone making funny videos or memes:

### 1. Pick a niche you actually live in

Regional identity is a powerful content niche because it's specific. A joke about "your state" beats a joke about "everyone," since specificity is what makes people tag a friend and say "this is so us."

### 2. Use shared references as shortcuts

Accents, local phrases, and familiar situations let you set a scene in seconds. That's gold for short-form video, where you have almost no time to explain context.

### 3. Keep the target the situation, not the audience

The goal is comedy that unpacks politics without sounding like a partisan rant. Aim at the absurdity of a process or a moment, and your viewers can laugh together instead of bracing for being lectured.

### 4. Turn state politics into sketches

Local government has built-in characters and conflicts: the long meeting, the confusing rule, the candidate with a very familiar campaign slogan. Those translate easily into short sketches or meme formats.

## Why This Works Beyond Wisconsin

You don't have to be from the Midwest to use this. Every region has its own vocabulary, rhythms, and running jokes. The principle is that identity and humor together make civic topics feel approachable, which is a rare thing in a crowded, loud feed.

There's also a timing bonus. With an election season in full swing for many viewers, people are saturated with political content. A funny, local, low-pressure take stands out precisely because it doesn't feel like more of the same.

## A Quick Test Before You Post

Before you publish a political joke, ask three things:

- Would a neighbor from the other side of the aisle still find the regional detail funny?
- Is the joke about a situation or process, rather than a group of people?
- Could someone with no strong opinions on the issue still enjoy it?

If you can answer yes to all three, you're following the same spirit NPR highlights in Berens' work.

## The Takeaway

Write your next political joke about where you're from first and what's in the news second. Start with the accent, the weather complaint, or the awkward local politeness, then let the civic topic ride along. People will laugh before they realize they're paying attention.
