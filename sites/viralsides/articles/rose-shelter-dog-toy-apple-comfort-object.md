Rose has an apple. It's a squeaky toy, and according to The Dodo, it has become the thing she's most attached to while she waits at the RSPCA Derby Branch for someone to take her home. Her story is a small, sweet reminder of why a cheap toy can mean a lot to a dog in a kennel, and why older shelter dogs deserve a second look.

## Meet Rose and her apple

Rose is 8 years old. Per The Dodo's reporting, she has watched all of her puppies get adopted while she's still waiting for a home of her own. Staff at the RSPCA Derby Branch are looking for the right family for her.

In the meantime, she has her toy apple. The Dodo describes her attachment to it as something like an emotional support symbol, and the outlet's description sums it up: it brings her joy while she looks for a home.

The details we have are modest, and we won't invent more. What comes through is a dog with one reliable source of comfort in a place that is anything but familiar.

## Why a comfort toy matters in a shelter

The Dodo's story frames the apple as Rose's comfort object. That fits something many dog owners will recognize: plenty of dogs pick a favorite item and carry it around like a security blanket.

A shelter is a stressful place by nature, with new smells, new noises and a lot of change. A toy that stays the same can be a steady point in all that. That's our read on it rather than anything the shelter has said, but it's easy to see why a squeaky apple would earn a special spot.

If you're adopting, this is practical:

- Ask staff whether the dog has a favorite toy, and whether it can go home with her.
- Bring the toy along on the first car ride and into the first night.
- Keep familiar items around while the dog settles in, rather than replacing everything at once.

## Mama dogs get left behind too

One of the quieter points in Rose's story is that she has already seen her puppies adopted. Puppies tend to go first. The mother, who is older and sometimes shy, is often the one left waiting.

Nothing here suggests anyone did anything wrong. Adopters often go for the youngest animals, and that's simply how it plays out. But it does mean dogs like Rose can sit in a kennel long after the litter has found homes.

## The case for adopting a senior dog

The Dodo's piece highlights the need to give shy, senior dogs a chance, and shelters generally struggle with overcrowding while older animals get passed over. If Rose's story makes you wonder about adopting an older dog, here are things worth asking a shelter:

1. What is the dog's personality like once she's out of the kennel?
2. How does she react to new people, other pets and children?
3. What does she love? A toy, a treat, a certain kind of attention?
4. How much exercise does she need day to day?

A dog who is 8 comes with a more settled personality than a puppy, and what you see in a meet-and-greet is closer to what you'll get. Again, that's general advice rather than something specific to Rose.

## How to help even if you can't adopt

Not everyone can bring a dog home, but you can still help a dog like Rose:

- Share her story so more people see an older dog who needs a family.
- Donate a toy or supplies to your local shelter.
- Volunteer for walks or play time, since shy dogs often warm up with regular visitors.
- Foster, if your shelter offers it, to give a dog a break from kennel life.

If you're near Derby and think you could be the right home for Rose, the RSPCA Derby Branch is the place to ask.

Rose isn't asking for much: a home, and probably to bring the apple. Next time you're scrolling a shelter's adoptable list, stop on the older dog in the corner photo. Read her bio first.
