Terry Bradshaw spent decades as the loud, funny, tough-guy face of football TV. Now the Hall of Fame quarterback and FOX Sports analyst is telling a quieter story. In his new memoir, "Looking for Terry: A Memoir," he opens up about a lifelong battle with depression, a subject he has kept largely private, as he told Fox News.

The most useful takeaway isn't the headline confession. It's a small, practical trick: give the thing a name, and it gets easier to talk about.

## The 'Demon in the Night'

Bradshaw describes his depression as a "demon in the night," per Fox News. According to the outlet's report, it can strike during stress or exhaustion. That detail matters. It frames depression less as a constant state and more as something that shows up when his defenses are down.

He also describes it as recurring. It "rears its ugly head," in the coverage's framing, but it doesn't define who he is. That's a healthier way to think about it than "I am a depressed person." It's something he deals with, not something he is.

## Why Naming It Helps: Meet 'Roy'

Here's the detail worth stealing. Bradshaw's wife calls his depression "Roy."

That might sound like a joke, and in a funny-guy's household it may well carry some humor. But the practical effect is real. A name gives a couple a shortcut. Instead of a heavy, awkward conversation that starts from zero, someone can say that Roy is around. It separates the person from the problem, and it lowers the stakes of bringing it up.

If you or someone you live with deals with low periods, you could try the same thing:

- **Pick a name together.** Silly is fine. Silly is the point.
- **Agree on a signal.** A single word beats a speech when you're exhausted.
- **Decide what happens next.** Maybe it's a walk, a quiet night, or a call to someone else.

That's our suggestion, not Bradshaw's prescription, but it follows directly from how he and his wife handle it.

## The Tough Guy Who Says It Out Loud

Bradshaw's career is the whole reason this lands. He's a legendary quarterback in a sport that prizes toughness, and a TV personality whose confidence is practically a brand. Nobody would have to guess whether he was "the type" to struggle. That's exactly why his candor carries weight.

Per Fox News, he's using the book to encourage men to be more open about mental health rather than turning to substances. Male mental health stigma is a persistent problem, and it helps when someone with a locker-room reputation says plainly that he's been fighting this for years.

It's also a reminder that success and struggle aren't opposites. Someone can hold a Hall of Fame résumé, a national TV job and a punchline for every occasion, and still have nights that are hard. High-functioning doesn't mean fine.

## Why a Partner Matters

The Roy nickname only works because someone is on the other end of it. Bradshaw's wife is the person he can talk to when depression resurfaces, and that support system is the quiet hero of the story.

If you're the supporter, the lesson is simple: be someone it's safe to tell. If you're the one struggling, pick one trusted person and let them in before the next rough night, not during it.

## What to Take From the Book

We haven't read "Looking for Terry," and the details above come from Fox News' coverage of his interview, so the book will surely hold more. But the core message is clear enough to act on without it.

If you've been carrying something quietly, say it out loud to one person this week. Give it a name if that helps. Bradshaw, of all people, just showed it doesn't make you any less tough.

If you're struggling, a doctor or mental health professional can help, and in the U.S. you can call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.
