England levelled the T20 series against Australia with an eight-wicket win, per the BBC, powered by a 151-run opening stand between Aneurin Donald and Phil Salt. Donald made 85 off 32 balls. The series is tied 1-1, and the Gabba match now looks like a momentum test. If you have tickets to a night like that, the practical question is what the sky will do. Here's how to read the forecast, what to pack, and what to do if the weather turns.

We haven't included a forecast for the match here, because forecasts change quickly. Check the Bureau of Meteorology (bom.gov.au) for your venue's latest forecast and radar, and look at it again on match day.

## Read the rain chance the right way

A "40% chance of rain" does not mean it will rain for 40% of the match. It means there's a 40% chance that measurable rain falls at a given point in the forecast area during the forecast period. Two things to look at instead:

- **The amount.** A forecast of 0-1 mm is a sprinkle. A forecast of 10-20 mm is a real soaking.
- **The timing.** Forecasts that break the day into hours tell you far more than a daily percentage. A storm at 3 pm matters much less than one at 7 pm.

A T20 game is short, usually around three and a half hours. A daily rain chance can look alarming even when the wet spell would be brief and well clear of the game.

## Check the radar, not just the percentage

On match day, the radar is your best tool. Open it two or three hours before you leave and look at three things:

1. **Where the rain is.** Cells to the west of you often move toward you in many places, though the direction varies by day.
2. **Which way it's moving.** Open the animation, not the still image.
3. **How intense it looks.** Orange and red areas mean heavy rain, and often thunder.

Popup summer storms can build quickly in warm, humid air. If the forecast mentions a thunderstorm risk, treat the radar as a live feed and check it again before you head in.

## Know what thunder means for the game

Lightning is a bigger problem than rain. Rain alone can often be waited out under cover. Lightning means getting indoors or into a covered concourse, and it can mean a stoppage even if the rain is light.

A widely used safety rule from weather services is to wait 30 minutes after the last thunder you hear before returning to open seating. If you're in an uncovered stand when thunder starts, don't wait for the announcement. Move under solid cover.

## What happens to the score if rain interrupts

If rain shortens a chase, officials can adjust the target to reflect the overs left. In practice that means a game can stay alive after a break, but the maths can change. Don't assume a washout just because the covers come on. Give it time, and keep an eye on the official match updates.

## Pack for the whole evening

Evening matches can shift from hot to damp to cool in a few hours. A good kit:

- **A light rain jacket or poncho.** Umbrellas are often restricted or unwelcome in crowded seating, so check the venue's rules before you bring one.
- **Sunscreen and a hat for the early part.** Sun can be strong before a game starts, even if storms are expected later.
- **Water.** Humid air makes you sweat more than you think.
- **A phone in a waterproof pouch,** plus a charged battery pack. You'll be checking the radar and your ticket.
- **Shoes you don't mind getting wet.** Concourses and steps get slippery.

## Plan your exit before the weather forces it

Heavy rain after a match can mean crowded trains, slow roads and flooded gutters. A few steps help:

- Know where the nearest covered area is when you take your seat.
- Check public transport updates before you leave the ground.
- Avoid driving through flooded roads. Even shallow moving water can be dangerous.
- Wait out the worst of a storm. Fifteen minutes under cover often beats a soaking, crowded walk.

## If you're watching from home

The same forecast helps if you're hosting a viewing party. If storms are likely, move the food and seating indoors, secure loose outdoor items, and keep devices charged in case of a power flicker. Wind and lightning can also interrupt broadcasts and streaming.

## The takeaway

With the series tied and England's top order on form, it's easy to get caught up in the cricket and forget the sky. Look at the Bureau of Meteorology forecast and radar the morning of the match, again two hours before you leave, and pack a light rain layer either way.
