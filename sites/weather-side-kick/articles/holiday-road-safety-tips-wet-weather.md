Thanksgiving storms hit one of the busiest travel stretches of the year, and the lesson from this week is simple: if you meet floodwater on the road, turn around and find another route. Here is how to make that call, and what else to have ready when a storm scrambles holiday plans.

## What happened this Thanksgiving

Per HuffPost, heavy rain, high winds, coastal flooding and snow battered much of the country on the holiday, with impacts reported from the East Coast to California. The extreme weather forced the postponement of Macy's 100th Thanksgiving Day Parade, and it brought power outages, flight delays and infrastructure damage to various areas.

For specifics in your area, check the National Weather Service (weather.gov) for current forecasts and alerts. Conditions change quickly, and this article reflects reporting published November 26, 2026.

## If you hit a flooded road: turn around

The standard safety advice from weather agencies is "turn around, don't drown." It is hard to judge how deep water is, and you can't see whether the road underneath is still intact. Storm-related infrastructure damage, like the kind reported this week, makes that unknown riskier.

A practical way to handle it:

- **Don't drive into water you can't clearly measure.** Dark, moving water is the worst case.
- **Pick another route before you need one.** Look at a map ahead of time for higher-ground alternatives.
- **Slow down in heavy rain.** Reduced visibility and wet pavement shorten your margin for error.
- **If your car stalls in rising water, get to higher ground** and call emergency services if you need help.
- **Heed barricades and closure signs.** They exist because someone already found the problem.

## Wind and rain: what changes on the road

High winds paired with heavy rain make driving tougher, especially for tall or light vehicles and anyone towing. Give yourself extra following distance, keep both hands on the wheel, and watch for debris. Where snow is part of the mix, as it was in some parts of the country this week, allow for even more time.

## Why coastal flooding can surprise you

The report highlights coastal flooding and king tides as part of the holiday disruption. A king tide is an unusually high tide that happens when the sun, moon and Earth line up. On its own it can push water into low-lying streets. Add a storm's rain and wind, and water can reach places that normally stay dry.

If you live or are traveling near a coast:

- Check the tide schedule alongside the rain forecast.
- Avoid parking in low-lying areas near the water during a storm.
- Expect some roads to close even when the sky looks calmer than the forecast suggested.

## Prepare for power outages

Widespread outages were part of this week's story. If you're hosting or staying with family, a few steps help:

- **Charge phones and power banks** before the storm arrives.
- **Keep flashlights handy** so you aren't relying on phone batteries.
- **Keep the fridge and freezer closed** as much as possible during an outage.
- **Report the outage** to your utility so crews know where to look.
- **Have a backup plan for the meal.** A postponed dinner beats a risky drive.

## Flight delays and cancelled plans

Storms during peak travel mean less slack in the system. If you're flying, check your airline's app for updates, and consider whether leaving earlier or later would help. If your route runs through a stormy region, build in buffer time, and decide ahead of time what you'll do if the trip falls apart.

The parade is a useful example. Per HuffPost, even Macy's 100th Thanksgiving Day Parade was postponed. If a major event with extensive planning can shift, your family dinner can too.

## A quick pre-trip checklist

1. Check the NWS forecast and any active alerts for your starting point, route and destination.
2. Confirm flights or transit with the carrier the day you leave.
3. Pack a phone charger, water, snacks, a blanket and a flashlight in the car.
4. Fill the gas tank before you go.
5. Tell someone your route and expected arrival time.

## The takeaway

Before any storm-day drive, look at the alerts, pick a backup route, and commit to one rule: if the road is under water, don't cross it. Arriving late is always better than not arriving.
