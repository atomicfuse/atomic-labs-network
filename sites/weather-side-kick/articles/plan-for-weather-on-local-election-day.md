Local elections can be decided by the people who actually show up. Plan your trip to the polls around the forecast, and rain, wind, or a cold snap won't decide whether your vote gets counted.

The example comes from Foster. The Providence Journal reported on a rarely seen event there: a write-in campaign knocked out four of the five Republican incumbents. The report doesn't say anything about the weather, so this isn't a story about forecasts. It's a reminder that small races can swing on a modest number of motivated voters. If you plan to be one of them, a little weather planning helps.

## Check the forecast the night before

Look at your hourly forecast from the National Weather Service (weather.gov), not just the daily summary. A "60% chance of rain" for the day tells you little about the hour you'll be walking from your car to the door.

Pick the dry window and aim for it. Hourly forecasts show when rain starts, when it tapers off, and when wind picks up.

## Know what a rain chance actually means

A 40% chance of rain means there's a 40% chance that measurable rain falls at any given point in the forecast area during the period. It doesn't mean it rains 40% of the day. A 30% day can still hand you a soaking at 7 a.m., so check the hours.

## Build a quick weather kit

Keep this by the door on voting day:

- A compact umbrella, or a hooded jacket if wind is expected (wind turns umbrellas inside out)
- Shoes with grip, since entrances and parking lots get slick
- Gloves and a hat if temperatures are near freezing
- Your ID and any voting paperwork in a zip-top bag so rain can't ruin it
- A phone charger or fully charged phone, in case you need to look up your polling place

## Allow extra time

Bad weather slows everything: traffic, parking, even the walk to the door. Add 15 to 20 minutes to your normal trip. If you can, go mid-morning or mid-afternoon rather than during the before-work or after-work rush.

## Watch for alerts that change the plan

On days with active advisories, such as winter weather, flooding, or high wind, check the National Weather Service alert page for your area. Roads can be affected, and you want to know before you leave. Confirm your polling place's hours and location with your local election office, since arrangements vary by town and state.

## Ask about alternatives before the day

Many places offer options beyond showing up on election day, such as early voting or mail-in ballots. Rules and deadlines differ widely, so check with your town clerk or state election office well ahead of time. Don't assume an option exists, and don't wait until the storm is in the forecast to find out.

## Why this matters most in small races

In a town the size of Foster, every vote carries real weight, and the Providence Journal's account shows what organized, motivated voters can do. A rainy morning that discourages a few dozen people can matter more in a local contest than in a statewide one. That is true whichever side you're on.

## Your plan in three steps

1. The night before, check the hourly forecast at weather.gov and choose your dry window.
2. Set out your kit: umbrella or hood, grippy shoes, ID in a bag.
3. Leave early, with 15 to 20 extra minutes built in.

The forecast can change your route and your timing. Don't let it change your vote.
