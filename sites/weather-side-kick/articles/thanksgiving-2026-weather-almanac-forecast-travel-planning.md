The Old Farmer's Almanac has put out its Thanksgiving week weather predictions for 2026, and the short version is a mix: rain, snow, sunshine and mild temperatures, depending on where you live, according to a Yahoo report on the outlook. The useful way to read that is not as a promise, but as a reason to build a backup plan now. This guide shows you how to do that, and when to swap the Almanac for live forecasts.

## What the Almanac forecast is, and isn't

The Almanac's predictions are long-range outlooks published well ahead of the holiday. They give you a general sense of what to expect, such as a wetter region or a milder one. They can't tell you whether your Wednesday flight will hit a rain band or whether snow will fall on your drive.

Per the Yahoo report, the outlook itself stresses checking local forecasts closer to departure, because conditions may shift. That's sound advice, and it shapes everything below.

For actual decisions, the official source is the National Weather Service (weather.gov), which issues forecasts and any watches or warnings for your exact location. Treat the Almanac as the early heads-up and the NWS as the final word.

## Use the Almanac to plan, then the NWS to decide

Here's a simple split for how the two fit together.

- **Now through mid-November:** Use the Almanac's regional outlook to decide what to prepare for. Rain? Pack waterproof layers. Snow? Plan extra drive time.
- **About a week out:** Start checking your NWS forecast for both your starting point and your destination.
- **48 hours out:** Check airline alerts and road conditions. This is when you make go, delay or reroute calls.
- **Day of travel:** Look at the forecast hour by hour, not just the daily high and low.

Remember that you're dealing with two forecasts, not one. Weather at your destination matters, and so does weather along the route or at any connecting airport.

## Packing for a mixed forecast

When the outlook includes rain, snow and mild stretches all at once, layers beat any single heavy item.

### If rain is possible

- A packable waterproof jacket with a hood
- Shoes you can get wet without regretting it, plus a spare pair of socks in your carry-on or day bag
- A zip bag for phones, chargers and boarding passes

### If snow is possible

- Gloves, a hat and warm boots, kept within reach rather than in a trunk or checked bag
- For drivers: a blanket, water, snacks, a phone charger and a small scraper in the car
- A full tank of gas before you leave, so a slow highway doesn't become a problem

### If it's mild and sunny

Pack a light layer anyway. Mornings and evenings in late November can cool off fast, and you'll want something for the walk from the car to the house.

## Flight checklist for Thanksgiving week

Thanksgiving is one of the busiest travel periods of the year, so even small weather delays can ripple. A few habits help.

- **Book earlier in the day when you can.** Morning flights have more room to recover if something slips. This is general travel practice, not a forecast claim.
- **Check your connecting city,** not just where you start and end.
- **Turn on airline app alerts** so you hear about changes before the gate does.
- **Keep essentials in your carry-on:** medications, chargers, a change of clothes and anything you'd hate to lose if a bag is delayed.
- **Know your rebooking options** before you're standing in a long line.

## Road-trip checklist

Driving gives you more control, but rain and snow both change the math.

- Check tire tread and pressure before you go.
- Make sure wipers and washer fluid are in good shape.
- Build in extra time. If the route normally takes four hours, plan for more if wet or wintry weather is in the forecast.
- Check the route on the day, using state transportation sites or the NWS for alerts.
- Be willing to leave earlier or later to dodge the worst of it. If conditions look rough, waiting can be the safer choice.

## Hosting: build a weather backup plan

If you're hosting, the outlook affects more than travel. Guests arrive wet or cold, parking gets messy, and any outdoor plan needs a Plan B.

- **Set up an entry zone.** A mat, a towel and a place for coats and boots keeps rain and slush from spreading through the house.
- **Have an indoor fallback** for anything you'd planned outside, like appetizers, a fire pit gathering or a football game in the yard.
- **If it's mild,** you may get an outdoor stretch. Keep it flexible with a few chairs and blankets, and don't make it the only plan.
- **Give guests a heads-up.** A quick message a day or two before saying "bring a rain jacket" or "roads may be slick" saves headaches.
- **Allow for late arrivals.** Weather delays are normal. Keep the timing of dinner loose enough that one late car doesn't derail it.

## Region matters more than the headline

The Almanac's outlook varies across the country, so a national summary like "rain, snow, sunshine and mild" really means different things in different places. I don't have the full regional breakdown from the report, so check the Almanac's own regional forecasts for your area rather than assuming the national mix applies to you.

Then compare it with your local NWS forecast as the holiday gets closer. When the two agree, you can plan with more confidence. When they don't, trust the NWS, which is based on current data.

## The one thing to do this week

Pick your travel day, write down a Plan B for it (an earlier departure, a different route, an indoor option for your hosting), and set a reminder to check your NWS forecast a week out. A plan you've already made is much easier to use when the weather shifts.
