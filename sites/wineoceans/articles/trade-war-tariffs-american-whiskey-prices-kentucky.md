Most people assume tariffs mean higher prices at the store. A new study on American whiskey turns that assumption on its head — and the twist depends almost entirely on your zip code.

Researchers found that when foreign governments slapped retaliatory tariffs on U.S. whiskey during the Trump-era trade wars, producers responded by cutting domestic prices across much of the country to make up for lost export revenue. If you were buying a bottle in, say, Ohio or Texas, you were likely paying less. But if you lived in Kentucky or Tennessee — the heartland of American whiskey production — prices actually went up.

By the end of this piece you'll understand exactly why that happened, what it reveals about how aged spirits work as an economic product, and what it means for your next purchase.

## The Basic Mechanic: Lost Exports, Cheaper Bottles at Home

Here's the core dynamic. When the EU, Canada, and other trading partners hit American whiskey with retaliatory tariffs — responses to U.S. steel and aluminum duties — overseas demand fell sharply. Whiskey that was destined for European bars and Japanese shelves suddenly had nowhere to go.

Producers couldn't just dial back production to match the new reality. Whiskey takes years to age. The barrels already sitting in rickhouses represent capital that's been locked up for four, eight, sometimes twelve or more years. You can't un-distill it, and you can't speed up the clock. The liquid exists, and it needs to be sold.

So distilleries did what any business does when supply outpaces demand: they competed harder on price in the markets they still controlled. That meant the American domestic market saw more competitive pricing, and in most of the country, bottles got cheaper.

## Why Kentucky and Tennessee Are the Exception

The regional split is the most revealing part of this story, and it comes down to local demand and brand loyalty.

Kentucky and Tennessee aren't just places where whiskey is made — they're places where people drink it with genuine regional pride. Consumers there are far more willing to pay a premium for home-state bottles. A bottle of bourbon from a distillery down the road carries local identity in a way it simply doesn't for a buyer in Seattle or Miami.

Producers recognized they had pricing power in their home markets that they didn't have elsewhere. While they were discounting to move product nationally and globally, they could simultaneously hold — or even raise — prices where loyal local demand insulated them from pressure. The study found that's exactly what happened: Kentucky and Tennessee whiskey drinkers paid more during the same period that prices fell in most other states.

This isn't cynical — it's rational market behavior. If your customers in Louisville will pay a higher price and your customers in Portland need a reason to choose your bottle over a competitor's, you price accordingly in both directions.

## The Aging Constraint Changes Everything

For wine drinkers, this pattern will feel familiar. Aged spirits and fine wine share a structural quirk that most consumer goods don't: supply can't respond quickly to price signals.

A winery or distillery deciding today to make more product won't see that product on shelves for years. This means that when demand shifts — because of a trade dispute, a recession, or a global pandemic — producers can't simply adjust output and wait for the market to rebalance. They're stuck with what they have. The only lever they can pull immediately is price.

The study highlights this explicitly: brands adjusted pricing faster than supply could respond. That gap between the short-term pricing flexibility and the long-term production constraint is where a lot of the interesting economics of aged beverages live.

For consumers, it also means that today's trade policy decisions have consequences that will play out across a decade of bottles, not just this quarter's shipment.

## What This Means for Buyers Right Now

If you're outside Kentucky and Tennessee, this research suggests the trade war created a real window of lower prices on American whiskey that may not last. Trade policy is still in flux, and if retaliatory tariffs ease, the pressure to discount domestically eases with them. The distilleries won't leave money on the table longer than they have to.

A few practical takeaways:

**Stock up on value now if prices are still soft in your market.** Whiskey doesn't spoil in an unopened bottle, and if domestic pricing was pushed down by export disruption, those conditions could reverse as trade relationships shift.

**Pay attention to regional pricing differences.** If you're visiting Kentucky or Tennessee, don't assume you're getting the best price by buying at the source. This research suggests you might actually pay more for local bottles than you would at a good retailer in another state.

**Look for distilleries that were export-heavy before the tariffs.** Those producers had the most to lose overseas and the most incentive to compete aggressively at home. Their bottles may represent better value per dollar during this period.

## The Bigger Picture for Trade Policy

Whiskey makes a surprisingly clean case study for how trade wars ripple through to everyday prices — not always in the direction you'd expect, and rarely evenly across a country as large and regionally varied as the United States.

The standard headline framing — tariffs equal higher prices — misses how producers actually respond when their export markets close. Sometimes the domestic consumer benefits, at least temporarily. Sometimes they don't, depending on exactly where they happen to live and how much local producers think they can charge them.

With trade policy still actively shifting, this study is a useful reminder that the effects land differently depending on your market position, your product's supply constraints, and how loyal your local customers are. The person buying bourbon in Nashville and the person buying the same brand in Chicago were living in meaningfully different price environments — and most of them had no idea.

If there's one thing to take from this: when trade disputes make the news, it's worth asking not just whether prices will go up or down, but *where* — because the answer is rarely the same for everyone.
