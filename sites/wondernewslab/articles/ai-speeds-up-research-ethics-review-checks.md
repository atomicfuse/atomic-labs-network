If you have ever waited weeks for a research project to clear ethics review, you know the delay is rarely about hard moral questions. Often it is paperwork: missing sections, inconsistent details, forms that don't match the standard they're meant to meet. A new study suggests AI could take much of that burden off people's desks, and it also shows where the line should stay firmly in human hands.

## What the Bristol study found

Per a report on Phys, researchers at the University of Bristol found that large language models can check research ethics applications against published standards in minutes. They compared three tools: Claude, ChatGPT-4o and Microsoft Copilot.

Claude outperformed the other two on accuracy, speed and error detection, according to the report. The study's core argument is that AI can streamline routine compliance checks, while human judgment remains essential for the ethical decisions themselves.

That is the angle worth keeping in mind: AI as an assistant, not a decision-maker.

## The best use for AI here is the boring part

The study points to a specific kind of task where AI earns its keep: structured, document-based checking. You have a written application on one side and a published standard on the other. The question is whether the first meets the second.

That work is repetitive, detail-heavy and easy to get wrong when you're tired. It is also not where the ethical weight sits. Spotting that a required element is absent is different from deciding whether a study's risks to participants are acceptable.

The idea, as the research is described, is that automating the first kind of work lets people spend their time on the second.

## Why the model you pick matters

It is tempting to treat AI chatbots as interchangeable. The Bristol comparison suggests otherwise. On the measures tested, accuracy, speed and error detection, Claude came out ahead of ChatGPT-4o and Copilot.

The practical lesson is not "use Claude." The summary available doesn't cover how the tests were run, so you can't assume the same ranking holds for every task or every new version. The lesson is to test a tool on your own kind of documents before relying on it. If you run an ethics office or any review process, a sensible approach looks like this:

1. Pick a set of past applications where you already know the correct outcome.
2. Run them through each tool against your published standard.
3. Compare how many errors each one catches and how many it misses.
4. Note how long each takes.

That mirrors the three things the study measured and gives you evidence for your own setting rather than someone else's.

## Where humans stay in charge

The research does not argue that AI should approve or reject studies. Ethics committees exist to weigh context, competing interests and the welfare of the people involved. Those are judgment calls.

A useful way to split the work:

- **AI handles:** checking applications against written standards, flagging errors, and doing it quickly.
- **People handle:** interpreting what the flags mean, weighing the ethics, and making the final decision.

Keeping that split clear also helps with trust. Institutions are under pressure to use AI responsibly without weakening oversight, and a tool that only pre-checks paperwork is far easier to explain and audit than one that issues verdicts.

## Why this could reduce delays

Research approvals are often slowed by administrative review. If a first-pass check that once took a person a long stretch can be done in minutes, applicants could fix mistakes before a committee ever sees them, and reviewers could spend their meetings on the substantive questions. That applies to academic and medical research alike, though the summary doesn't quantify how much time any institution would save, so treat the speed-up as promising rather than proven at scale.

## What to do with this

If you work anywhere near review or compliance, don't ask whether AI can make the decision. Ask which part of your process is a document checked against a standard, and pilot a tool there first, with a person still signing off.
