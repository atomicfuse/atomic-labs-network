BuzzFeed Shopping recently published a roundup of 24 products it says will make you realize life could have been easier all along. Its tagline singled out one find: a $15 puppy paw cleaner. The piece is a list of everyday problem-solvers, pitched as items that make routines easier, faster, or less frustrating.

We can only point to what BuzzFeed has shared publicly, and we won't pretend to know what's on the other 23 slots. What we can do is take the idea behind the list and make it useful: a way to tell which small, cheap gadgets are worth your money and which will end up in a drawer.

## Why These Roundups Work on Us

The appeal is easy to see. A low-priced item that promises to fix one irritating moment asks very little of you. There's no big lifestyle change, no learning curve, and no major spend. A paw cleaner, to use BuzzFeed's own example, targets one specific annoyance for about $15, per the roundup's description.

That's the pattern behind most "wish I'd known sooner" lists: a narrow problem, a narrow solution, a small price. It's also why they're easy to overbuy. Each item feels like a bargain, but the total can add up quickly, and a product only saves money if you use it.

## Step 1: Name the Annoyance Out Loud

Before you add anything to your cart, finish this sentence: "Every time I ___, I get annoyed because ___."

If you can fill it in with a specific, recurring moment, such as wiping muddy paws after every walk, you have a real problem. If you can only say "this looks neat," you have an impulse.

A useful check is frequency. Something that irritates you daily is worth solving. Something that bothers you twice a year probably isn't worth storage space.

## Step 2: Count How Often You'd Use It

Do a quick cost-per-use estimate. Divide the price by the number of times you realistically expect to use the item in a year.

Take a $15 item you'd use once a day. That's roughly four cents a use over a year, assuming it holds up. Use the same math on a $15 item you'd use five times, and it's three dollars a use. The first is a fix. The second is a novelty.

This is our own rule of thumb, not something from BuzzFeed, but it cuts through a lot of list-induced temptation.

## Step 3: Check What It Replaces

The best convenience products swap out something you already do badly. Ask what the gadget replaces:

- **A workaround you already use.** If you're improvising with a towel, a spare bowl, or a clumsy routine, a dedicated tool may genuinely improve things.
- **Nothing at all.** If the gadget adds a new step to your day, be skeptical. Extra steps rarely stick.
- **Something you already own.** If a drawer item does 80 percent of the job, skip the purchase.

## Step 4: Run a Two-Week Test

BuzzFeed's roundup is a recommendation list, and a list can't tell you how a product fits your home. The only real test is your own routine.

When something arrives, put it where you'll actually use it, not in a cabinet. Give it two weeks. Then ask:

1. Did I reach for it without thinking?
2. Did it save time or reduce a hassle, even slightly?
3. Would I be annoyed if it vanished tomorrow?

Three yeses mean keep it. Two noes mean return it while you still can. Check the return window before you buy, since that's what makes the test free.

## Step 5: Beware the Bundle Effect

Long lists make it feel normal to buy several items at once. Resist that. Buy one or two, test them, and add more only if they earn it. A single useful gadget beats a counter full of half-used ones.

This matters more as shopping content leans toward quick, low-cost "life hack" finds. They're fun to browse, and many may well be good. But a list is built to make you want everything on it. Your job is to want only what solves your problem.

## What a Good Pick Looks Like

Using BuzzFeed's featured example as a model, a strong candidate usually has these traits:

- It targets one clear, repeated annoyance, like dirty paws after a walk.
- It's inexpensive enough that a miss doesn't hurt.
- It fits into a routine you already have.
- You can describe in one sentence what it does.

If a product needs a paragraph of explanation, it's probably solving a problem you don't have.

## The Takeaway

Before your next "wish I'd known sooner" purchase, write down the annoyance it fixes and how many times a week you'll hit it. If you can't answer both in ten seconds, leave it in the cart and let it sit for a day.
