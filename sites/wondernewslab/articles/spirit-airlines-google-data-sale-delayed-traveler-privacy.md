Spirit Airlines' planned $10 million data sale to Google has been delayed after lawmakers questioned both companies about AI-related risks, per Travelpulse. By the end of this piece, you'll know why a deal like this draws political attention, what kinds of information airlines hold about you, and what you can do to keep tabs on your own data.

## What happened

According to Travelpulse, lawmakers sent a letter to the CEOs of both Spirit and Google outlining concerns related to artificial intelligence. The planned sale, valued at $10 million, has since been delayed.

The reporting centers on political scrutiny of how corporate data may end up in AI systems. The details of what exactly was to be sold, and what the lawmakers specifically asked, are not spelled out in the material we have, so we won't guess at them. What we can say is that the pause itself signals something: data deals with AI companies are now getting a second look before they close.

## Why airline data is sensitive

Not all data is equal. Airline data can involve travel behavior, identity signals, and consumer patterns, according to the reporting. Think about what that covers in everyday terms:

- Where you go and how often
- When you book, and how far ahead
- The details that tie a trip to a specific person

That's our plain-language reading of the categories, not a list of what Spirit planned to sell. But it shows why regulators pay attention. A pattern of trips can say a lot about someone's life, even when no single booking seems revealing.

## Why AI makes the question bigger

AI companies are hunting for more data to train and improve their models, and regulators are increasingly focused on privacy, consent, and corporate accountability. Those three words are the heart of the matter.

**Privacy:** What happens to personal details once they enter a model or a larger data set?

**Consent:** Did customers who booked a flight ever expect their information to feed an AI system?

**Accountability:** If something goes wrong, who answers for it: the company that collected the data or the company that used it?

The delay suggests lawmakers want answers to questions like these before the money changes hands. That's our analysis, not a stated position from the letter's authors.

## What the data might be worth

One of the angles around this story is what airline data could be worth to Google. The only figure we have is the $10 million price tag on this planned sale. Beyond that, we don't have details on how Google would use the data, so any claims about its value to the company would be speculation.

What the number does show is that data has become a line item. For a company, selling information it already holds can look like a straightforward revenue source. For the people the data describes, it can feel very different.

## The bigger tension: AI growth versus oversight

This story is a small window into a larger tug of war. AI developers want large amounts of data. Lawmakers want guardrails. Companies sitting on customer information are caught between the two, with a financial incentive on one side and public and political pressure on the other.

1. Whether the sale is revised, approved, or dropped after the delay
2. Whether the companies respond publicly to the lawmakers' letter

## What you can do about your own travel data

You don't need to wait for a ruling to take practical steps. These are general good habits, not advice drawn from the Spirit case:

### Read the privacy policy for the sections that matter

Skip the legal boilerplate and look for headings about sharing, third parties, and partners. Those sections describe who else may receive your information.

### Check your account settings

Many loyalty and booking accounts include marketing or data-sharing toggles. Spend five minutes turning off what you don't want shared.

### Share only what a booking requires

If an optional field asks for extra details, you can often leave it blank.

### Look for opt-out or deletion options

Some companies let you request that your data be deleted or not sold. Availability varies by company and location, so check what's offered in your case.

## The takeaway

A $10 million deal paused by a letter from lawmakers is a reminder that your travel history is an asset to someone. Before your next booking, open the account settings and privacy policy of the airline you use most, and decide for yourself what you're comfortable sharing.
