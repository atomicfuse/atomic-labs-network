A blog post from Yyhh, titled "AI is a tool and using it well takes skill," has been drawing attention on the Hacker News front page, with 287 points and 238 comments at the time of writing. Its core idea is useful whether you code, write, research, or study: the quality of what you get from a large language model (LLM) depends heavily on what you bring to it. By the end of this piece, you'll have a clear way to think about that and a few habits to put it into practice.

## The argument being pushed back on

Yyhh's post takes aim at Rich Hickey's claim that LLMs are automation rather than tools. Per the post, the counterpoint is that AI can be used skillfully when it is guided by human judgment, feedback, and oversight. In this framing, an LLM is an instrument that amplifies the user's habits, standards, and expertise instead of replacing them.

That distinction matters in practice. Automation is something you start and walk away from. A tool is something you steer, check, and correct. Which one you are doing changes how you should behave.

## Treat the output as a mirror of your judgment

If an LLM amplifies what you bring, then sloppy input and low standards get amplified too. So does careful thinking. This is our reading of the post's framing, but it suggests a simple test: when a result disappoints you, ask whether the instructions, the context, or your own bar for "good" was the weak link.

A few ways to act on that:

- **Write down what "good" looks like before you ask.** Even two sentences about the audience, constraints, and success criteria give you something to judge the answer against.
- **Give context you'd give a new colleague.** Background, goals, and what you've already tried.
- **Compare the answer to your own expectation.** If you can't tell whether it's right, that's a signal to learn more, not to accept it.

## Use AI for learning, critique, and iteration

The strongest uses, as the post's framing implies, are those where AI supports your thinking rather than standing in for it. Some concrete workflows:

### Ask it to critique your draft
Write the first version yourself, then ask for weaknesses, missing counterarguments, or unclear passages. You stay the author; the model plays editor.

### Ask it to explain, then test yourself
When learning a topic, have it explain a concept, then try restating it in your own words or solving a problem without help. If you can't, you haven't learned it yet.

### Iterate in small loops
Rather than requesting a finished product in one go, work in steps: outline, review, revise, review again. Each loop is a chance to apply your judgment, which is where the feedback and oversight come in.

### For programmers and researchers
Use it to explore options, spot edge cases, or summarize ideas, but read and verify the result yourself. You remain accountable for what you ship or cite.

## Keep accountability with you

The post is timely because the conversation is shifting. Per the summary of Yyhh's argument, the question is moving from "should we use it?" to "what counts as responsible use?" The piece pushes back on both AI hype and AI rejection by focusing on skill, agency, and accountability.

Responsible use, in practical terms, means you can answer yes to these:

1. Could I explain this result in my own words?
2. Did I check the parts that matter?
3. If it's wrong, am I willing to own that?

If the answer to any is no, you've probably drifted from using a tool toward handing off the work.

## Don't treat it like a person

One of the angles in the discussion around the post is the danger of anthropomorphizing AI. It's easy to read confidence in a fluent answer as understanding. Treating the model as an instrument helps you keep your skepticism: you wouldn't trust a calculator you never verified on a hard problem, and fluent text deserves the same scrutiny.

## Where the debate stays open

Yyhh's post is one side of an active argument, and the Hacker News thread, with hundreds of comments, shows people disagree. Whether LLMs are best described as tools or as automation may depend on how a given person uses them. That is our observation, not the post's claim. Someone who pastes in a request and accepts whatever returns is closer to automation. Someone who sets standards, checks results, and revises is closer to using a tool.

## The takeaway

Before your next AI session, write down what a good result looks like, then judge the output against it. Skill with these systems is mostly the habit of staying in charge of the work.
