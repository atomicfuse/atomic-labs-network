Bat Boy is getting a day job in television. Per Vulture, Robert Ben Garant is developing an improvised comedy series based on Weekly World News, the tabloid famous for Bat Boy and other stories that sit somewhere between "fantastical" and "absolutely not." Garant and Cathy Shim are showrunning, according to Vulture's report.

The concept, as described, is a mockumentary in a Reno 911!-style format. The twist is that the show treats the paper's bizarre stories as real. The series follows reporters who want to be taken seriously while covering absurd "true" stories.

That's the news. Now for why it might actually work.

## The Premise Does the Heavy Lifting

Plenty of mockumentaries start with a quirky workplace and then hunt for the funny. This one begins with a workplace whose product is already a punchline. Weekly World News, home of Bat Boy, is a ready-made comic engine, and the show's central joke (the staff is deadly serious) is a stable one.

It's hard not to see the appeal of the reporters' predicament. A journalist chasing respect while assigned to cover Bat Boy is a sitcom setup that practically writes itself. The comedy lives in the gap between professional ambition and the material on the desk.

## Why Improv Is the Right Tool

Vulture notes the show is improvised, and that detail matters. Deadpan absurdity is a hard thing to script and a natural thing to improvise. When performers commit completely to a ridiculous premise and react to each other in real time, the straight-faced energy tends to feel lived-in rather than written.

The Reno 911! comparison is the show's own calling card, so it's fair to read it as a promise: a cast treating nonsense as routine, with the camera along for the ride. Garant is the name attached, and the format points to a style fans of that kind of comedy will recognize immediately.

## Fake News Then, Conspiracy Culture Now

The timing is interesting. The project arrives amid renewed interest in media satire, conspiracy culture and nostalgia for print-era weirdness, along with a broader conversation about journalism in crisis. A show about a tabloid whose stories are openly bonkers lands differently when the information landscape feels anything but playful.

This is my take, not anything the creators have said: there's a built-in contrast waiting here. Weekly World News's brand of fake news was winking, a grocery-checkout-aisle joke the audience was in on. Today's misinformation often isn't winking at all. A series that plays the absurdity completely straight while asking its characters to be taken seriously could tap into that tension without needing to lecture anyone. The laughs can come first, and any commentary can ride along quietly.

## The Nostalgia Economy Gets Its Bat Boy

There's also an undeniable nostalgia hook. Print tabloid weirdness is the kind of pop-culture artifact people remember fondly, and Bat Boy is its most recognizable mascot. For heavy streamers who love a recognizable brand with a fresh comedic spin, this is the sort of title that gets a click on name alone.

What we don't know yet is plenty. Based on what Vulture reported, there's no word here on a network or streamer, a cast, a premiere date, or how many episodes are planned. Treat anything beyond the concept, the format and the two showrunners as unconfirmed until more details surface.

## What to Watch For

The risk with any mockumentary about a weird institution is repetition: once the audience gets the joke, the show has to find new ones. The upside here is a deep well of material, since the paper's whole identity is escalating strangeness. If the writers and cast lean into the reporters' desperate desire for credibility, there's an ongoing character engine beyond the monster-of-the-week gags.

For now, the pitch is simple and strong: serious people, unserious stories, cameras rolling. Bat Boy has survived on newsstands, and a TV newsroom that refuses to blink might be the most fitting home he's had yet.
