You have three nights in a new city and a phone full of conflicting recommendations. Every meal feels like it counts. This guide gives you a simple way to choose: decide what the meal is for, check a few signals, and book or walk in without second-guessing.

The idea comes from a recent New York Times piece on choosing a restaurant while traveling. I'm working from its overview rather than the full text, so what follows is my own practical framework on the same theme, not a summary of the article's specific advice.

## Start with what the meal is for

Before you open a map, name the job of the meal. Most dinners on a trip fall into one of three categories:

- **The anchor meal.** One planned, possibly pricey dinner that reflects the place you're visiting. Book it early.
- **The casual gem.** A relaxed, affordable spot where you eat what locals eat. Walk-ins are often fine.
- **The fuel stop.** Something quick and decent so you can get back to your day.

Most trips need only one anchor meal for every two or three of the others. Deciding this first stops you from treating every lunch like a once-in-a-lifetime event, which is how people end up exhausted, overspent, and disappointed.

## Use reviews as a filter, not a verdict

Reviews are most useful for ruling places out, not for crowning a winner. Here's a low-stress way to use them:

1. **Read the middle.** Skim a handful of three- and four-star reviews. They tend to describe what the place is actually like, without the rapture or the rant.
2. **Look for specifics.** Comments that name dishes, noise level, or wait times are more useful than "amazing!"
3. **Check who is writing.** If every review is in one language and mentions the view, you may be looking at a place built for visitors. That isn't automatically bad, but know what you're getting.
4. **Stop at ten minutes.** If you can't decide after skimming a few options, pick the one that matches your meal's job and move on.

Social media hype deserves the same caution. A place that looks incredible in a video may also have a two-hour line. Ask whether the payoff matches the time it will cost you.

## Read the local cues

Once you're on the street, your eyes are a good research tool. These are signals I'd look for, not guarantees:

- **A short, focused menu.** A kitchen that does a few things tends to do them with care. A laminated menu with forty items across five cuisines is a flag.
- **Hawkers out front.** If someone is working hard to pull you in, the food probably isn't doing that job on its own.
- **Who's eating.** A room with a mix of ages, groups of coworkers, and people who look like regulars is a good sign.
- **Timing.** Look at when people are eating. If locals are filling tables at an hour that suits their habits, not yours, follow their lead.
- **Location.** Restaurants directly beside the biggest landmarks often depend on foot traffic. Walking two or three blocks away frequently changes the picture.

No single cue settles it. Two or three pointing the same way is enough.

## Splurge or casual gem?

Here's a quick way to choose.

### Splurge when

- The cuisine or technique is a signature of the place you're visiting.
- You're celebrating something and the occasion matters as much as the food.
- Reservations are scarce. Book as soon as your dates are firm, since the best tables can disappear well ahead.

### Go casual when

- You're tired, jet-lagged, or have a packed day.
- The local specialty is a street food, a market stall, or a neighborhood counter, not a tasting menu.
- You'd rather save your budget for the one anchor meal.

A good rhythm is to plan the anchor dinner first, then leave the rest loose. Having one confirmed reservation takes pressure off everything else.

## Balance famous spots with spontaneous finds

Famous restaurants are famous for a reason, and skipping them out of principle is its own kind of snobbery. If one is on your list, go with realistic expectations: book ahead, go at an off-peak time if you can, and order the thing it's known for.

For the rest, leave room to wander. Keep a short list of two or three backup options near wherever you'll be at mealtime, saved on your map. If a spontaneous find looks promising, you can try it. If it doesn't, you have a fallback and no scramble.

## A checklist for picking dinner fast

When you're hungry and deciding, run through this in under five minutes:

1. What is tonight's meal for: anchor, casual gem, or fuel?
2. Does it fit the budget I set for this meal?
3. Does a quick skim of reviews show specific, recent praise for dishes?
4. Is the menu focused, and are there people eating who seem local?
5. Is it a short walk away, with a realistic wait?
6. Do I have a backup within a few blocks if the answer is no?

If you get four or more yeses, sit down and order. The best meal on a trip is rarely the most researched one. It's the one you were relaxed enough to enjoy.

**Before you leave home, book one anchor dinner and save two backup spots near each neighborhood you'll be exploring. Then let the rest of the trip surprise you.**
