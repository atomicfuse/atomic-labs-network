Travelling for food is having a moment. The Business Times reports that culinary tourism is on the rise, with travellers choosing destinations based on food experiences, local cuisine, restaurants, markets, and dining culture. Per the report, people are looking for more immersive, authentic experiences beyond sightseeing.

If you want to join them, the good news is that planning a food-first trip is a skill you can build. Below is a simple way to do it, in the order you would actually do it. Think of it as a recipe for an itinerary: start with the main ingredient, then build around it.

## Start With the Cuisine, Not the Landmark

Most trips begin with a place and add food later. Flip that. Ask yourself what you most want to eat, then look for where that food is at its best.

Try this 10-minute exercise:

1. Write down three dishes or food styles you keep craving or feel curious about.
2. Note what they have in common: spicy, fresh, smoky, comforting, seafood-heavy.
3. Look for regions known for that profile.

This gives you a destination shortlist that matches your taste rather than a generic top-ten list. It also makes every day of the trip feel purposeful, because you know what you are hunting for.

## Learn the Local Food Identity Before You Go

Every place has a culinary identity: a handful of staple ingredients, signature dishes, and habits around when and how people eat. Spend an hour or two researching it before booking anything.

What to look up:

- **Signature dishes:** The two or three things locals would say you cannot skip.
- **Staple ingredients:** Rice, noodles, bread, seafood, spices, whatever anchors the cooking.
- **Meal rhythm:** When people eat lunch, whether dinner is early or late, and when markets are busiest.

That last point saves real frustration. Arriving at a famous market after it has wound down is a common way to miss the best of it.

## Build the Itinerary Around Meals

Here is the shift that makes a trip food-first: treat meals as fixed points and everything else as filler between them.

### Anchor one big meal per day

Pick one meal each day that is the main event. It might be a long lunch at a well-regarded restaurant or a dinner you booked ahead. Plan your sightseeing near it so you are not crossing a city hungry.

### Leave the rest flexible

Leave the other meals open for snacks, market wandering, and following your nose. Over-scheduling every bite is the quickest way to turn a pleasure into a chore. A good rule of thumb is one planned meal, one half-planned meal, and one wildcard per day.

### Reserve early for the meals that matter

If a restaurant is a must, book it as soon as your dates are set. Leave your other days loose.

## Mix Street Food and Sit-Down Dining

Street food and fine dining are different experiences, and a strong food trip usually includes both.

- **Street food and markets** show you everyday eating: what locals grab, how ingredients are used, and how a dish tastes at its most casual. They are quick and easy to fit between other plans.
- **Restaurants and tasting-style dining** show you how a cuisine is interpreted by chefs and presented as a full experience. They take more time and planning.

You do not have to choose. A simple pattern is street food for breakfast and lunch, with a sit-down dinner you planned in advance. Pace yourself: sharing dishes and ordering small portions lets you taste more without hitting a wall by mid-afternoon.

## Visit a Market Early in the Trip

Markets are the fastest way to understand a place through its ingredients. Go early in your stay, ideally on day one or two, and use it as a map for the rest of the trip.

While you are there:

- Notice which produce, seafood, or spices dominate the stalls.
- Ask vendors what is in season or what they would eat themselves. Most are happy to point you toward something good.
- Taste before you buy when sampling is offered.

What you see in the market will start showing up on menus, and you will recognize it.

## Add One Hands-On Experience

Since travellers are chasing immersion, add one activity where you do more than eat. A cooking class, a guided food walk, or a market tour with a local cook all work. Even if you never recreate the dish exactly, you will come home understanding the technique and the ingredients behind it.

Bring that home practically: buy one small, packable ingredient that defines the cuisine, and cook with it within a week of returning. It keeps the trip alive in your kitchen.

## Keep a Simple Food Log

You will forget more than you think. Each evening, spend five minutes jotting down:

- What you ate and where
- What you would order again
- One flavor or technique you want to try cooking at home

This becomes your own personal guide for the next trip, and a shopping list for your own kitchen.

## A Sample Food-First Day

Here is how the pieces fit together:

- **Morning:** Market visit and a street breakfast.
- **Midday:** Light snacks while you explore, nothing planned.
- **Afternoon:** A sight or neighborhood walk near tonight's restaurant.
- **Evening:** The reserved dinner, your anchor meal.

It is relaxed, it leaves room for surprises, and it guarantees at least one great meal.

## The Takeaway

Before you book anything for your next trip, write down the three dishes you most want to eat. Let them choose the destination, and build the rest of the itinerary around them.
