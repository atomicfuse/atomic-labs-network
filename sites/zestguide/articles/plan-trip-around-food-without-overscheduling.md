A food-first trip has a reputation for being exhausting: spreadsheets of reservations, timed tastings, and a stomach that gives out by day two. It doesn't have to work that way. Real Simple's piece on planning vacations around food offers nine tricks for eating better while traveling, and the useful thread running through them is this: anchor the trip with a few great meals, then leave the rest loose.

Here's how to do that, in the order you'll actually use the ideas.

## Before You Go: Do the Light Homework

### Research local specialties

Start by learning what the place is known for. A short list of dishes, regional ingredients, and baked goods gives you something to look for, and it makes menus far less intimidating when you sit down. Keep it to a handful of items. You're building a shortlist, not a syllabus.

### Build the itinerary around standout meals

Pick the meals you'd be sad to miss and plan the days around them, rather than squeezing food in around sightseeing. One anchor meal per day is a sensible starting point (that number is my suggestion, not the article's). It gives each day a shape without filling every hour.

### Book key restaurants in advance

If a particular restaurant is the reason you're going somewhere, reserve it ahead of time. Reserve only those. Booking every meal is how a food trip turns into a schedule you resent.

## On the Ground: Eat Where the Place Actually Eats

### Ask locals where they actually eat

The phrasing matters. "Where should I eat?" tends to get you the famous place. "Where do you go on a regular weeknight?" gets you closer to the real answer. Hotel staff, shop owners, and the person next to you at the bakery counter are all fair game.

### Visit markets, bakeries, and food halls

These are the easiest way to taste a lot of local food without committing to a full sit-down meal. You can graze, compare, and point at what looks good. They also work well when your plans fall apart or you're short on time.

### Leave room for spontaneous finds

This is the antidote to overplanning. Keep at least one meal a day, or one open afternoon, unscheduled. The line out the door at a place you stumbled across is often better than anything you researched.

## Go Deeper: Learn Something You Can Take Home

### Take a food tour or cooking class

A food tour gives you context and a curated route in an unfamiliar neighborhood. A cooking class gives you something more lasting: a technique or dish you can repeat in your own kitchen. If you only do one, pick the class if you like to cook, and the tour if you want to get your bearings quickly.

### Shop for edible souvenirs

Food is a souvenir you'll actually use. Look for shelf-stable local products from markets and specialty shops, then check your airline and destination rules on bringing food home before you buy. A jar of something regional on your pantry shelf brings the trip back every time you open it.

### Document meals as part of the trip memory

Snap the dish, the market stall, the menu. A quick note about what you ate and where makes a trip easier to recall (and easier to recommend) later. It's also a handy cheat sheet for recreating a favorite at home.

## A Simple Way to Put It Together

Here's one way to combine all nine without overbooking, as a rough template rather than a rule:

- **Before the trip:** write your short list of local dishes and book the one or two restaurants you can't miss.
- **Each day:** one planned meal, one market or bakery stop, and one open slot.
- **Once during the trip:** a food tour or cooking class.
- **Last day:** buy edible souvenirs, then review your photos and notes.

## The One Habit to Keep

Plan the meals that matter, then protect the gaps between them. Reserve the table you'd cross an ocean for, and leave the rest open for whatever smells good on the way there.
