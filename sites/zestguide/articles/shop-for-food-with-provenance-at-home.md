Forbes recently looked at how travelers are chasing food and drink tied to a specific place, from Oktoberfest in Munich to whisky tourism in Scotland. The core idea, per Forbes, is that provenance (where something is made, who makes it, and the culture around it) adds value, authenticity, and emotional pull.

You don't need a plane ticket to use that idea. The same questions that make a trip to Munich or Scotland feel meaningful can make your grocery shopping smarter and your dinners more interesting. Here's how to put provenance to work in your own kitchen.

## Start With Three Questions

Provenance, as Forbes frames it, comes down to place, people, and culture. Translate those into questions you can ask at the store or market:

- **Where was this made or grown?** Look for a region or town on the label, not just a country.
- **Who made it?** A named farm, producer, or maker is more useful than a generic brand line.
- **What's the tradition around it?** Knowing how people in that place actually use an ingredient tells you how to cook with it.

You won't get all three answers every time. Even one changes how you pick and how you cook.

## Pick One Pantry Staple to Upgrade

Don't overhaul your whole pantry. Choose one item you use weekly and trade up to a version with a clear origin story. Good candidates:

- Olive oil
- Salt
- Honey
- Canned tomatoes
- Mustard
- Vinegar

Taste the new one next to your usual version. Pour a teaspoon of each oil into small bowls, or dip a piece of bread in each. This takes about two minutes and tells you whether the upgrade is worth the price for you.

That comparison is the point. Provenance should earn its place on flavor, not just on a label.

## Read Labels Like a Traveler

A few habits make labels more useful:

1. **Look for specifics.** A named region or producer beats vague phrasing.
2. **Check for dates.** Harvest or production dates, where listed, help you judge freshness.
3. **Notice what's missing.** If a product leans hard on heritage imagery but tells you nothing concrete, treat that as marketing until proven otherwise.

Forbes notes that brands, regions, and creators are leaning into origin stories to stand out in crowded markets. That's a reminder to verify. A good story is a reason to try something, not proof it's better.

## Shop Where You Can Ask Questions

The best version of the travel experience is talking to the person who makes the thing. You can recreate that locally:

- **Farmers markets:** Ask what's in season this week and how the grower cooks it.
- **Cheese counters and butchers:** Ask where the product came from and what they'd serve it with.
- **Specialty shops:** Staff often know the story behind a bottle, tin, or jar.

A two-minute conversation often yields a better dinner idea than a recipe search.

## Cook the Ingredient the Way Its Home Does

Once you have an ingredient with a clear origin, let that place guide the dish. The simpler the preparation, the more the ingredient shows.

### A 15-minute example: pasta with good canned tomatoes

This works with any quality canned tomato you've chosen for its origin.

**Ingredients (serves 2 to 3):**
- 8 ounces dried pasta
- 3 tablespoons olive oil
- 3 cloves garlic, thinly sliced
- 1 can (14 to 15 ounces) whole peeled tomatoes
- Salt to taste
- Grated hard cheese, for serving

**Steps:**
1. Boil salted water and cook the pasta according to the package, usually 9 to 11 minutes.
2. While it cooks, warm the oil and garlic in a wide pan over medium-low heat for 1 to 2 minutes, until fragrant but not browned.
3. Add the tomatoes and crush them with a spoon. Simmer 10 minutes, until slightly thickened.
4. Drain the pasta, reserving a half cup of cooking water. Toss the pasta in the sauce, adding splashes of water as needed.
5. Season with salt and finish with cheese.

With only a handful of ingredients, there's nowhere for a mediocre tomato to hide, which makes it a good test for any upgrade.

## Make a Small Ritual Around It

Oktoberfest works as a model because it's more than a drink. Per Forbes, the culture around a product is part of its pull. You can borrow that at home on a small scale:

- Serve a regional ingredient with the foods people traditionally pair it with.
- Set the table for it, even on a Tuesday.
- Tell your guests, or your family, where it came from.

This costs nothing and tends to make people slow down and pay attention to what they're eating.

## Know When Not to Pay Extra

Provenance is worth paying for when it changes taste, texture, or how you feel about the meal. It isn't worth it for ingredients that disappear into a dish, like a tomato in a long-simmered, heavily spiced stew. Spend where you'll taste it: finishing oils, simple sauces, fresh produce, cheese, bread, and anything eaten more or less on its own.

## Your Next Step

This week, pick one staple, find a version with a named place and maker, and taste it side by side with what you usually buy. If you can tell the difference, you've found where provenance belongs in your cart.
